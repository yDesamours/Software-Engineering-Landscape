# Priority, Fairness, and Isolation

_Module 10.2b — Background Jobs & Task Queues_

## The Problem

A customer-feedback platform. Tenants build surveys, collect responses, and pull reports. The background fleet is twenty workers against one queue, and it handles a healthy mix of work:

```
email.send        invitations, password resets      ~200ms    latency matters to a human
response.ingest   scoring and webhook fan-out       ~800ms    matters within a minute
report.pdf        one report                        ~12s      someone clicked a button
export.build      full CSV of 400k responses        ~35min    someone will check later
```

Tuesday, 09:12. One enterprise tenant kicks off twelve exports before a board meeting.

FIFO does exactly what FIFO does. Twelve workers pick up exports. The remaining eight pick up the next eight exports as they're enqueued, or finish quickly and take more. By 09:14 the entire fleet is inside `export.build`, and it stays there for thirty-five minutes.

```
worker  1 ████████████████████████████████████  export  (35 min)
worker  2 ████████████████████████████████████  export
   …
worker 20 ████████████████████████████████████  export

queue     [reset][invite][ingest][reset][ingest][reset] … 900 jobs
                                                  oldest: 34m 12s
```

The tickets that arrive don't mention exports. They say people can't log in, because a password-reset email that takes 200 milliseconds to send has been waiting half an hour for a worker. A job's latency turns out to be a property of the *other* jobs on the fleet.

This is **head-of-line blocking**, and it generalizes: the responsiveness of a queue is bounded below by the duration of the longest job running on it.

**So you add a priority column.** `ORDER BY priority, run_at`. Emails get 0, exports get 9. It works beautifully for about a week, and then three things happen.

The first is that it doesn't fix Tuesday. At 09:12 those twenty workers are *already inside* a 35-minute export. A priority-0 email jumping to the head of the queue has jumped ahead of nothing that matters, because there is no worker to give it to. **Priority reorders the queue; it does not free a worker.** Ordering solves contention for the *next* dispatch, and Tuesday's problem was contention for capacity already committed.

The second is starvation. Once emails and ingests flow continuously during business hours, strict priority means exports run only in the gaps — so the board-meeting export requested at 09:12 completes at 01:40 the following morning.

The third is social. An engineer whose feature felt important sets its jobs to priority 0. Then another does. By the next quarter, 80% of enqueued jobs are priority 0, and you have a FIFO queue with an extra column.

**And then there's the axis priority can't express at all.** A single tenant bulk-imports a contact list and enqueues 40,000 `email.send` jobs. Every one is genuinely priority 0 — they're invitations, the same job type as everyone else's. Tenant B enqueues three. Tenant B's three sit behind forty thousand.

```
FIFO across tenants                    what you wanted
  A A A A A A A A A A A A … B            A B A C A A B A A A
  ↑ B waits behind 40,000                ↑ B waits behind ~2
```

Nothing here is a priority problem. Tenant A's jobs are not less important. There are simply too many of them at once.

## What It Is

Three different problems wearing one word, and the first step is refusing to conflate them:

- **Isolation** — guaranteeing that one class of work cannot consume the capacity another class needs. Answered by *partitioning capacity*.
- **Priority** — deciding, when capacity is contended, which waiting job goes next. Answered by *ordering*.
- **Fairness** — preventing one tenant, user, or key from consuming a shared queue. Answered by *dispatch policy across sources*.

Collectively this is the queue's **scheduling policy**, and the terms you'll meet are head-of-line blocking, starvation, the **noisy neighbour** problem, and **fair queueing**.

Worth separating from a neighbour it resembles: this is not backpressure (9.1b). Backpressure is what you do when work arrives faster than the system can ever process it — the total is too much. Scheduling is how you distribute a capacity that is adequate in aggregate but badly allocated in the moment. A fleet that is permanently behind has a capacity problem, and no scheduling policy will fix it; it will only change who suffers.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A job type whose p50 is 200ms and whose p99 is twenty minutes.
- Queue depth looks fine, but the oldest ready job is half an hour old.
- Support tickets about missing emails that cluster around a known batch window.
- Someone asks for "priorities," and when you ask what they need, they describe not waiting behind someone else's work.
- The share of jobs enqueued at the highest priority rises every quarter.
- One `tenant_id` accounts for most of the ready queue.
- A runbook line that says not to run exports during business hours.
- Scaling the fleet fixes latency, and the fix lasts exactly until the next batch.
- A queue exists that no worker is configured to consume, and nobody noticed for a week.

## How It Works

**Isolation: partition the capacity.** Separate queues with *dedicated* workers — `critical` gets six, `default` ten, `bulk` four. The bulk pool cannot borrow from critical, so critical's worst-case wait is now a function of critical work only. This is the only one of the three mechanisms that actually bounds a fast job's latency, because it is the only one that guarantees a free worker.

Note the trap here, because most queue libraries offer the weaker thing and call it the same name. Configuring one worker to poll `critical, default, bulk` *in order* gives you ordering, not isolation: the moment that worker picks up a bulk job, it is gone for thirty-five minutes regardless of what arrives next. Polling order is not preemption. Jobs, unlike threads, are not interrupted mid-flight — so capacity has to be reserved in advance or it isn't reserved at all.

**Priority: order, with something to prevent starvation.** Strict ordering starves the tail whenever the head is continuously fed. Two standard fixes:

```
strict priority      critical → default → bulk
                     bulk runs only when the others are empty  →  starves

weighted dispatch    each poll picks a queue at random:  7 : 2 : 1
                     bulk gets 10% of dispatches no matter what  →  cannot starve

aging                effective_priority = priority − (wait_minutes / 5)
                     everything eventually reaches the front
```

Weighted dispatch is usually the better mechanism, because it's starvation-free by construction rather than by tuning, and it degrades sensibly: when a chosen queue is empty, the poll falls through to another, so no capacity is wasted on an idle class.

**Fairness: dispatch across sources, not just across jobs.** Two mechanisms, and they compose:

*Per-tenant concurrency caps.* No tenant may hold more than N of the M leases at once. Tenant A's 40,000 invitations occupy at most four workers; the other sixteen stay available. Simple, cheap, and it directly encodes "no single customer can take the fleet."

*Round-robin across per-tenant sub-queues.* The dispatcher takes one job from each active tenant in turn. With 40,000 jobs from A and 3 from B, B's wait drops from forty thousand jobs to about two. Where job sizes vary a lot, deficit round robin weights the rotation by work done rather than jobs taken, so a tenant submitting 35-minute jobs doesn't get the same share as one submitting 200ms jobs.

Either way, you must choose the **unit of fairness** explicitly — tenant, user, API key, or source system. Fair queueing is only fair with respect to the thing you named, and picking the wrong unit produces a system that is scrupulously fair along an axis nobody cares about.

**The mechanism people skip: make the long job shorter.** Split the 35-minute export into 400 chunk jobs of five seconds each. Nothing else changes, and the worst-case head-of-line delay drops from 35 minutes to 5 seconds — a 400× improvement with no scheduling machinery at all. Chunking also makes progress observable, retries cheap, and deploys safe, which is why 10.4 is mostly about it.

**Job duration is a scheduling parameter.** It's the one that never appears in a queue configuration file, and it dominates all three of the mechanisms above. Before building weighted fair queueing, check whether the fleet simply has a job on it that is too long to schedule around.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Mixed workloads with an order-of-magnitude spread**, in either duration or latency requirement. 200ms emails and 35-minute exports on one fleet is the canonical case; the spread is what makes FIFO indefensible.
- **A latency objective someone will hold you to.** "Password resets within 60 seconds" cannot be met on a shared FIFO queue at any fleet size, because the bound depends on the longest job, not the average one.
- **Multi-tenant systems where a single user action can enqueue unbounded work** — bulk import, re-index, "export everything." If one click can create 40,000 jobs, you need a fairness answer before someone clicks it.
- **Tiered products.** If you sell a faster tier, priority becomes a product feature rather than an engineering preference, which also makes it defensible against inflation.
- **Genuinely constrained capacity.** If the fleet is small because each worker holds an expensive licence, a GPU, or a rate-limited third-party connection, you can't scale your way out and scheduling is the only lever.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Homogeneous jobs.** If everything takes roughly the same time and matters roughly the same amount, FIFO is not just adequate, it's correct — and it's the only policy with no configuration to get wrong.
- **The queue drains to empty.** If the oldest ready job is never more than a few seconds old, scheduling policy never binds. Build it when the waiting is real, not when the diagram is appealing.
- **Two priority levels where one is always empty.** A priority column that only ever holds one distinct value is a column.
- **When more workers is cheaper than more policy.** For I/O-bound work on a small fleet, four more workers may cost less per month than the ongoing complexity of weighted dispatch, and they take an afternoon.
- **When the real answer is "stop enqueuing that."** Twelve simultaneous exports from one tenant is a product decision made by omission. A cap at the API — two concurrent exports per tenant — prevents the problem instead of scheduling around it.

## Trade-offs

- **Partitioned capacity is idle capacity.** Three pools of ten are strictly less efficient than one pool of thirty; you're buying predictability with utilization, and the bulk pool will sit idle while critical is saturated. That waste is the product, not a defect in it.
- **Every queue is a thing to monitor, scale, and remember.** Each needs its own depth and oldest-age alert. The recurring failure is a queue that ends up with no workers assigned after a config change, into which jobs disappear silently — which is worse than the head-of-line blocking you started with.
- **Priority is a social system.** Without an owner and a written meaning per level, it inflates until it carries no information. Tie levels to something external — a customer-facing SLO, a tier — so the definition isn't a matter of opinion.
- **Anti-starvation contradicts priority on purpose.** Aging means a high-priority job will sometimes wait behind an aged low-priority one. That's the design working, and whoever asked for priority needs to hear it said out loud.
- **Fair dispatch costs something at dispatch time.** Per-tenant counters must be atomic, the claim query gets more expensive, and at high dispatch rates the scheduler itself becomes the bottleneck. A fair queue that dispatches 200 jobs/sec is worse than a FIFO queue that dispatches 5,000.
- **Fairness breaks per-tenant ordering.** Round-robin dispatch interleaves, so a tenant's own jobs can complete out of sequence. If sequence matters, that constraint belongs in the job design, not the scheduler.
- **Concurrency caps penalize your largest customer**, who is often your largest customer. "Max four concurrent exports per tenant" is a commercial decision wearing a config value's clothes, and it should be made by someone who knows that.

## Alternatives

- **Add workers.** Unfashionable, frequently correct, and instantly reversible. It does not fix head-of-line blocking in the limit — twelve exports on forty workers still blocks twelve of them — but it moves the threshold.
- **Admission control at enqueue.** Refuse or defer the thirteenth concurrent export instead of scheduling around it. The queue never receives the 40,000 jobs, no dispatch policy is needed, and the user gets an immediate, honest answer. This is very often the better design.
- **Scheduled windows.** Run exports at 02:00. The runbook version of isolation: zero infrastructure, and it fails the moment you have customers in another timezone or a customer who wants their report now.
- **Chunking** (10.4) — reduce the maximum job duration until scheduling stops mattering.
- **Separate deployments, not just separate queues.** Past a certain scale the bulk pipeline gets its own service, its own database replicas, and its own scaling policy. Isolation taken to its structural conclusion.
- **Shuffle sharding.** Rather than one pool per tenant, assign each tenant a random *subset* of workers. A noisy tenant degrades only the few tenants sharing its subset, and with modest numbers the probability of full overlap becomes tiny — tenant-level isolation at a fraction of the capacity cost.
- **Dedicated infrastructure for the largest tenants** (Module 5). A commercial answer to a scheduling problem, and the honest one for a customer whose workload genuinely is different in kind.
- **Per-job elastic execution** (Module 41). If every job gets its own execution context, head-of-line blocking mostly disappears, and you pay for it in money and cold starts.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — measure wait, not depth.** Per job type and per tenant: p50 and p99 of the gap between enqueue and start. Most queue dashboards show depth, which is the wrong number — depth of 40,000 draining in a minute is fine, and depth of 12 with a 30-minute oldest is an incident.
_Signal you're here:_ a discussion about priorities in which nobody can say how long anything currently waits.

**Level 1 — split critical from bulk, with dedicated workers.** Two queues, two pools. This is 80% of the benefit in an afternoon.
_Signal you need it:_ a user-facing job waiting behind a batch job.

**Level 2 — cap the offender at enqueue.** N concurrent exports per tenant, enforced at the API with a clear response. Cheaper and more predictable than any downstream policy.
_Signal you need it:_ one user action can enqueue unbounded work.

**Level 3 — weighted dispatch across queues, replacing strict order.** Guarantees the bulk queue a share of capacity during business hours.
_Signal you need it:_ a queue that only drains overnight.

**Level 4 — per-tenant fairness inside a queue.** Concurrency caps in the claim, or round-robin across per-tenant sub-queues.
_Signal you need it:_ within one queue and one priority, a single tenant dominates the ready set.

**Level 5 — isolation per tenant or tier, and autoscaling per queue on oldest-job age.** Shuffle sharding, or dedicated pools for enterprise accounts.
_Signal you need it:_ a contractual latency commitment for a specific customer.

## Implementation

**Java.** Chosen because the isolation mechanism is structural here — separate `ExecutorService`s make partitioned capacity a thing you can see — and because the JDK ships a tempting wrong answer that's worth naming.

Capacity is partitioned before any job is dispatched:

```java
Map<String, ExecutorService> pools = Map.of(
    "critical", Executors.newFixedThreadPool(6),
    "default",  Executors.newFixedThreadPool(10),
    "bulk",     Executors.newFixedThreadPool(4)
);
```

> **Aside 1.** The tempting wrong answer is `new ThreadPoolExecutor(20, 20, …, new PriorityBlockingQueue<>())`, which looks like it solves all of this in one line. It solves none of it. The queue is in-memory, so every job in it is lost on restart (10.1a); it's unbounded, so a burst becomes an `OutOfMemoryError`; it's strict priority, so the low end starves; and it orders only jobs you have *already claimed* from the durable queue, which is the wrong place to make the decision. Priority belongs in the claim.

Queue selection is weighted rather than strictly ordered:

```java
// 7 : 2 : 1 — applied to every poll, not to every job
private String pickQueue() {
    int r = ThreadLocalRandom.current().nextInt(10);
    return r < 7 ? "critical" : r < 9 ? "default" : "bulk";
}
```

> **Aside 2.** Most libraries offer the strict version — poll `critical`, then `default`, then `bulk` — and it is the direct cause of "bulk only runs at night." Weighted selection gives the tail a floor it cannot be pushed below. When the chosen queue is empty the poll falls through to the next, so idle classes cost nothing.

Fairness goes into the claim itself, extending 10.1a's statement:

```sql
WITH busy AS (
    SELECT tenant_id
      FROM jobs
     WHERE leased_until > now()
     GROUP BY tenant_id
    HAVING count(*) >= 4          -- no tenant holds more than 4 of the 20 leases
)
UPDATE jobs
   SET leased_until = now() + interval '60 seconds',
       attempts     = attempts + 1
 WHERE id = (
     SELECT id FROM jobs
      WHERE queue = ?
        AND run_at <= now()
        AND (leased_until IS NULL OR leased_until < now())
        AND tenant_id NOT IN (SELECT tenant_id FROM busy)
      ORDER BY priority, run_at
        FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED
      LIMIT 1
 )
RETURNING id, type, payload, attempts;
```

> **Aside 3.** The cap is evaluated *inside* the statement that grants the lease. Reading the count in Java and then claiming is a check-then-act race that two workers will lose simultaneously, and only under the load that made you build the cap in the first place. The cost is real, though: this aggregate runs on every dispatch, and on a large leased set it is the kind of query that quietly turns the queue table into the bottleneck. Measure dispatch rate before and after; when it stops being acceptable, the counters move to Redis and the atomicity moves into a Lua script.

The Java-shaped version of the same cap is a `Semaphore` per tenant, and it's worth seeing why it isn't equivalent:

```java
Semaphore permits = tenantPermits.computeIfAbsent(tenantId, t -> new Semaphore(4));
```

> **Aside 4.** This enforces four concurrent jobs per tenant *per process*. Run twelve worker pods and the real limit is forty-eight — a number nobody chose, which changes whenever someone scales the deployment. In-process coordination expresses a per-process constraint; the constraint you meant is fleet-wide, so the counter has to live where every worker can see it. This is the same lesson as 9.1c's fan-out, arriving from the other direction.

What none of this does is interrupt work already running. A job that has held a worker for thirty-four minutes will hold it for the thirty-fifth no matter what arrives, which is why the strongest fix remains the one with no code in this section: don't have thirty-five-minute jobs.

## Further Reading

- **Kubernetes documentation, "API Priority and Fairness"** — a production system that implements exactly this article's two axes as separate concepts: priority levels for importance, and fair queueing by *flow* for tenant isolation, with concurrency shares per level. The best available worked example.
- **Amazon Builders' Library, "Workload isolation using shuffle-sharding"** — why random subsets beat both a shared pool and per-tenant pools, with the combinatorics.
- **Shreedhar & Varghese, "Efficient Fair Queuing using Deficit Round Robin" (1995)** — fairness when work units differ in size, which is the case every job queue actually has.
- **Demers, Keshav & Shenker, "Analysis and Simulation of a Fair Queueing Algorithm" (1989)** — the original statement of what "fair" should mean when sources compete for a server.
- **Google SRE Book, ch. 21, "Handling Overload"** — criticality levels, per-customer quotas, and the argument that a global limit without a per-customer one protects nothing.
- **Sidekiq wiki, "Advanced Options"** — the strict-versus-weighted queue ordering setting, which is this article's Level 3 as a single configuration line, and the documentation is candid about the starvation trade.

A Go sibling would be worth writing, because the fair dispatcher becomes a data structure rather than a query: one dispatcher goroutine holding a ring of per-tenant channels, handing work to a worker pool over a single channel. Round-robin fairness stops being something you express in SQL and becomes something you can read off the type declarations. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: when someone asks for priorities, ask which of the three they actually want. Nearly always they are describing isolation — "my job shouldn't wait behind theirs" — and isolation is not something an ordering can provide, because ordering only controls what happens next, and their problem is what is already happening.
