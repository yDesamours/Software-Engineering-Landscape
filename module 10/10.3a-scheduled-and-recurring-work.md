# Scheduled and Recurring Work

_Module 10.3a — Background Jobs & Task Queues_

## The Problem

A subscription product. Every night at 02:00, a job finds the subscriptions renewing that day, charges the card on file, and issues an invoice.

```java
@Scheduled(cron = "0 0 2 * * *")
public void runDailyBilling() { … }
```

It works for a year. Then five things happen, roughly in this order.

**You scale to three replicas for availability.** All three contain the scheduler. All three fire at 02:00. Every renewing customer is charged three times, and you find out from Twitter.

**So you add a lock.** `SETNX billing:daily`, thirty-minute TTL, first replica wins. This is correct until the month where the batch is big: the run takes ninety minutes, the lock expires at minute thirty, and a second replica picks it up while the first is still working. Two concurrent runs again — but now it happens rarely, only on the busiest night, and only in production.

This deserves saying plainly, because it's the most common misunderstanding about locks: **a lock with a timeout guarantees at most one lease holder, not at most one runner.** The first process doesn't stop when its lease expires. It doesn't know.

```
replica A   ├── lock (TTL 30m) ──┤ …still charging cards… 90 min ────────┤
replica B                        ├── acquires lock ── charges again ─────┤
                                 ↑
                        nothing told A to stop
```

**The hourly job starts taking seventy minutes.** The next tick fires while the previous run is still going. Whether that produces two concurrent runs, a skipped tick, or a queued one depends on a setting in your scheduler that nobody has read, and the default is usually "run it anyway."

**There's a deploy at 01:55 that takes until 02:30.** The 02:00 tick simply doesn't happen. Cron has no memory: a tick either fires while a process is alive to receive it or it is gone forever. Nothing catches up, nobody is charged that night, and the discrepancy surfaces three weeks later as an unexplained dip in revenue.

**And then March.** The cron expression says 02:00 local. On the spring-forward Sunday, 02:00 does not exist in that timezone — the clock jumps 01:59:59 → 03:00:00 — so the job doesn't run. In October, 02:00 happens twice, and it runs twice. A job that has been flawless for two years fails on exactly two days a year, in opposite directions.

## What It Is

**Scheduled work**: work triggered by time rather than by a request or an event. Two shapes that get conflated:

- **Recurring** — every hour, every night at 02:00, the first of the month. Described by a cron expression or an interval.
- **One-off future work** — send a reminder 24 hours before the appointment; end the trial in 14 days.

That second shape is worth separating immediately, because it usually isn't a cron problem at all. A one-off future action is just an enqueue with a future `run_at` — the field that was already sitting in 10.1a's jobs table. Booking an appointment enqueues the reminder for that appointment, at that time. No scan, no schedule, exact timing. A great deal of hourly-sweep code exists because nobody noticed that the queue already supported this.

(The catch, and the reason sweeps persist: if the appointment moves, the delayed job has to move with it, which means finding and cancelling it. Delayed jobs are excellent when the trigger time is fixed at creation and awkward when it isn't.)

The vocabulary for the recurring case: a **tick** is one firing of the schedule; the **overlap policy** decides what happens when a tick arrives while the previous run is still going; the **catch-up** or **misfire policy** decides what happens to a tick that was missed; and **leader election** or a **lock** decides which replica acts.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The same schedule exists in three places: an annotation in the app, a Kubernetes CronJob, and a crontab on a machine someone set up in 2021.
- A job whose protection against double-running is a comment.
- Raising the replica count feels risky.
- Duplicate charges or emails reported clustering at the same time of day.
- The cron expression is in UTC and the requirement was in the customer's local time, or the reverse.
- "Did last night's job run?" cannot be answered without grepping logs.
- A job scheduled hourly that took twenty minutes last year and takes three hours now.
- After an incident, nobody can list which scheduled work was missed.
- One job is scheduled at 02:30 *because* the 02:00 job takes about twenty minutes.
- A schedule that was disabled by commenting it out, and it's still commented out.

## How It Works

Five decisions, and most outages come from one of them having been made by a default.

**1. Where the schedule lives.** In-process (`@Scheduled`, Quartz, node-cron) is easy and couples the schedule to your deploy and to your replica count. Platform-level (Kubernetes CronJob, EventBridge Scheduler, Cloud Scheduler) is durable and external, and invisible from the codebase. Queue-native recurring jobs (BullMQ repeatables, Sidekiq-cron) usually sit in the sweet spot: one place, visible to developers, and already coordinated across workers.

**2. Who runs it.** A lock or a leader. Given the lease problem above, the rule to internalize is that **mutual exclusion is an optimization and idempotency is the guarantee.** Build the run so that a second concurrent execution finds nothing to do, and use the lock to avoid the wasted work rather than to prevent the disaster.

**3. What a tick means when the last one is still running.** Three policies, and you must pick one on purpose:

```
allow    run concurrently            fine for independent work, catastrophic for scans
skip     drop the tick               fine when the next tick will cover it
queue    run it when the current run finishes    backlog grows if duration > interval
```

Kubernetes spells this `concurrencyPolicy: Allow | Forbid | Replace`; Quartz calls it a misfire instruction. The names differ, the decision doesn't, and `Allow` is very often the default.

**4. What happens to a tick that was missed.** Some schedulers catch up, some don't, some catch up with a deadline (`startingDeadlineSeconds`). But the durable fix is at a different level:

**Write scheduled jobs as convergence over state, not as actions at a time.** Not "charge everyone whose renewal is today" but "find every active subscription whose `next_renewal_at` has passed and which has no invoice for that period, and charge it." The difference is total:

```
action-at-a-time                    convergence
missed run  → that day is lost      missed run → next run picks it up
double run  → double charge         double run → second finds nothing
run by hand → unpredictable         run by hand → safe
backfill    → a bespoke script      backfill    → is just the normal query
```

This is the same idea 10.2a raised as an alternative to retry, and it matters more here than anywhere else in the module, because a scheduled job is precisely the kind of code that will one day not run.

**5. Time is not a number line.** Local wall-clock schedules have two broken days a year: one where the scheduled instant doesn't exist and one where it happens twice. "The 31st" doesn't exist in February and cron will silently skip it, which is how a monthly report acquires eleven data points. A multi-region product running something "at 02:00 local" is running it at thirty different instants. The workable rule: schedule in UTC, and express business-local rules *inside* the job, where you can see and test them.

**One more, about shape.** The tick should almost never do the work.

```
bad    02:00 tick ──▶ [ one job, 90 minutes, all-or-nothing, one retry for 40,000 charges ]

good   02:00 tick ──▶ enqueue 40,000 × [ charge one subscription ]
                          ↓
                      the fleet, with per-job retry (10.2a) and fairness (10.2b)
```

The dispatcher shape gives you parallelism, per-unit retry, progress, isolation, and the property that a failure on subscription 4,001 doesn't discard four thousand successes. It also creates a burst — forty thousand jobs enqueued at 02:00:00, all hitting one payment provider — so spread `run_at` across a window. Your own scheduled peak is not the only one; everyone's cron fires at the top of the hour.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Genuinely periodic aggregate work.** Nightly rollups, daily digests, monthly invoicing, index rebuilds, retention deletions.
- **Reconciliation sweeps.** Every event-driven pipeline eventually needs a periodic job that looks for drift: paid orders without labels, subscriptions with no invoice, files uploaded but never processed. This is the highest-value scheduled job most systems have, because it catches the failures no retry policy can see — including the job that was never enqueued.
- **Polling something that won't push.** A partner with no webhooks, an SFTP drop, a bank file at 06:00. The schedule is imposed from outside and you're matching it.
- **Housekeeping.** Expired sessions, orphaned uploads, soft-deleted rows past their retention window.
- **Externally mandated windows.** Reporting deadlines, settlement cut-offs, a batch file that must be delivered before 07:00.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **The trigger time is known when the record is created.** A reminder 24 hours before an appointment is a delayed job, not a sweep. It's exact, it scales with the number of appointments rather than the number of minutes in the day, and it needs no scheduler.
- **An event already tells you.** If a webhook or a state change can trigger the work, poll for it only as a backstop — every fifteen minutes, not every ten seconds.
- **Running it three times is harmless and cheap.** Refreshing a cache on all replicas needs no leader election, no lock, and no article. Build coordination when duplication costs something.
- **You're about to build a scheduler.** You already have at least two, and one of them is your queue.
- **The work could be done lazily instead.** A nightly job that flips `trial_expired` can often be deleted entirely by computing expiry at read time. No job, no missed run, no double run — at the cost of read-path complexity, and it only works while no side effect is required. The moment an email must be sent, you need the job back.

## Trade-offs

- **Nothing invokes a scheduled job, so nothing notices when it stops.** This is its defining operational hazard. It needs inverted monitoring — alert on the *absence* of a successful run — which almost no default dashboard provides.
- **Time coupling makes testing awkward.** The clock has to be injectable, and you will still have tests that pass except on the first of the month or in a CI runner set to UTC.
- **Scheduled work creates self-inflicted peaks.** Everything at midnight, everything at the top of the hour, your fleet idle for 23 hours and saturated for one. Jitter and spread are not optional at scale.
- **Catch-up can be worse than the miss.** A scheduler that fires two hundred missed hourly ticks at once turns a small outage into a large one. Cap the backfill, or make the job convergent so one run covers everything.
- **The schedule's home is a split-brain risk.** In code it needs a deploy to change and it rolls back silently with the app. In infrastructure it's invisible to the people writing the job. Whichever you pick, write down that it's the only one.
- **Locks bring their own failure modes:** the expired lease described above, the lock never released after a crash (hence TTLs), and the TTL that was right when the job took five minutes.
- **Timezone rules change by government decree**, sometimes with weeks of notice, and your scheduling behaviour changes when the tzdb updates. That's maintenance you inherit permanently.

## Alternatives

- **Delayed jobs** — a future `run_at` on an ordinary job. The right answer for anything whose time is known at creation.
- **Event-driven triggers plus a reconciler.** React immediately to the event; sweep periodically for what the events missed. Nearly always better than sweeping alone.
- **Platform schedulers** — Kubernetes CronJob, EventBridge Scheduler, Cloud Scheduler. Survive your application entirely, and they can't see your code, which is both the point and the cost.
- **Per-entity scheduled tasks at scale** — Cloud Tasks and similar accept a schedule time per task, so "millions of individually timed reminders" becomes a supported product feature rather than a sweep.
- **Workflow orchestrators** — Airflow, Dagster for dependent DAGs with real backfill semantics; Temporal for durable timers, where "wait 30 days, then do X" is a line of code that survives restarts. If your cron expressions encode dependencies ("02:30 because the 02:00 job takes twenty minutes"), you have a DAG and should say so.
- **`pg_cron`** and similar database-native schedulers — the schedule lives next to the data, and it runs whether or not your app does.
- **Lazy evaluation** — compute the derived state on read and delete the job.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — inventory the schedules.** Every cron expression, everywhere, with an owner and a stated consequence of not running. Most teams find one they'd forgotten and one that has been failing silently.
_Signal you're here:_ nobody can produce the list.

**Level 1 — make each job convergent and idempotent.** Query by state, not by "today." Add the unique constraint that makes a double run a no-op.
_Signal you need it:_ a missed run requires a human with a SQL console.

**Level 2 — one home for schedules, plus mutual exclusion.** A lock or leader, understood as an optimization on top of Level 1 rather than a substitute for it.
_Signal you need it:_ scaling replicas is frightening.

**Level 3 — a run ledger and absence alerting.** Record every run: name, start, finish, outcome, count of items processed. Alert when the last success is older than the interval allows.
_Signal you need it:_ "did it run?" takes more than one query to answer.

**Level 4 — the tick enqueues per-unit jobs**, spread over a window, with overlap and catch-up policies chosen explicitly and written down.
_Signal you need it:_ one long all-or-nothing job, or a tick that overlaps its predecessor.

**Level 5 — a durable workflow engine** for multi-step dependent schedules, real backfills, and per-entity timers in the millions.
_Signal you need it:_ schedules that encode dependencies, or a backfill request that would take a bespoke script to satisfy.

## Implementation

**TypeScript**, continuing 10.1a's Postgres-backed queue, because the interesting parts here are all coordination and query shape rather than language features.

Mutual exclusion first, using a Postgres advisory lock rather than a TTL-based one:

```ts
const { rows: [{ locked }] } = await db.query(
  `select pg_try_advisory_lock(hashtext('cron:billing.daily')) as locked`
);
if (!locked) return; // another replica is running it
```

> **Aside 1.** An advisory lock is held by a *session*, not for a duration. A crashed process releases it when its connection dies, so there's no TTL to guess and no window where the lease expires while the work continues — which removes the failure in the problem statement's second beat. Two caveats: it requires a dedicated connection (a pooler in transaction mode will quietly give the lock to someone else's query), and it is still not a correctness guarantee for the *work*. It stops two replicas duplicating effort. It does not stop a human running the job by hand.

The selection query is convergent — note what isn't in it:

```ts
const due = await db.query(
  `select s.id, s.tenant_id, s.period_key
     from subscriptions s
     left join invoices i
       on i.subscription_id = s.id and i.period = s.period_key
    where s.status = 'active'
      and s.next_renewal_at <= now()
      and i.id is null
    order by s.next_renewal_at
    limit 5000`
);
```

> **Aside 2.** There is no reference to "today" anywhere. A run skipped for two days picks up two days of work on the next tick. A run that happens twice finds nothing the second time. A run started by hand at 3pm does the same thing as the 02:00 one. The tick has been demoted from the source of truth to a nudge, which is exactly the status it deserves given how reliably it will one day fail to arrive.

Then the tick enqueues rather than charges, with spread:

```ts
for (const s of due.rows) {
  const jitter = Math.floor(Math.random() * 20 * 60 * 1000); // 20-minute window
  await enqueue(db, "billing.charge",
    { subscriptionId: s.id, period: s.period_key },
    { runAt: new Date(Date.now() + jitter) });
}
```

And the line that all of the above is ultimately leaning on:

```sql
create unique index invoices_period on invoices (subscription_id, period);
```

> **Aside 3.** This is what actually prevents a double charge. The advisory lock, the overlap policy, and the leader election are defence in depth; the unique index is the only one of them still true when the lock is lost, the clock steps backwards an hour, and an engineer runs the job manually during an incident. Note also that it needs the charge and the invoice insert to be in one transaction, or the constraint protects the record and not the money.

The run ledger, which is what makes the job observable at all:

```ts
const { rows: [run] } = await db.query(
  `insert into job_runs (name, started_at) values ($1, now()) returning id`,
  ["billing.daily"]
);
// … work …
await db.query(
  `update job_runs set finished_at = now(), items = $2, outcome = 'ok' where id = $1`,
  [run.id, due.rows.length]
);
```

Alert on the inverse of the usual condition: `max(finished_at) where outcome = 'ok'` being older than about 26 hours. A job that stops being scheduled produces no errors, no logs, and no alerts — only silence that looks identical to success.

Finally, the two broken days:

```ts
const next = Temporal.ZonedDateTime.from({
  timeZone: "Europe/Berlin",
  year, month, day, hour: 2, minute: 0,
  disambiguation: "reject",
});
```

> **Aside 4.** On one Sunday in March that instant doesn't exist; on one in October it exists twice. Every date library has a policy for both, and most have a *default* policy — meaning the behaviour of your billing run on those two days was chosen by a library author who didn't know it was billing. `reject` throws, which is the right setting for a test and forces the question to be answered deliberately: `later`, `earlier`, or move the schedule to 04:00 where the problem doesn't arise.

## Further Reading

- **Martin Kleppmann, "How to do distributed locking"** — why a lease with a timeout is not mutual exclusion, and what a fencing token would have to do about it. The article the second beat of this piece is an instance of.
- **Kubernetes documentation, "CronJob"** — `concurrencyPolicy`, `startingDeadlineSeconds`, and an admirably blunt statement that a CronJob may create two Jobs or none for a single schedule, and your job must be idempotent.
- **Quartz Scheduler documentation, "Misfire Instructions"** — the most complete enumeration anywhere of what can be done with a tick that didn't happen on time.
- **PostgreSQL documentation, "Advisory Locks"** (Explicit Locking) — session versus transaction scope, and the connection-pooling caveat above.
- **`crontab(5)` and Vixie cron's DST handling notes** — a real scheduler documenting what it does on the two broken days. Short, and clarifying about how little the tool can promise.
- **IANA Time Zone Database announcements** — worth subscribing to once, to see how often the rules you're scheduling against change and with how little notice.
- **Temporal documentation, "Schedules" and durable timers** — what scheduling looks like when the timer itself is persistent, and the clearest illustration of what cron structurally cannot do.

A Java sibling would earn its place, because Quartz gives misfire handling a first-class vocabulary rather than leaving it implicit, and `java.time`'s `ZonedDateTime` makes the DST gap and overlap rules explicit at construction time rather than hiding them in a default — the two weakest points of this implementation are the two things that ecosystem names best. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: for every scheduled job, ask two questions — what happens if it doesn't run tonight, and what happens if it runs twice tonight. If either answer is worse than "nothing much," the job is written as an action performed at a time, and it wants to be rewritten as a statement about what should be true.
