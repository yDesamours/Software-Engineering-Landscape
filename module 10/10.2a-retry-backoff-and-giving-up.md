# Retry, Backoff, and Giving Up

_Module 10.2a — Background Jobs & Task Queues_

## The Problem

A print-on-demand shop. Every paid order enqueues a `label.purchase` job that calls a carrier's API, buys a shipping label, and stores the tracking number. Roughly 50 orders a minute on a weekday.

**Beat one: no retry.** The carrier has a bad forty seconds — a load balancer swap, nothing dramatic. Two hundred jobs fail with a connection reset and are marked failed. Nothing reprocesses them. Two hundred parcels sit unlabelled until someone notices the next morning and re-runs them by hand from a spreadsheet.

Obvious fix: retry. Five attempts.

**Beat two: retry, immediately.** The next blip is identical. This time the 200 failing jobs each retry five times as fast as the workers can loop — a thousand requests into a carrier API that is already unwell, inside about two seconds. Two things have now gone wrong, and the second one is subtler than the first. The load you added arrived exactly when the dependency had the least capacity to absorb it, and all five attempts were spent in the first two seconds of a forty-second outage. The retries expired before the recovery.

That first effect has arithmetic worth internalizing:

```
failure rate      load multiplier with 3 automatic retries

    1%                 1.03×      invisible
   10%                 1.30×      noticeable
   50%                 2.50×      the graph everyone screenshots
   90%                 3.70×      ← dependency is at 10% capacity
                                    and receiving 3.7× its normal traffic
```

Retries are a load amplifier whose gain is a function of the failure rate. They are quietest when nothing is wrong and loudest at the moment the system can least afford them, which is how a forty-second blip becomes a four-minute outage.

**Beat three: retry after 30 seconds.** Better — the attempts now span the outage. But the 200 jobs failed within a few seconds of each other, so they all wake up within a few seconds of each other, forever. The carrier sees a flat 50/min baseline with a 200-request spike dropped on it every thirty seconds, each spike synchronized by the very outage it's responding to.

```
                    outage
                  ├────────┤
  normal    ▁▁▁▁▁▁          ▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁
  retries               ▁▁▁▁█▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁█▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁█▁▁▁▁▁
                             ↑          ↑         ↑
                      a herd that stays a herd because every
                      member was given the same delay
```

**Beat four: the job that will never succeed.** One order has a postcode the carrier rejects with `422 invalid destination`. It is retried five times over four minutes, then gives up — or worse, on a queue configured to retry indefinitely, it is retried every thirty seconds until someone notices the log volume. No amount of waiting will fix a malformed postcode. It's a different kind of failure wearing the same shape.

**Beat five: the multiplication nobody configured.** The HTTP client has `retries: 3` because a sensible default shipped with it. The job has `attempts: 5`. A single order therefore makes up to fifteen calls, and the job's apparent 90-second timeout is actually three consecutive 90-second timeouts. Nobody chose either number with the other in mind.

## What It Is

A **retry policy** is three separate decisions, and most incidents come from having made only one of them:

- **Whether** this particular failure is worth retrying at all.
- **When** to try again — the backoff schedule.
- **When to stop**, and what happens to the job then.

The vocabulary: **exponential backoff** for delays that grow, **jitter** for the randomness that keeps a cohort from moving in lockstep, a **deadline** or attempt cap for giving up, and a **dead-letter queue** for where jobs go when you do.

One neighbour to separate now, since it's the one people substitute for this: a **circuit breaker** (Module 36.3) is not a retry policy. Retry is a per-job decision about one failure. A breaker is a per-*dependency* decision that stops jobs from attempting the call in the first place, based on the recent aggregate failure rate. They solve adjacent halves of the same problem — backoff controls how hard one job pushes, a breaker controls whether the fleet pushes at all — and a system under real load usually wants both.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A dependency recovers, and is immediately knocked over again.
- The same job id appears in your error logs at exactly `:00` and `:30` of every minute.
- A retry count configured somewhere with no delay configured anywhere.
- A job with `attempts: 41,203`.
- Duplicate side effects after an outage — two labels bought, two charges, two emails — because a request that timed out had actually succeeded.
- A dead-letter queue with five figures in it and no dashboard showing that number.
- Support asks "did the label get bought?" and the honest answer is that nobody can tell.
- Retry counts configured in two layers by two people, and no one has multiplied them.
- Your failure rate looks like zero because everything eventually succeeds on attempt three, and you have no idea that it does.

## How It Works

**Classify the error first.** Everything downstream depends on this, and it is domain knowledge rather than a library setting.

- **Transient** — connection reset, timeout, `503`, `429`, a database serialization failure or lock timeout. The same call may well work in a moment.
- **Terminal** — `400`, `422`, a validation failure, a missing record, malformed input. The same call will fail identically until something outside the job changes.
- **Ambiguous** — and this is the category people forget. A timeout is not a failure; it's an *absence of information*. The label may have been purchased and the response lost. Retrying is usually right, and it is exactly what produces the duplicate charge, which is why ambiguous failures are why idempotency keys exist.

Where the protocol gives you a hint, take it: `429` and `503` may carry `Retry-After`, and a value the server chose beats a formula you invented.

**Then choose the delay.** Three generations of policy, each fixing the previous one's failure:

```
fixed        30s, 30s, 30s, 30s        cohort stays synchronized forever
exponential  2s, 4s, 8s, 16s, 32s      spans the outage, still synchronized
+ jitter     1.4s, 0.9s, 6s, 3.2s      cohort spreads out
```

Exponential growth is what lets a small number of attempts cover a long outage: five attempts at a fixed 2 seconds covers 10 seconds, while five doubling from 2 seconds covers about a minute. Jitter is what stops the two hundred jobs that failed together from retrying together. The common form is **full jitter** — pick uniformly from `[0, delay)` rather than waiting exactly `delay`:

```
delay   = min(cap, base × 2^attempt)
wait    = random(0, delay)
```

It looks like it weakens the backoff, and it does halve the average wait. That's a good trade: the point was never the waiting, it was the spreading.

**Cap two different things.** A per-delay cap (don't let doubling reach four hours) and an overall stopping condition. For the stopping condition, a **deadline** usually beats an attempt count: "this label must be bought within six hours or a human needs to know" is a statement about the business, whereas "five attempts" is a number whose meaning silently changes every time someone tunes the backoff curve.

**Then stop properly.** The job moves to a terminal state and its payload, its error, and its attempt history are retained somewhere durable — the **dead-letter queue**. What makes a DLQ useful is not that it exists; it's that something alerts when it's non-empty, and that there is a command to replay from it once the underlying cause is fixed. A DLQ nobody watches is a slower, more expensive way of dropping the work.

**And control the aggregate.** Per-job backoff limits one job's pressure but says nothing about the fleet's. A **retry budget** caps retries as a proportion of total requests to a dependency — say, retries may not exceed 10% of normal traffic — so that a total outage produces 1.1× load instead of 3.7×. This is what gRPC and Finagle implement, and it's the piece that turns retry from a local decision into a system-level one.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any call over a network.** The base rate of transient failure on a busy network is not zero, and a job that fails permanently on a single reset packet is not doing its job.
- **Database contention.** A serialization failure under `SERIALIZABLE`, or a deadlock victim, should be retried *immediately* with little or no backoff — the contention is momentary and the retry usually wins. This is the one case where the naive policy is the correct one, because you're not queueing behind an overloaded dependency, you're re-running a transaction that lost a coin flip.
- **Rate limits.** A `429` is a dependency telling you your policy is wrong. Honour `Retry-After` and slow down.
- **Work with a long tolerance window.** Nightly exports, reconciliation, anything where "within six hours" is the real requirement. Long backoff curves are free here.
- **Third-party outages you can outlast.** Sidekiq's default schedule is 25 retries spread over about three weeks, which sounds absurd until you have a vendor with a two-day incident and no lost jobs.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **The operation isn't idempotent and has no idempotency key.** Retrying a charge, a label purchase, or an outbound payment without a stable key is not resilience, it's a duplicate generator. Either add the key or fail loudly and let a human decide.
- **The error is deterministic.** Retrying a `422` is pure waste: worker time, log volume, and a poisoned queue. The correct response to invalid input is a terminal state and, usually, a human-facing review queue.
- **A user is waiting synchronously.** Inside a request you have a latency budget measured in seconds. One or two fast retries, then fail. Backoff curves belong on the other side of the queue.
- **The failing dependency is your own database, already overloaded.** Retrying adds load to the thing that's failing because of load. This is where a breaker or a concurrency limit is the answer, not a backoff curve.
- **You'd be retrying past the point of relevance.** A "your order shipped" notification retried successfully at hour nine is worse than the alert you didn't send at minute ten.

## Trade-offs

- **Retries amplify load precisely when capacity is lowest.** The table at the top of this article is the cost of the feature, not a misconfiguration of it. Jitter and budgets reduce the gain; nothing removes it.
- **Completion latency becomes long-tailed.** "The job succeeded" can mean four hours later. Anything downstream with an SLA — an export the customer is waiting on, a webhook with a delivery guarantee — now inherits that tail.
- **More attempts means more duplicates.** Every retry of an ambiguous failure is a chance to do the work twice. Retry policy and idempotency are the same design decision viewed from two sides.
- **Ordering degrades further.** Job A, retried twice, lands after job B that was enqueued later. If your jobs assume sequence, backoff will break that assumption long before your queue's concurrency does.
- **Delayed jobs outlive deploys.** A job waiting out a two-hour backoff will be executed by code that shipped after it was created — the payload-versioning problem from 10.1a, made much more likely by long curves.
- **Retries mask chronic failure.** A dependency degrading from 0.1% to 4% errors is invisible if attempt two always succeeds. Instrument attempts, not just outcomes, or you lose the early warning entirely.
- **Replay is itself a thundering herd.** Twelve thousand dead-lettered jobs re-driven with one command will reproduce the outage that created them. Replay needs throttling, which means it needs tooling, which means someone has to build it before the night they need it.

## Alternatives

- **Fail fast and surface it.** For user-visible synchronous work, an immediate error a person can act on beats invisible persistence.
- **Circuit breakers** (36.3) — the aggregate complement, not a competitor. The breaker decides whether to attempt; the policy decides how to attempt.
- **A concurrency or rate limit toward the dependency.** If the carrier handles 20 requests/sec, a token bucket on the worker side enforces that continuously, while backoff only reacts after you've already exceeded it.
- **Idempotency keys and at-most-once semantics.** For money-moving operations, make the retry safe rather than rare.
- **Reconciliation instead of retry.** Rather than retrying the act, run a periodic job that compares desired state to actual state and fixes the difference: "find every paid order with no tracking number and buy a label." It's self-healing regardless of *why* a label is missing — including the failures your retry policy never saw, like the job that was never enqueued. Convergence is strictly more robust than retry for anything that is fundamentally state synchronization; it costs you a scan and a clear definition of "correct."
- **A human review queue.** For terminal failures with a human-fixable cause — the malformed postcode — the right destination isn't a DLQ, it's a screen in the admin tool with an edit box.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — count failures by class.** Group a week of job errors by error type. You cannot choose a policy without knowing whether your failures are transient blips or deterministic bad data, and most teams are surprised by the ratio.
_Signal you're here:_ a debate about the right number of attempts, conducted with no data.

**Level 1 — exponential backoff with full jitter and an attempt cap.** One global policy. Most queue libraries do this with a configuration object; verify the jitter, because several do exponential without it.
_Signal you need it:_ any fixed delay, or any retry with no delay at all.

**Level 2 — classify errors and route terminal failures straight to dead.** No backoff, no attempts consumed.
_Signal you need it:_ a validation error that was retried five times.

**Level 3 — a real dead-letter store and an alert.** Payload, last error, attempt history, and a `replay` command. Alert on the DLQ being non-empty at all, not on a threshold; if a threshold is needed, the number is too high.
_Signal you need it:_ you cannot answer "what did we lose during Tuesday's outage."

**Level 4 — per-job-type policies and deadlines instead of counts.** The label purchase gets six hours; the "order shipped" push notification gets fifteen minutes and then is not worth sending. Honour `Retry-After` where the dependency sends it.
_Signal you need it:_ one policy that is simultaneously too patient for one job type and too impatient for another.

**Level 5 — aggregate control.** A retry budget or circuit breaker per dependency, worker-side rate limiting, and throttled replay.
_Signal you need it:_ a post-mortem in which your own retries are named as a contributing factor.

## Implementation

**Go.** Chosen because error classification is the load-bearing part of this article and Go makes it explicit at the call site — `errors.As` against a typed error, with no annotation quietly deciding the policy elsewhere. `context` deadlines also make the per-attempt and overall budgets visible as two separate things, which they are.

Classification first, because it decides everything else:

```go
type Decision int

const (
	Succeeded Decision = iota
	Retryable
	Terminal
)

func classify(err error) (Decision, time.Duration) {
	if err == nil {
		return Succeeded, 0
	}

	var apiErr *carrier.APIError
	if errors.As(err, &apiErr) {
		switch {
		case apiErr.Status == 429, apiErr.Status >= 500:
			return Retryable, apiErr.RetryAfter // zero if the header was absent
		case apiErr.Status >= 400:
			return Terminal, 0 // a malformed postcode stays malformed
		}
	}

	if errors.Is(err, context.DeadlineExceeded) || errors.Is(err, syscall.ECONNRESET) {
		return Retryable, 0
	}

	return Retryable, 0 // unknown: retry, and let the deadline be the backstop
}
```

> **Aside 1.** The unknown case retries. That's a deliberate bet — an unrecognized error is more often a transport problem than a permanent one — and it's only safe because something else stops it. If you default unknowns to retryable *and* your stopping condition is weak, every new error type your dependency invents becomes a poison job. Note also that the `>= 400` branch is a simplification worth auditing against the specific API: `409` is often retryable, and a `401` may mean a token that a refresh would fix.

The delay function, which is four lines and the subject of a great deal of production pain:

```go
func nextDelay(attempt int) time.Duration {
	const base = 2 * time.Second
	const ceiling = 10 * time.Minute

	d := min(ceiling, base<<attempt) // 2s, 4s, 8s, 16s …
	return time.Duration(rand.Int63n(int64(d)))
}
```

> **Aside 2.** `rand.Int63n(int64(d))` is full jitter: uniform over `[0, d)`, so the average wait is half the ceiling. That feels like it weakens the backoff, and the trade is deliberate — the retry that matters is the *distribution* of a cohort, not the patience of one job. If you need a floor as well, `d/2 + rand(d/2)` gives you spread without letting any attempt come back immediately.

Then the failure branch of the worker loop from 10.1a, with the three decisions visible in one `switch`:

```go
deadline := job.EnqueuedAt.Add(6 * time.Hour)

attemptCtx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(ctx, 20*time.Second)
err := purchaseLabel(attemptCtx, job)
cancel()

decision, retryAfter := classify(err)

switch {
case decision == Succeeded:
	q.Ack(ctx, job)

case decision == Terminal:
	q.DeadLetter(ctx, job, err)

case time.Now().After(deadline):
	q.DeadLetter(ctx, job, fmt.Errorf("gave up after %d attempts: %w", job.Attempts, err))

default:
	delay := retryAfter
	if delay == 0 {
		delay = nextDelay(job.Attempts)
	}
	q.Reschedule(ctx, job, time.Now().Add(delay))
}
```

> **Aside 3.** Two timeouts, doing different jobs. `attemptCtx` bounds one call so a hung connection can't hold a worker for an hour. `deadline` bounds the whole effort in wall-clock terms and is what the business actually cares about. Keep them independent: when someone later raises the per-attempt timeout to 60 seconds, the overall promise shouldn't silently change with it.

Finally, the line that makes all of the above safe:

```go
req.Header.Set("Idempotency-Key", fmt.Sprintf("label-%d", job.ID))
```

> **Aside 4.** The key is derived from the job id, so it is identical on attempt one and attempt nine. Generating it inside the request function — a fresh UUID per call — is the bug that this whole mechanism exists to prevent, and it passes code review every time because it looks like exactly the same line. Remember the ambiguity: a timed-out attempt may have bought the label. Without a stable key, the retry that "fixed" the outage is the one that charged the shop twice.

What's left undone here is the case where the queue itself is the bottleneck rather than the dependency — when one job type's retries are consuming the workers that another job type needs. Backoff doesn't help with that, because the problem isn't timing, it's sharing.

## Further Reading

- **Marc Brooker, "Exponential Backoff And Jitter"** (AWS Architecture Blog) — the simulation that made full jitter the default recommendation. Short, with graphs that make the argument better than prose can.
- **Amazon Builders' Library, "Timeouts, retries, and backoff with jitter"** — the same material as operational doctrine, including the case for retry budgets.
- **Google SRE Book, ch. 22, "Addressing Cascading Failures"** — retry amplification as a named failure mode, and why a recovering service is the most fragile one.
- **gRPC proposal A6, "Retry Design"** — a complete, precise specification of retry policy including throttling. Worth reading even if you use none of gRPC.
- **RFC 9110 §10.2.3** — `Retry-After`. Two paragraphs, routinely ignored.
- **Stripe API documentation, "Idempotent requests"** — the reference model for making an ambiguous retry safe, including how long keys are retained and what happens when the same key arrives with a different body.
- **Sidekiq wiki, "Error Handling"** — the actual retry schedule formula and the reasoning behind 25 attempts over three weeks.

A Java sibling would be genuinely revealing, because Spring Retry and Resilience4j turn the policy into an annotation, and the thing an annotation is worst at expressing is the nesting from beat five — `@Retryable` on a method calling another `@Retryable` method multiplies attempts with nothing at either site to indicate it. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: whenever you see a retry configured, look for the other two decisions. There is almost always a count and almost never a classification or a deadline, and the missing two are where the incidents come from. A retry is a bet that the failure was temporary — worth making, as long as someone has decided how the system behaves when the bet is wrong.
