# Factors, Passwordless, and Phishing Resistance

_Module 4.1 — Authentication & Authorization_

## The Problem

The previous two articles took the login for granted. One asked how identity is carried after it's established; the other asked who establishes it. This one is about the moment itself: what, exactly, does someone do to convince you they're the account holder?

Start from a system that does the default thing correctly. Passwords hashed with Argon2id at sensible parameters. No password in any log. Rate limiting on the login endpoint. Reset tokens single-use and short-lived. Everything Module 4.2 will tell you to do, done.

You still get account takeovers. Here's why none of that work prevented them.

### The passwords weren't broken here

Late one evening, your login endpoint takes 40,000 attempts over six hours. Every email address is real — they're your users. Each individual account sees one or two attempts, from a different IP each time. Your per-account rate limiter never fires, because nothing is being brute-forced.

The attacker isn't guessing. They have a list of email/password pairs from a breach at some other company — a forum, a retailer, a fitness app — and they're testing which of your users reused theirs. Success rates on this are usually somewhere in the low single digits, which sounds like failure until you multiply it by 40,000.

Your hashing was perfect. It was irrelevant. **The password's weakness was never in your storage; it's that the same secret exists somewhere you don't control.**

### So you add a second factor, and it works

You ship TOTP. Six digits from an authenticator app. The credential-stuffing wave stops dead — a leaked password alone no longer produces a session. This is a genuinely enormous improvement and you should ship it.

Three months later, a finance manager at one of your customers receives an email about an unpaid invoice, clicks through to a login page that looks exactly like yours, and enters her email, her password, and her six-digit code. Twenty seconds later someone in another country is inside her account changing the bank details on your payouts page.

The site she landed on wasn't a static clone collecting credentials for later. It was a proxy. Everything she typed was forwarded to your real login endpoint in real time, and your real session cookie was forwarded back to the attacker.

```
  PHISHABLE FACTORS                      ORIGIN-BOUND CREDENTIAL

  user ─┬── password ──┐                 user ── tap ──► authenticator
        └── 6 digits ──┤                                      │  which site is asking?
                       ▼                                      │  "acme-l0gin.com"
              acme-l0gin.com                                  ▼
                (live proxy)                        no credential registered
                       │ replays both                 for that origin
                       ▼ within seconds                       │
                   acme.com                                   ▼
                       │                                  nothing to type,
                       └── session ──► attacker           nothing to forward,
                                                          nothing to steal
```

The second factor raised the attacker's cost from "run a script against a leaked list" to "register a domain, stand up a proxy, and phish a specific person." That's a large increase and it eliminates the bulk attacks entirely. But notice what didn't change: **the code is still a secret the user types into whatever box is in front of them, and nothing about it knows which site it's for.** Same category of credential, different length and lifetime.

Two independent questions fall out of this, and conflating them is why "we have MFA" is not an answer to "are you phishing-resistant":

1. **How many factors** does authentication require?
2. **Is the factor bound to your origin**, or can a person be talked into replaying it somewhere else?

## What It Is

A **factor** is a category of evidence: something you know (password, PIN), something you have (a phone, a security key, control of an inbox), something you are (fingerprint, face).

One clarification worth making immediately, because the "something you are" category misleads people: in practice biometrics are not sent to your server and are not a factor you receive. A fingerprint unlocks a key stored on the device. What reaches you is proof that a *device* signed something, plus a flag saying the device verified its user locally. Biometrics are a local unlock of a "have" factor. Nobody is transmitting your face to an API, and any design that would is a bad design.

**Multi-factor authentication** requires evidence from more than one category. Two passwords are not two factors. A password plus an emailed code is arguably one and a half, since the inbox is often protected by a password too.

**Passwordless** means replacing the knowledge factor, not removing authentication. A magic link and a passkey are both passwordless and they sit at opposite ends of the security range, which is why the word on its own tells you almost nothing.

**Phishing-resistant** is a specific, checkable property: the credential is cryptographically bound to the site requesting it, and the binding is enforced by software rather than by the user's judgement. There is nothing for the user to retype, so there is nothing for them to retype into the wrong place.

That last property is the one that splits the field. Passwords, TOTP codes, SMS codes, emailed links, and push approvals are all phishable — some much more expensively than others, but all in the same category. WebAuthn credentials are not. Everything else in this article is detail.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Your login endpoint sees bursts of attempts spread across many accounts and many IPs, one or two per account. That's credential stuffing, and per-account rate limits won't see it.
- Password reset is one of your highest-volume features.
- A support ticket says "someone changed my email address and I can't get back in."
- Your MFA is SMS and you've had a SIM-swap incident — or your SMS bill jumped and the traffic is going to countries where you have no users. (That second one is fraud: attackers farm premium-rate revenue by triggering your signup messages.)
- A security questionnaire asks about MFA *and* about phishing-resistant MFA as separate rows, and you realize they're separate questions.
- You'd like shorter sessions, but logging back in is painful enough that you don't dare shorten them.
- Someone changed a payout account, an email address, or an API key using a session that was established hours earlier, and there was no additional check at the moment it mattered.
- You've deployed strong authentication, and account recovery is still "we'll email you a link."

That last one deserves its own sentence, because it recurs through the rest of this article: **your recovery path is your real authentication strength.** Everything else is the strength of the front door on a house with a permanently unlocked back door.

## How It Works

### Magic links and emailed codes

Generate a high-entropy token, store its hash with a short expiry and a single-use flag, email it, consume it on arrival. Mechanically it's a session token from the first article with a one-shot lifecycle.

What it buys: no stored password, nothing to reuse, nothing to leak in a breach. What it costs is more interesting than it first appears:

- **Email deliverability is now on your login critical path.** A message in a spam folder is an outage for that user, and you'll find out from support rather than monitoring.
- **Link scanners consume tokens.** Corporate mail security follows every URL in every message to check it. If your link logs the user in on `GET`, the scanner just used it, and the user clicks a dead link. The fix is a confirmation step, or an entered code rather than a clicked link.
- **Cross-device breakage.** Requested on a laptop, opened on a phone — now the session exists in the wrong browser. Emailing a short code that the user types into the original tab avoids this entirely, which is why most mature implementations do that instead.
- **Forwarding.** A link is a bearer credential in an inbox that may be shared, archived, or synced somewhere unexpected.

And it is thoroughly phishable: a proxy page simply asks the user to paste the code it just caused to be sent.

One honest reframe, though. If your password reset is an emailed link — and it is — then email already controls every account you have. A magic link isn't a downgrade from that baseline; it's the same security with one fewer secret to manage. The question is whether you're comfortable with the baseline, not whether magic links are worse than it.

### TOTP

The server and the authenticator app share a secret, established at enrollment via a QR code. Both compute `HMAC(secret, current 30-second interval)` and truncate the result to six digits. No network involved, which is why it works on a plane.

Three implementation details that produce support tickets if you skip them:

- **Accept a small window** — typically one interval either side — because device clocks drift.
- **Record the last interval used** and reject anything at or below it, or a code observed in flight can be replayed for the rest of its life.
- **The enrollment QR is the secret in plaintext.** Screenshots of it live in photo libraries and in shared Slack channels forever.

Note also that the server stores a secret capable of generating valid codes. Unlike a password, it can't be hashed — you need it to compute. Your TOTP table is a table of live second factors, which is a meaningfully different thing to protect than a table of Argon2 hashes.

### SMS and voice codes

Delivered to whoever controls the phone number, which is not always the person. SIM swapping is a customer-service attack against the carrier, not against you, and you have no visibility into it. There are also signalling-layer interception issues, and the toll-fraud problem mentioned above.

Guidance from standards bodies has moved from "acceptable" to "restricted, with warnings," and that's the right level of nuance. SMS is materially weaker than every other option here. It is also, for a large segment of real users, the only second factor they will actually enroll in, and it defeats bulk credential stuffing exactly as well as TOTP does. "Weaker than a passkey" and "better than nothing" are both true, and a product decision has to hold both.

### Push approval

The server sends a prompt, the user taps approve. The UX is the best of the phishable options, which produces its own failure: **MFA fatigue.** An attacker with a valid password triggers prompts repeatedly, at 2am, until someone taps approve to make their phone stop buzzing. The standard mitigation is number matching — the login screen shows a two-digit number that must be selected in the app — which converts a reflex into a deliberate act, plus throttling repeated prompts.

### WebAuthn and passkeys

This is the one that changes the category, so it's worth the mechanism in full.

**Registration.** Your server sends a challenge and your Relying Party ID — your domain. The authenticator (a platform one built into the phone or laptop, or a removable security key) generates a fresh key pair *scoped to that RP ID*, keeps the private key, and returns the public key and a credential id. You store those against the user.

**Authentication.** Your server sends a random challenge. The browser looks for credentials registered for the current origin's RP ID. The authenticator asks the user for a gesture — touch, PIN, fingerprint — and signs a structure containing the challenge, the origin, and the RP ID. You verify the signature against the stored public key, and verify that the origin and challenge are the ones you expected.

Two properties follow, and both are structural rather than procedural:

**Nothing shared is stored.** Your credential table holds public keys. An attacker who dumps it gets a list of things that are already, by design, public. Compare that to the password table, the TOTP secret table, and the magic-link token table — all of which are dangerous at rest in a way this simply isn't.

**Origin binding is done by software.** The browser will not offer `acme.com` credentials to `acme-l0gin.com`. The user isn't being asked to notice the difference, and there's no code for them to forward — the signature is over a challenge that only the real site issued, for an origin that doesn't match. The phishing proxy has nothing to relay.

A few practical notes:

- **Discoverable credentials** (resident keys) store enough on the authenticator for the login page to offer an account without the user typing an identifier first — the "just tap" experience.
- **User verification** — the PIN or biometric — is what makes a single gesture count as two factors: possession of the authenticator plus the local knowledge or biometric that unlocked it.
- **A passkey** is a discoverable WebAuthn credential, usually synced through a platform keychain. Syncing is the change that made adoption viable, and it's a real trade: the private key is no longer bound to one piece of hardware, so your security now inherits the security of the user's Apple, Google, or password-manager account. Device-bound credentials on hardware keys remain the high-assurance option, and some regulated environments require them.
- **Attestation** — cryptographic proof of what kind of authenticator was used — is available, privacy-sensitive, and almost always unnecessary outside enterprise policy enforcement. Don't require it by default.
- **Support multiple credentials per user from day one.** People have a laptop, a phone, and sometimes a hardware key, and a passkey confined to one device is a lockout waiting for a broken screen.
- **The RP ID is your domain.** Changing domains means migrating credentials, which is to say re-enrolling everyone.

### Step-up authentication

Login is one decision, made once, possibly hours ago. Some actions deserve a fresh one: changing the account email, adding a payout destination, revealing an API key, deleting a workspace, disabling MFA.

The mechanism is small, and it's already half-built if you followed the first article: your session record gets `last_verified_at` and the set of methods used. Sensitive routes require a recent verification with a sufficiently strong method, and if the check fails you re-prompt rather than rejecting. In OIDC terms, this is what the `acr` and `amr` claims are for; in your own system it's two columns and a middleware.

This is also the cheapest available mitigation for stolen sessions, which — per the first article — is the risk that neither token format addresses. If a session cookie is worth an account takeover, step-up is what makes it worth less than that.

### Recovery

The unglamorous section that determines everything.

**Recovery codes** generated at enrollment: ten high-entropy strings, hashed at rest, single-use, shown once. Cheap, effective, and the user has to actually store them somewhere.

**Multiple enrolled authenticators** are better than any recovery mechanism, because they aren't a weaker parallel path — they're the same path again.

**Email-based recovery** is the default and it silently caps your security at the strength of the user's email provider. That's acceptable for a lot of products. It is not acceptable for the ones where you deployed passkeys specifically because the account is worth attacking.

**Admin-mediated recovery** is the underrated answer for B2B. A workspace administrator who knows the person can reset them, which is a far stronger check than an emailed link, and it moves the social-engineering surface to someone with context. For SSO tenants, recovery isn't yours at all — it's the identity provider's, which is one of the quieter arguments for the previous article.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Breach-list checking and stuffing defences: always.** Rejecting passwords that appear in known breach corpora, plus rate limiting across the whole endpoint rather than per account, removes the majority of real attacks for a few days of work.
- **TOTP: the pragmatic default second factor.** Free, offline, no carrier, no per-authentication cost, and most users already have an app. If you must ship MFA this quarter, ship this.
- **SMS: when the population won't install anything**, or where phone-number possession is independently useful to you. Offer it, don't make it the only option, and don't let it override a stronger factor during recovery.
- **Magic links: low-frequency consumer products** where users would otherwise arrive at the reset flow anyway. A tool used monthly, not one used hourly.
- **Passkeys: when phishing is your actual threat model.** Money movement, administrative capability, customer data, support staff with impersonation powers, or anyone your users would describe as "the account someone would target." Also when you want to reduce login friction, which is the part people don't expect — a tap is faster than a password manager.
- **Step-up: any irreversible or financially meaningful action**, regardless of what else you've deployed.
- **Enforcement policies: B2B.** Let a tenant admin require MFA, or require phishing-resistant MFA specifically, for their users. This is a checkbox on a lot of purchasing decisions.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

**Prompting for MFA when SSO already enforced it.** The enterprise IdP challenged the user thirty seconds ago. A second prompt from you is friction that teaches people to click through prompts, which is precisely the habit you don't want. Read the assurance claims in the token and trust them.

**Implementing WebAuthn from the specification.** The spec is large, the failure modes are subtle, and the libraries are good. This is a "use the library" situation as clearly as anything in the module.

**Passkeys as the only option, today, for a mass-consumer product.** Shared computers, kiosks, older devices, users who don't know what a passkey is and will not learn during checkout. Offer it, default to it eventually, but keep a path for everyone else for now.

**Magic links for a tool people open twenty times a day.** Every login becomes an email round trip, and the user's inbox becomes their workflow.

**Stacking friction until people route around it.** Short sessions plus mandatory MFA on every login plus a 90-day password rotation produces passwords on sticky notes and approval taps given without reading. Security measures that exceed the perceived stakes get defeated by the user, not the attacker.

**A full authenticator-management surface for an internal tool with eleven users behind SSO.** You already have MFA; it's someone else's.

## Trade-offs

- **Every factor you add is also a way to be locked out.** This is the central cost and it's paid by users, in a form they experience as your product failing. Enrollment friction costs signups; lost devices cost support hours; a user who can't get back in may simply leave.
- **Recovery caps everything.** Say it once more, because it's the most commonly skipped design step: an account protected by a hardware key and recoverable by an emailed link is protected by an emailed link.
- **Magic links** put email deliverability, spam filtering, and corporate link rewriting on your login path — three systems you don't operate and can't debug.
- **TOTP** leaves you holding a table of live secrets, generates clock-skew tickets, and produces a steady stream of "I got a new phone" recoveries.
- **SMS** costs money per message, is subject to pumping fraud, has wildly variable international deliverability, and can be defeated by a phone call to a carrier.
- **Passkeys** bring platform fragmentation, a vocabulary users don't have yet, and a dependency on the user's platform account for synced credentials. They also make deliberate credential sharing hard — which is a security win and, for products where a team genuinely shares one login, a support problem you will hear about.
- **The enterprise/consumer split is real.** Enterprises want attestation and device-bound keys; consumers want sync and recoverability. Serving both means supporting both, with policy per tenant.
- **Step-up adds friction exactly where users are most anxious**, and a badly placed re-prompt reads as "something has gone wrong."
- **You've acquired an authenticator lifecycle.** Register, list, label, remove, show last-used, notify on changes, prevent removal of the last one. That's a settings surface, a set of notification emails, and an audit trail — not a weekend.

## Alternatives

- **Delegate all of it.** Use an identity provider and inherit its factors, its enrollment UI, its recovery flows, and its compliance posture. For most teams this is the correct answer, and it's the previous article.
- **Password plus breach-list checking plus stuffing defences.** Not glamorous and not phishing-resistant, but it removes the attack that actually happened at the top of this article, and it costs a fraction of an MFA rollout. A legitimate stopping point for low-stakes products — see Module 4.2 for the mechanics.
- **Risk-based (adaptive) authentication.** Challenge only when signals look unusual: new device, new country, impossible travel, a fingerprint you've never seen. Invisible when it works, and when it misfires the user experiences an unexplained lockout with no way to argue. Needs enough traffic to have a baseline, and pairs well with step-up rather than replacing it.
- **Device binding / "remember this device."** A durable second-factor exemption tied to a specific browser. Reduces friction substantially; make sure it's revocable from the session list you built in the first article.
- **Client certificates or smartcards.** Standard in government and some regulated industries, phishing-resistant for the same reason WebAuthn is, and operationally heavy in a way that only makes sense when someone else runs the PKI.
- **Fraud and anomaly detection on actions rather than logins.** Sometimes the right control isn't at the door — it's a hold on a payout destination changed less than 24 hours ago. Authentication isn't the only place to spend a security budget, and for money movement it's often not the most effective one.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — make the password less bad.** Check new and changed passwords against a known-breach corpus, rate-limit the endpoint globally rather than only per account, and email the user on login from an unrecognized device. Days of work, and it addresses the 40,000-request scenario directly.
*Signal you're behind:* you can't tell credential stuffing from normal traffic.

**Level 1 — optional TOTP, with the right data model.** One table, polymorphic from the start:

```
authenticators(id, user_id, type, label, secret_or_public_key,
               created_at, last_used_at, UNIQUE(user_id, label))
```

`type` is `totp` today and `webauthn` later. Getting this shape right now means adding passkeys is a new row type rather than a migration and a re-enrollment. Issue hashed, single-use recovery codes at the same moment — not as a follow-up ticket, because the follow-up ticket is where lockouts come from.
*Signal:* your first takeover report.

**Level 2 — step-up on the dangerous actions.** Add `last_verified_at` and `methods` to the session record, and gate email changes, payout details, API key reveal, and MFA removal behind a fresh check.
*Signal:* an incident where the session was old and the action was expensive.

**Level 3 — add passkeys alongside, never instead.** Discoverable credentials so the login page can offer them directly, multiple per user, clear labels, and a settings page that makes the set visible. Keep password and TOTP working.
*Signal:* a phishing incident, or a customer asking for phishing-resistant MFA by name.

**Level 4 — policy, per tenant.** Admins can require MFA, or require phishing-resistant methods specifically, for their organization. SSO tenants bypass all of it and defer to the identity provider — and make sure that's genuinely a bypass, because "SSO enforced but the password login still works" is a finding on every security review.
*Signal:* the enterprise questionnaire.

**Level 5 — password-optional.** Let a user delete their password once they hold two or more passkeys plus recovery codes. This is the endpoint worth aiming at, and reaching it before support and recovery are solid is how you generate a lockout event that ends up on social media.
*Signal:* passkey usage is a large share of logins and your recovery flows have survived contact with real users.

## Implementation

TypeScript, because WebAuthn is half a browser API and half a server verification, and seeing both halves in one language is what makes the origin binding legible. `@simplewebauthn` on both sides.

### Registration: what the server offers

```ts
import { generateRegistrationOptions } from "@simplewebauthn/server";

const options = await generateRegistrationOptions({
  rpName: "Acme",
  rpID: "acme.com",                       // the origin these credentials will be bound to
  userName: user.email,
  userID: Buffer.from(user.id),
  attestationType: "none",                // don't ask for device provenance you won't use
  excludeCredentials: existing.map(c => ({ id: c.credentialID })),
  authenticatorSelection: {
    residentKey: "preferred",             // discoverable → usernameless login
    userVerification: "preferred",        // PIN/biometric → two factors in one gesture
  },
});

req.session.pendingChallenge = options.challenge;   // server-generated, single use
res.json(options);
```

The challenge has to come from the server, be unpredictable, and be usable exactly once. A client-supplied or reused challenge turns a signature into a replayable artifact, which removes the only thing the signature was proving.

### The browser half

```ts
import { startRegistration } from "@simplewebauthn/browser";

const attestation = await startRegistration({ optionsJSON: await fetchOptions() });
await fetch("/webauthn/register/verify", {
  method: "POST",
  body: JSON.stringify(attestation),
});
```

Three lines, and one of them is a `fetch`. The key generation, the user gesture, the biometric prompt, and the RP ID scoping all happen inside `startRegistration`, in the browser and the platform authenticator — which is the point. None of it is code you can get wrong.

### Verifying, and what you store

```ts
const { verified, registrationInfo } = await verifyRegistrationResponse({
  response: attestation,
  expectedChallenge: req.session.pendingChallenge,
  expectedOrigin: "https://acme.com",
  expectedRPID: "acme.com",
});

if (!verified) return res.status(400).json({ error: "registration failed" });

await db.authenticators.insert({
  userId: user.id,
  type: "webauthn",
  credentialID: registrationInfo.credential.id,
  publicKey: registrationInfo.credential.publicKey,   // ← public
  transports: attestation.response.transports,
  label: "MacBook Pro",
  createdAt: new Date(),
});
```

Stop on that column for a moment, because it's the whole argument in one line. The `authenticators` table now holds public keys next to TOTP secrets. The TOTP rows are live credentials that must be encrypted, access-controlled, and treated as a breach if they leak. The WebAuthn rows are, by construction, safe to print on a billboard. That asymmetry is not a matter of how carefully you store them — it's a property of the scheme.

### Authentication, and the line that does the work

```ts
const { verified, authenticationInfo } = await verifyAuthenticationResponse({
  response: assertion,
  expectedChallenge: req.session.pendingChallenge,
  expectedOrigin: "https://acme.com",   // ← this is the phishing resistance
  expectedRPID: "acme.com",
  credential: { id: stored.credentialID, publicKey: stored.publicKey },
});

if (!verified) return res.status(401).json({ error: "authentication failed" });

await startSession(res, stored.userId, { methods: ["webauthn"], verifiedAt: Date.now() });
```

`expectedOrigin` is enforced twice — once by the browser, which won't offer the credential to the wrong site, and once here, by you. The redundancy matters because the browser check protects a user with a modern browser and the server check protects against everything else.

There is one specific way to destroy it: deriving `expectedOrigin` from the incoming request's own `Host` or `Origin` header, which people do when they have several environments and want the code to work everywhere. That makes the expected origin whatever the caller says it is, which is to say, no check at all. Configure it per deployment, from configuration, never from the request.

And note the last line: authentication finishes by creating a session, recording *how* the user proved themselves and *when*. That's what step-up reads later, and it's the seam where this article hands back to the first one.

## Further Reading

- **W3C, _Web Authentication_ (WebAuthn).** The normative spec. Dense, but §5 and §7 — the API and the verification procedures — are worth reading once so you know what your library is checking on your behalf.
- **passkeys.dev** (W3C/FIDO community) and **webauthn.io**. The practical companions to the spec: working demos, platform support tables, and the UX patterns that have shaken out.
- **FIDO Alliance, passkey UX guidelines.** Most passkey rollouts fail on naming, placement, and recovery rather than on cryptography, and this is the accumulated evidence on all three.
- **RFC 6238 (TOTP) and RFC 4226 (HOTP).** Short, readable, and the source of the window-and-replay guidance that tutorials tend to omit.
- **NIST SP 800-63B, _Authentication and Lifecycle Management_.** Defines the assurance levels, says precisely what "phishing resistance" means, and is the document your enterprise customers' security teams are quoting at you. Also the source for the restricted status of SMS.
- **OWASP, _Authentication_ and _Multifactor Authentication_ cheat sheets.** Checklist form, current, and good for auditing an implementation you already have.
- **Have I Been Pwned's Pwned Passwords API.** The k-anonymity range query lets you check a password against billions of breached ones without sending it anywhere. This is the level-0 step, and the documentation is a five-minute read.
- **Published analyses of adversary-in-the-middle phishing kits** (Microsoft's threat intelligence write-ups are the most detailed public ones). Worth reading once to see the scenario from the second section built end to end — it's more routine and more automated than most engineers expect.

A **Java** sibling would reveal something this one can't. Recent Spring Security versions ship first-class WebAuthn support, and more interestingly the framework already has a place to put "how did this user authenticate and how recently" — the authentication object and its authorities — so step-up and MFA state become part of the security context that authorization decisions already read, rather than two columns you hand-rolled onto a session row. Seeing where a framework has pre-decided this, and what it costs to want something slightly different, is the other half of the lesson. Not yet written.

Three articles, one arc. The first asked where the truth about a live session lives. The second asked who asserts an identity and to whom. This one asked what the assertion is made of. They compose into a single question worth asking about any system you're handed: **what is the cheapest way for someone who isn't the user to end up holding a valid session?** Sometimes the answer is the password. Sometimes it's a relay proxy and a six-digit code. Very often — after all the work — it's the recovery email, which is why that path deserves the same scrutiny as the front door and almost never gets it.
