# Session Management

_Module 4.4 — Authentication & Authorization_

## The Problem

A customer emails at 08:40. Someone else is in their account.

You do the right things immediately. Force a password reset. Delete their session. Tell them it's handled.

Here is what is still alive at 08:45.

```
  session you deleted            ✗ gone
  attacker's session (Chrome,
    created 03:12 from Lagos)    ✓ ALIVE — you revoked the one you knew about
  mobile app refresh token       ✓ ALIVE — different credential, different table
  access token at the CDN edge   ✓ ALIVE for 11 more minutes
  "remember this device" cookie  ✓ ALIVE — and it skips MFA
  third-party OAuth grant the
    attacker connected           ✓ ALIVE — outlives every session you own
  personal access token they
    created at 03:20             ✓ ALIVE indefinitely
  Google session behind
    "Sign in with Google"        ✓ ALIVE — they click login, they're back in
```

Seven things survived, and the user has been told the problem is solved. None of this is exotic; every item is a normal feature of a normal product, added by a different person in a different quarter, each with its own lifetime and its own storage.

The uncomfortable observation is that **the session was never one thing.** It was a set of credentials descended from one authentication event, scattered across a database, a cache, a mobile keychain, an edge cache, and two external providers — and "log out" was only ever implemented against the one in the middle.

### And the opposite failure

So you tighten everything. Thirty-minute idle timeout. No remembered devices. Re-authentication on every sensitive action.

Support volume goes up, not down. Users get logged out mid-form and lose work. A 40-minute file upload dies at 30. The mobile app prompts for a password every morning, so people pick a shorter password. Two of your customers' teams now share one login because re-authenticating four people is worse than sharing one credential.

Both failures are the same design gap from opposite ends. The first article in this module asked *what a session credential is*. This one asks the two questions that turn out to be harder: **what does a session consist of, and how does it end?**

## What It Is

A **session** is the record of an authenticated relationship between one user and one device, and the set of credentials derived from it.

That definition is doing deliberate work. It's not "a token" — a single session may involve a chain of many tokens over its lifetime, as refresh rotation replaces one with another. It's not "a login event" — the login is a moment, the session is a duration. And it's not the same as a **grant**: when a user authorizes a third-party application (Module 4.1b), that authority is independent of any session and usually outlives all of them.

The practical consequence is that **the unit of revocation is the session family**, not the token. Everything descended from one authentication event should die together, and building that requires the descent to be recorded rather than implied.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Logout clears a cookie client-side and deletes nothing server-side.
- You cannot answer "how many devices is this account signed in from?"
- "Log out everywhere" doesn't exist — or exists and misses the mobile app.
- A password reset leaves existing sessions authenticated.
- An audit log entry says which user acted but not from which session, so you can't tell the attacker's actions from the victim's.
- Sessions never expire, or expire on a fixed schedule regardless of whether they're in use.
- Users report being logged out at random, in waves, correlated with nothing in your application logs.
- Your session store has no eviction policy and grows forever.
- A user with two tabs open gets logged out of one of them.
- Support needs to end a customer's session and the only tool is a database console.
- An enterprise customer deprovisioned an employee in their identity provider and that person still has a working session with you.
- Logging out and clicking "sign in with Google" logs the user straight back in without any prompt, and they report it as a broken logout button.

## How It Works

### The session record

Everything downstream depends on what you wrote down at login:

```
sessions
  id_hash          -- never the raw identifier (4.1a)
  user_id, tenant_id
  family_id        -- links a refresh chain into one session
  created_at, last_seen_at
  idle_expires_at, absolute_expires_at
  auth_methods     -- ["password","totp"] or ["webauthn"]  (4.1c)
  verified_at      -- last strong proof, for step-up
  device_label, user_agent, created_ip, last_ip
  revoked_at, revoked_reason
```

Two fields earn their place for reasons that aren't obvious. `family_id` is what makes "kill everything descended from this login" expressible as one query. `revoked_reason` is what lets you tell a user *why* they were signed out — "password changed," "you revoked this device," "we detected unusual activity" — and the absence of that field is why most products log people out silently and generate a support ticket.

### Storage strategies

The first article said to get sessions out of process memory. Where they land matters more than that framing suggested.

**Sticky sessions** — routing a user back to the instance holding their state — is the tempting shortcut and a bad trade. Deploys log everyone out, one instance's death takes its users with it, autoscaling redistributes traffic unevenly, and you've made a load balancer configuration into a correctness dependency.

**Redis** is the common answer: microsecond reads, native TTL, trivially shared. Three failure modes to configure around.

*Eviction.* If `maxmemory-policy` is `allkeys-lru`, Redis will discard sessions to make room for whatever else lives there — producing exactly the "random logouts, correlated with nothing" symptom above, worst during traffic peaks. Use `volatile-ttl` or `noeviction`, and strongly prefer a dedicated instance so cache pressure and session storage aren't competing for the same memory.

*Persistence.* Default configurations can lose recent writes on restart; no persistence at all means a restart signs out every user simultaneously. Decide deliberately whether a mass logout is acceptable, because the default decides for you.

*Availability.* It's now on the critical path of every authenticated request. Replication, failover, and a considered answer to what the application does during the failover window — which is usually "fail closed," which is usually an outage.

**A relational table** is slower per read and better at everything else: durable, transactional with the user changes that trigger revocation, and *queryable*, which is what makes "list this user's devices" and "revoke every session created before 03:00" one statement instead of a key scan. It needs an index on `user_id`, an index on the expiry column, and a cleanup job — expired rows don't remove themselves the way a Redis TTL does.

**Both** is where most mature systems land: the database is the record of truth, Redis is a read-through cache with a TTL shorter than the session. Revocation writes to the database and deletes the cache key; a cache miss falls back. The cost is a second place to get invalidation wrong, and the discipline is that nothing ever writes to the cache without writing to the database first.

For sizing: a session record is a few hundred bytes, so a million concurrent sessions is comfortably under a gigabyte. Session storage is almost never the expensive part. The write rate from `last_seen` updates is — which is why 4.1a throttled it.

### Expiry: two clocks, not one

**Idle expiry** ends a session that stops being used. **Absolute expiry** ends it regardless.

You need both, and the reason is specific: a sliding idle window alone never terminates. An attacker whose stolen session is used every few minutes keeps it indefinitely, and the session that renews itself forever is functionally a permanent credential. The absolute cap is the only thing that guarantees an ending.

Lifetimes belong to contexts, not to a global constant:

```
  surface           idle        absolute     re-verify for sensitive actions
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  consumer web      14 days     90 days      15 min
  mobile app        90 days     1 year       15 min
  admin console     30 min      12 hours     5 min
  support tooling   15 min      8 hours      every privileged action
```

Mobile expectations are genuinely different — people do not expect to log into their phone apps — and meeting that with a long-lived refresh token plus short access tokens is the standard shape. Admin surfaces are where short lifetimes cost little, because the population is small and the blast radius is large.

### Refresh, and the race nobody warns you about

Rotation with reuse detection was introduced in 4.1a: each refresh issues a new token and invalidates the old one; presenting an already-used token means two parties hold copies, so the whole family dies.

In production that rule collides with concurrency immediately.

```
  t=0     access token expires
  t=0.01  request A → 401 → refresh with token R
  t=0.02  request B → 401 → refresh with token R   ← same token, still in flight
  t=0.05  A succeeds, R is now consumed, R' issued
  t=0.06  B arrives with R → "reuse detected!" → family destroyed
          → user is logged out of everything for having two tabs open
```

Three mitigations, and you want at least two of them:

**A grace window on the server.** For a short period after consumption — 10 to 30 seconds — the previous token is still accepted and returns the *same* successor rather than minting another. Reuse detection then triggers only outside the window, which is where genuine theft looks different from a race.

**Single-flight on the client.** One refresh in progress at a time; concurrent callers await the same promise. This is the real fix, because it prevents the collision instead of tolerating it, and it lets the server's grace window stay short — which keeps reuse detection meaningful.

**Refresh early, not on failure.** Renewing at 80% of the token's life avoids a thundering herd of 401-triggered refreshes and tolerates clock skew between your servers and the client's device.

One more mobile-specific case: a device that's offline can't refresh. Expiring the session the moment a refresh fails means an aeroplane logs the user out. Distinguish "couldn't reach the server" from "the server said no."

### Invalidation: the matrix

This is the part that the opening incident was about. Write it down explicitly, because the failure mode is always partial coverage.

| event | sessions | refresh families | remembered devices | API tokens | third-party grants | IdP session |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| user logs out | this one | this family | keep | keep | keep | see below |
| log out everywhere | all | all | all | keep¹ | keep¹ | — |
| password changed | all but current | all but current | all | keep | keep | — |
| password reset (forgot) | all | all | all | keep | keep | — |
| MFA removed / recovery code used | all | all | all | keep | keep | — |
| user deprovisioned by admin | all | all | all | **revoke** | **revoke** | provider's |
| account deleted | all | all | all | revoke | revoke | — |
| suspicious activity | all | all | all | flag | flag | — |
| role or permission changed | keep² | keep | keep | keep | keep | — |

¹ Keep, but *say so in the UI*. A user clicking "sign out everywhere" after a compromise reasonably believes it covers everything; if it doesn't, the screen has to tell them where the rest lives.
² Sessions survive, but any cached permission set must not — that's Module 4.3's staleness problem, and it's a different cache from this one.

The mechanism differs per column, which is exactly why coverage ends up partial. Reference sessions are a delete. Self-contained tokens need short lifetimes or a denylist. Third-party grants need an outbound call to the provider's revocation endpoint. The identity provider's session needs an RP-initiated logout redirect, and propagating a logout the other way — the IdP terminating your session — needs back-channel logout, which is a webhook you have to implement and most products don't.

### One column that covers everything

There's a technique worth knowing because it collapses most of the fan-out:

```sql
ALTER TABLE users ADD COLUMN sessions_valid_after timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now();
```

Set it to `now()` and every session and token issued before this instant becomes invalid, without enumerating anything. Each verification compares the credential's issue time against it. One write, global effect, works identically for database sessions and for self-contained tokens whose `iat` you can read.

It's not a complete answer — it doesn't reach an access token already sitting in an edge cache with no store access, and it can't express "all except the current one" without a second condition. But as the backstop behind "we must be able to make everything stop, right now," it's a column and a comparison.

### Fixation, and rotating on privilege change

If an attacker can cause a victim's browser to hold a session identifier *before* login — through a subdomain that can write cookies, a URL parameter your framework accepts, or an XSS — then that identifier becomes an authenticated one the moment the victim signs in, and the attacker holds a copy.

The fix is mechanical: **issue a new session identifier whenever the privilege level changes.** At login, at step-up verification, at the start of impersonation, and at the end of it. Most frameworks do the login case for you; the others are yours.

Resist the related temptation to bind sessions hard to an IP address. Mobile users change networks constantly, carrier-grade NAT reassigns addresses mid-session, and corporate egress pools rotate. Binding to IP produces a steady stream of logouts for legitimate users and stops an attacker who is behind the same NAT as the victim not at all. Treat an IP or user-agent change as a *signal* worth logging, notifying on, or challenging — not as grounds for termination.

### Device management as a product feature

All that metadata has a user-facing payoff, and it's the cheapest security feature you will ever ship:

- a list of active sessions, with a readable label ("Chrome on macOS"), an approximate location, and a last-active time
- the current session marked as such, so revoking the others is unambiguous
- a revoke button per device, and one for everything else
- an email when a new device signs in

That last item is worth more than the list. It turns every user into a detector for their own account, which is the only monitoring that scales to the case where the credentials were valid and the login looked normal. The opening incident was reported by a customer; the goal is for that report to arrive within minutes rather than hours.

Two cautions. Approximate location from IP is frequently wrong, and a user seeing a login "from Frankfurt" when they're in Munich generates alarm and a support ticket — label it as approximate. And revoking *other* sessions should require step-up verification (4.1c), because otherwise an attacker who holds one session can use this feature to lock out the legitimate owner.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A server-side session record with two clocks and a real logout**: any product with accounts. This is the floor, not a feature.
- **Device management UI and new-device notifications**: consumer products, and anything where the user is the party best positioned to notice a compromise. Which is most things.
- **Short absolute lifetimes with frequent re-verification**: admin consoles, support tooling, anything with impersonation, anything that moves money.
- **The global invalidation timestamp**: any system that issues self-contained tokens, because it's the only cheap way to make "stop everything" mean everything.
- **Refresh rotation with reuse detection**: whenever a long-lived credential exists on a device you don't control — which is every mobile app.
- **Back-channel logout and deprovisioning webhooks**: B2B products sold to organizations that will ask, in a security questionnaire, what happens when they terminate an employee.
- **Per-session identifiers in the audit log**: compliance-facing products, and any product where post-incident analysis needs to separate the attacker's actions from the owner's.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

**A device management screen for an internal tool with eleven users.** The support channel is a desk.

**Sub-hour idle timeouts on a product people use all day.** This is the case where the security measure reliably loses: people share logins, disable the screen lock, or keep a script clicking. Friction that exceeds the perceived stakes gets defeated by the user.

**Hard IP or user-agent binding.** Discussed above. It generates constant false positives and stops almost nothing.

**A dedicated session service** when a table and a cache do the job. Sessions are one of the least interesting distributed systems problems you own; don't promote it.

**Cross-region session replication** for users who don't cross regions. The consistency problems are real and the benefit is hypothetical.

**Revoking third-party grants on ordinary logout.** The user will be surprised and annoyed when their calendar integration stops syncing because they signed out on a laptop.

## Trade-offs

- **The friction/security tension is not resolvable, only placed.** Every shortening of a lifetime buys revocation coverage and spends user goodwill, and the exchange rate differs per surface. The mistake is picking one global number.
- **Every invalidation rule is a support case.** "Why was I logged out?" has to have an answer in the product, or it has one in your inbox.
- **Storage is cheap; write amplification isn't.** `last_seen` at full request volume turns a read-mostly store into a write-heavy one (4.1a), and expired rows in a relational store need a cleanup job that someone has to own.
- **The session store's availability is your availability** — noted in 4.1a, and worth restating only because the hybrid cache-plus-database arrangement makes it easy to believe you've solved it when you've actually created two places to be inconsistent.
- **Revocation doesn't propagate to systems you don't control.** Your kill switch stops at the boundary of your own verification. The CDN, the identity provider, and every third-party grant have their own lifetimes, and the matrix above is partly a list of things you must remember to ask someone else to do.
- **Device management is an information surface for an attacker who already has a session** — they can see the owner's other devices and, without step-up, revoke them.
- **Session records are personal data.** IP addresses and device fingerprints, retained indefinitely, are a GDPR question (Module 34) and a retention policy you now need. The security value of long-lived session history and the compliance value of deleting it point in opposite directions.

## Alternatives

- **Delegate lifetime to the identity provider.** Keep sessions short and re-authenticate silently against the IdP, which already holds a long-lived session and already applies the organization's policy. Fewer lifetimes for you to manage, and revocation lands in one place — at the cost of a redirect on the critical path and a hard dependency on the provider's availability.
- **Cheap re-authentication instead of long sessions.** Passkeys change this calculation meaningfully: when signing in again is one tap rather than a password and a six-digit code, short sessions stop being a usability disaster. A product that is fully on WebAuthn can afford lifetimes that would be intolerable with passwords, which is an argument for 4.1c that doesn't appear in 4.1c.
- **Capability tokens instead of sessions** for narrow, short-lived operations — a download link, a one-time upload, a webhook callback. No session to manage, and nothing to revoke because it expires first.
- **Device certificates or mTLS** for managed fleets. The device's identity is established at the transport layer and revoked centrally through the certificate authority, which is a much more reliable kill switch than anything above — and only available when someone else manages the devices.
- **Continuous access evaluation.** The emerging standard answer to "revocation doesn't propagate": identity providers push signals — session revoked, credential changed, device compromised — to relying parties as events, so a termination in one system reaches the others in seconds instead of at the next token expiry. The OpenID Foundation's Shared Signals work is where this lives. Worth watching, and worth asking your provider about.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — a real session record with a real logout.** Server-side, both clocks, and a logout that deletes rather than forgets. Covered in 4.1a; if it isn't done, nothing below matters.
*Signal:* logout is client-side only.

**Level 1 — one revocation function.** Write the matrix above down as a table in your repository, then implement exactly one `revokeSessions(userId, reason, options)` that every event path calls. The failure being prevented is six code paths each implementing a different subset.
*Signal:* password reset and "log out everywhere" are two different pieces of code that do similar things.

**Level 2 — device list and new-device email.** The user-facing payoff, and your cheapest detection mechanism.
*Signal:* the first "was this you?" support thread, which you learned about by email rather than by alert.

**Level 3 — harden refresh.** Rotation with reuse detection, a short server-side grace window, and single-flight on every client. Ship the client fix with the server rule, not after it.
*Signal:* users being logged out in pairs, or complaints that correlate with having two tabs open.

**Level 4 — global invalidation and short token lifetimes.** The `sessions_valid_after` column, access tokens short enough that the revocation gap is acceptable, and a documented number for what that gap actually is.
*Signal:* you added a second service that verifies tokens without touching your store.

**Level 5 — cross-system termination.** Deprovisioning driven by SCIM or provider webhooks, OIDC back-channel logout implemented rather than acknowledged, and third-party grants revoked on account deletion.
*Signal:* an enterprise security questionnaire, or the first time a customer asks how long a fired employee keeps access.

## Implementation

TypeScript, because the refresh race needs both halves — the server's grace window and the client's single-flight — and they only make sense as one mechanism.

### One choke point for revocation

```ts
type RevokeReason =
  | "user_logout" | "logout_all" | "password_changed"
  | "mfa_changed" | "admin_deprovisioned" | "suspicious_activity";

interface RevokeOptions {
  exceptSessionId?: string;   // "all but this one"
  includeApiTokens?: boolean;
  includeGrants?: boolean;
}

export async function revokeSessions(
  userId: string, reason: RevokeReason, opts: RevokeOptions = {},
) {
  const sessions = await db.sessions.findActive(userId, { except: opts.exceptSessionId });

  await db.transaction(async (tx) => {
    await tx.sessions.markRevoked(sessions.map(s => s.id), reason);
    await tx.refreshFamilies.revokeByFamilyIds(sessions.map(s => s.familyId), reason);
    await tx.rememberedDevices.deleteForUser(userId);

    if (opts.includeApiTokens) await tx.apiTokens.revokeForUser(userId, reason);
    if (opts.includeGrants)    await tx.oauthGrants.markForRevocation(userId);
  });

  // Cache after the durable write, never before.
  await redis.del(...sessions.map(s => `sess:${s.idHash}`));

  if (opts.includeGrants) await enqueueProviderRevocations(userId);  // outbound, retryable
  await notifyUser(userId, reason, sessions.length);
}
```

The ordering is the interesting part. The database write commits first and the cache delete follows, because the reverse order has a window in which a concurrent request repopulates the cache from a row that hasn't been revoked yet. And the outbound revocation calls to third-party providers are queued rather than awaited: they involve networks you don't control, they can fail, and a provider being slow must not make your logout endpoint hang.

`notifyUser` isn't a nicety. It's the difference between a user who knows their sessions ended and why, and a support ticket.

### Refresh: rotation with a grace window

```ts
const GRACE_MS = 15_000;

export async function refresh(presented: string) {
  const row = await db.refreshTokens.findByHash(sha256(presented));
  if (!row) throw new AuthError("unknown_token");

  if (row.consumedAt) {
    // Consumed recently → almost certainly a concurrent request from the same client.
    if (Date.now() - row.consumedAt.getTime() < GRACE_MS && row.successorId) {
      return db.refreshTokens.materialize(row.successorId);   // same successor, not a new one
    }
    // Consumed long ago → two parties hold this token. Assume theft.
    await revokeSessions(row.userId, "suspicious_activity");
    throw new AuthError("token_reuse_detected");
  }

  const next = await db.refreshTokens.rotate(row);   // marks consumed, links successor
  return next;
}
```

The grace window is a deliberate weakening of reuse detection, and it's worth being precise about what it costs: for fifteen seconds after a legitimate refresh, a stolen copy of that same token would be honoured. That's a narrow window against an attacker who must already hold the token, and it's the price of not logging people out for opening a second tab. Keep it short, and keep it short by fixing the client.

Returning the *same* successor matters too. Minting a new token for the second caller would leave two live tokens in one family, which is the state the whole scheme exists to prevent.

### Single-flight on the client

```ts
let inFlight: Promise<Tokens> | null = null;

async function getFreshTokens(): Promise<Tokens> {
  if (tokens.expiresAt - Date.now() > 60_000) return tokens;   // renew early, not on 401
  if (inFlight) return inFlight;                                // ← the whole fix

  inFlight = refreshRequest(tokens.refresh)
    .then(t => { tokens = t; return t; })
    .finally(() => { inFlight = null; });

  return inFlight;
}
```

Six meaningful lines, and they're what let the server's grace window stay at fifteen seconds instead of the length of your slowest parallel request. Note that this only covers one JavaScript context — a second browser tab has its own module instance and its own `inFlight`, which is exactly the case the server's grace window is there to catch. The two mechanisms are complementary, and shipping only one of them is how this bug survives into production.

### The global cutoff

```ts
export function isStillValid(session: Session, user: User): boolean {
  if (session.revokedAt) return false;
  if (session.createdAt < user.sessionsValidAfter) return false;   // covers everything at once
  if (Date.now() > session.absoluteExpiresAt.getTime()) return false;
  if (Date.now() > session.idleExpiresAt.getTime()) return false;
  return true;
}
```

Four conditions, and the second is the one that requires no enumeration: set `sessionsValidAfter` to now and every credential issued before this instant fails, including ones you never listed and tokens verified by services that can't reach your session table — provided they read the same user record, or the claim is in the token they verify. It is the cheapest complete kill switch available, and it belongs in the check from the beginning rather than being retrofitted during an incident.

## Further Reading

- **OWASP, _Session Management Cheat Sheet_.** The sections on fixation, identifier rotation on privilege change, and logout behaviour are the most useful part, and the most commonly skipped.
- **OpenID Connect RP-Initiated Logout, Front-Channel Logout, and Back-Channel Logout.** Three separate specifications for three different directions of logout propagation. Read them together; the reason there are three is instructive about what's actually hard.
- **OpenID Foundation, Shared Signals Framework and CAEP.** The standardized answer to "revocation doesn't propagate": continuous event-driven communication of session and credential changes between identity providers and relying parties.
- **RFC 7009, _OAuth 2.0 Token Revocation_.** The endpoint you call to make a third-party grant actually stop, and the reason the last column of the matrix is an outbound HTTP request rather than a delete.
- **RFC 7644, _SCIM Protocol_.** How enterprise deprovisioning reaches you at all. The `active: false` update is the event that has to trigger your revocation function.
- **Redis documentation on key eviction (`maxmemory-policy`).** Two pages, and they explain the "random logouts under load" symptom completely. Worth reading before choosing Redis as a session store rather than after.
- **Vendor documentation on refresh token rotation and reuse detection** — Auth0's and Okta's are the most detailed public write-ups, including the grace-window behaviour, which is otherwise folklore passed between implementations.

A **Go** sibling would add something real here, on the distributed side. When many services verify tokens independently, revocation becomes a propagation problem: a short-TTL local cache of the revocation list per instance, refreshed in the background, with `singleflight` collapsing the stampede when it expires — and the honest arithmetic of how stale each instance is allowed to be. Go's concurrency primitives make that legible in a way a single-process example can't, and the pattern generalizes well past sessions. Not yet written.

Six articles, and they compose in one direction: identity is **carried** (4.1a), **asserted** by someone (4.1b), **proven** by some factor (4.1c), **stored** as a secret you must assume will leak (4.2), **interpreted** into what it permits (4.3), and finally **ended** (4.4).

The habit worth taking into the next module is the one this article kept running into: **design the ending first.** Almost every authentication system is designed forward from the login screen, and almost every failure in it lives in the parts nobody designed backward from "make this stop, right now, everywhere." The question that surfaces those parts is unglamorous and it takes about ten minutes: *if I learned in the next hour that this account was compromised, what exactly would still work?* Write the list. The items you can't cross off are your actual security posture.
