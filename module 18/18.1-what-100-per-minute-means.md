# What "100 Requests per Minute" Actually Means

_Module 18.1 — API Rate Limiting & Throttling_

## The Problem

A shipping-rates API. Around 1,400 online stores call `POST /rates` during checkout to quote shipping for a cart. The fleet handles about 2,500 requests/second at a p99 under 250ms. Normal peak is 1,100.

Tuesday, 02:10. A developer at one of those stores ships a catalog-sync script. It quotes rates for every SKU against every destination zone, and its retry loop has no delay. It settles at 1,600 requests/second, from one API key.

```
                        capacity: 2,500 req/s
                                       │
normal peak   ███████████              │            1,100
02:10         ███████████▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓│▓▓          2,700
                         └─ one key ─────┘ = 1,600 req/s
```

p99 goes from 180ms to six seconds. Checkouts time out at 1,399 stores that did nothing wrong. The on-call engineer finds the key by grepping access logs and revokes it by hand, forty minutes in.

Without a limit, shared capacity is allocated to whoever asks fastest. Your service is fair to requests, not to callers, and the heaviest caller wins.

The fix everyone reaches for is a sentence: **"100 requests per minute per API key."** It sounds like a specification. It isn't one, and three customers show why in the following week.

**The flash sale.** A store sends a promotional email. Within a few seconds, 80 checkout quotes arrive, and then nothing for twenty minutes. Its hourly average is far below any limit you'd set. Whether those 80 requests succeed depends entirely on how "per minute" is enforced. Count per calendar minute, and all 80 are fine. Enforce the same rate as "no more than one every 0.6 seconds," and 79 of the 80 are rejected. Same number, opposite outcome, and the rejected ones are paying customers at checkout.

**The boundary.** You implement it as a counter that resets at the top of every minute. An integration notices the reset and learns to send its batch just before and just after it:

```
            minute 12:00                        minute 12:01
  ├────────────────────────────────────┤├────────────────────────────────────┤
                                  ████ ││ ████
                                   100 ││ 100
                                       ▲
               200 requests in 0.2 seconds — each minute is "under the limit"
```

**The herd.** Thirty integrations run on cron at `:00`. Every one is within its limit, and every one's counter resets at the same instant. At the top of each minute, 3,000 requests arrive in a single second, into a fleet sized for 2,500.

Across all three, "100 per minute" said how much in total. It never said **how bursty** that total may be, or **which minute** it's counted over. Those two unstated properties decide who gets rejected, and the algorithm you pick is what answers them.

## What It Is

This is **rate limiting**. Each request is attributed to a **key** (an API key, a user, an IP), measured against a **budget**, and then either admitted, rejected, or delayed.

The algorithms that do the measuring have names: **fixed window**, **sliding window log**, **sliding window counter**, **token bucket**, **leaky bucket**, and **GCRA**. They differ in how much burst they permit, how much state they keep per key, and how exactly they honor the number you configured.

Two terms get used loosely enough to need pinning down. **Policing** rejects excess requests immediately, usually with `429 Too Many Requests`. **Shaping** delays excess requests so they leave at a steady rate. Industry usage is inconsistent: some writers use "rate limiting" for policing and "throttling" for shaping, while AWS API Gateway calls its rejection behavior "throttling." In this module, *rate limiting* is the umbrella term, and policing versus shaping is the distinction that matters.

Several neighbors look similar and do different jobs: quotas, concurrency limits, load shedding, backpressure, circuit breakers. They're grouped in *Alternatives*.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A small number of keys or IPs account for a double-digit share of total traffic.
- Latency degrades for every customer while one customer runs a batch job.
- You already have a limit, but request-rate graphs spike to twice that limit right at minute or hour boundaries.
- Load has a sawtooth pattern aligned to `:00`.
- Customers report 429s while their per-minute totals are nowhere near the limit. This is burst intolerance: the effective window is shorter than the one you advertised.
- The limiter's store is growing faster than the number of keys. Typically this is sorted sets of timestamps, which means a sliding log at a high limit.
- A customer asks "how many requests can I make right now?" and your system can't give a precise answer.
- A downstream system (a database, a carrier API) fails under bursts even though the per-minute totals you admit are within its capacity. You're policing when that downstream needs shaping.
- Bills from a metered dependency (SMS, LLM, geocoding) jump without any matching growth in users.

## How It Works

Every rate limiter makes four decisions:

```
request ──► whose is it? ──► how much have they used? ──► admit / reject / delay ──► tell them
             (key)            (algorithm + state)          (policy)                 (response)
```

This article covers the middle two. Choosing keys and setting per-plan budgets is 18.2b. Keeping the state correct across many instances is 18.2a. Telling the client (`429`, `Retry-After`, rate-limit headers) is 18.3.

Everything below runs in one process on purpose, so that each algorithm's behavior is visible before distribution complicates it.

### Fixed window counter

Keep one counter per key per window, identified by the window's start time (`key:12:01`). Increment it on each request and reject once it passes the limit. Discard it when the window ends.

It's the cheapest option: one integer per key, one increment per request. The reset is also easy to explain ("your limit resets at the top of the hour"), which is why long-period quotas are usually fixed windows.

It has two costs, both from the problem above. A caller can put up to **twice the limit** into a short span straddling a boundary. And because every key resets at the same instant, clients that wait for the reset all arrive together. You can reduce the herd by offsetting each key's window by a hash of the key, but the boundary burst remains.

### Sliding window log

Store the timestamp of every admitted request. On each new request, drop timestamps older than the window, count what's left, and admit if the count is under the limit.

This is **exact**: in any trailing 60 seconds, there are never more than 100 admitted requests. The cost is state proportional to the limit. A key allowed 10,000 requests an hour holds up to 10,000 timestamps, and a million such keys is a real memory bill. It suits low limits where precision matters, such as 3 OTP messages per phone number per hour. There the state is tiny, and an attacker who got a fourth or fifth message would be costing you money.

### Sliding window counter

Keep only two fixed-window counters, the previous window and the current one, and estimate the trailing window by weighting the previous count by how much of it still overlaps:

```
             |──────── 12:00 ────────|──────── 12:01 ────────|
                                           ▲ now = 12:01:15  (25% into current)
trailing 60s:        [──────────────────────]

estimate = prev × (1 − 0.25) + curr  =  84 × 0.75 + 20  =  83
```

This uses two integers per key and removes the boundary burst. Just after a reset, the previous window's weight is close to 1, so a caller who used their budget at 12:00:59 has almost nothing available at 12:01:00.

It's an approximation because it assumes the previous window's requests were spread evenly. A caller whose previous requests were all at the very end gets a slightly generous estimate. Under adversarial timing, a trailing window can admit more than the limit, but not as a concentrated double burst, because the allowance only returns gradually. Cloudflare measured this approach on hundreds of millions of real requests and found the error to be a tiny fraction of a percent. For most API limits, that level of imprecision doesn't matter to your capacity.

### Token bucket

A different model, and the one most public APIs use (Stripe and AWS API Gateway both document it). Each key has a bucket with a **capacity B**. Tokens refill at a **rate r** per second, and the count never exceeds B. Each request spends a token, or several if it's expensive. If there aren't enough tokens, the request is rejected.

No background timer refills anything. The bucket stores two numbers, tokens and last-seen time, and on each request it credits `elapsed × r` before deciding:

```
capacity B = 50, refill r = 10/s

t = 0.0s   50 tokens   ── 50 requests ──►  50 served             →  0 left
t = 0.5s    5 tokens   ── 20 requests ──►   5 served, 15 rejected →  0 left
t = 8.0s   50 tokens   (7.5s × 10 = 75 earned, capped at B — idle time doesn't bank beyond it)
```

The important property is that a token bucket has **two parameters because a limit has two dimensions**. B is the permitted burst and r is the sustained rate. That's exactly what the flash-sale store needed: B = 100 absorbs its 80-request spike, and r keeps the runaway script to a trickle once its initial burst is spent.

There's a consequence that surprises people. The most a token bucket admits in any interval of length T is **B + r·T**. A bucket described as "100 per minute" with B = 100 and r = 100/60 per second can admit 200 in one minute: the full bucket at the start, then another 100 refilled over the next sixty seconds. That's correct behavior, not a bug. If you promised customers "never more than 100 in any minute," a token bucket is the wrong definition, or B needs to be much smaller.

### Leaky bucket: two different things with one name

**As a queue**, which is the original meaning: requests enter a bounded queue, a worker takes them out at a constant rate, and anything arriving at a full queue is dropped. This is **shaping**. Bursts go in, and a smooth stream comes out.

```
bursty arrivals          bounded queue            constant outflow
 ███  █  ████   ──►   [ ■ ■ ■ ■ ■ □ □ ]   ──►    ■ ■ ■ ■ ■ ■ ■ ■
                              │ full
                              ▼
                           reject
```

The downstream sees a steady rate, which is valuable when it can't handle spikes. The caller pays in latency, and a queued request holds a connection while it waits. NGINX's `limit_req` is a leaky bucket of this kind. Its `burst` setting sizes the queue, and its `nodelay` option serves queued requests immediately while still consuming their slots at the configured rate. That option turns shaping back into policing.

**As a meter**: a counter that "leaks" at rate r and fills with each request, rejecting when full. This is mathematically the mirror image of a token bucket, measuring used capacity instead of remaining capacity. Shopify's REST Admin API describes its limit as a leaky bucket and returns 429 when the bucket is full. That's meter semantics, not a queue. When someone says "leaky bucket," ask which one they mean.

### GCRA: token-bucket behavior, one number

The **Generic Cell Rate Algorithm** comes from ATM networking and gives token-bucket semantics while storing a single timestamp per key. That timestamp is the **theoretical arrival time (TAT)**: the moment by which this caller's usage would be paid off if they had sent at exactly the steady rate.

```
emission interval T = 1/r        tolerance τ = T × B

now ──────────┬───────────────────── TAT ────►
              └── is TAT within τ of now?  admit, TAT += T
                                           otherwise reject; retry in (TAT − τ − now)
```

A caller may run ahead of schedule by up to τ, which is the burst. Each request pushes the schedule forward by one interval. It's one value, one comparison, and the key can expire at TAT. Libraries such as redis-cell and Go's `throttled` use it. When state per key and operations per request matter, GCRA gives you a token bucket more cheaply.

### Weighted requests

Every algorithm above works with a **cost** instead of a count. An export endpoint costs 10 tokens and a lookup costs 1. The GraphQL version, where cost is computed from the query, was Module 8. The mechanism doesn't change, only the unit.

### Side by side

| | State per key | Burst behavior | Accuracy | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed window | 1 counter | Up to 2× at boundaries; herds at reset | Exact per window | Login counters, daily/monthly quotas |
| Sliding log | 1 timestamp per admitted request | None beyond the limit | Exact | Low limits, high stakes (OTP sends) |
| Sliding counter | 2 counters | Smoothed | Approximate | Per-key API limits across huge key counts |
| Token bucket | Tokens + timestamp | Up to B, then r | Exact for its (B, r) | General API limits |
| Leaky bucket (queue) | A queue | Absorbed, output smoothed | Exact | Shaping toward a fragile downstream |
| GCRA | 1 timestamp | Same as token bucket | Same as token bucket | Token-bucket semantics, minimal state |

## When It's Appropriate

Rate limiting as a whole pays for itself when capacity is shared and callers are identifiable: a public or partner API, a multi-tenant product, authentication endpoints, and anything that costs you money per call. Choosing between algorithms is a narrower question:

- **Fixed window**: "5 failed logins per account per 15 minutes," or "10,000 API calls per day, resetting at midnight UTC." A boundary burst of 10 login attempts is harmless, and a reset customers can predict is a feature.
- **Sliding window log**: "3 SMS verification codes per phone number per hour." At three timestamps per key, exactness costs nothing, and every extra message is SMS spend and a toll-fraud opportunity.
- **Sliding window counter**: a public API with two million active keys at 600/minute each, where you want real per-minute semantics without the boundary burst and can't afford 600 timestamps per key.
- **Token bucket**: a dashboard whose page load fans out into 30 API calls, followed by quiet. Sustained 10/s, burst 50. This is the flash-sale case, and the right default for most HTTP APIs with human-driven clients.
- **Leaky bucket as a queue**: your workers calling a carrier API that allows 20 requests/second and suspends accounts that exceed it (Module 17). Also appropriate inbound when clients are machines that tolerate latency better than errors.
- **GCRA**: token-bucket policy at a scale where one value per key, instead of two, meaningfully reduces memory or round trips.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Agonizing over the algorithm before you have any limit at all.** A fixed window at the gateway this week beats a carefully tuned sliding counter next quarter. Every algorithm here would have stopped the 02:10 incident.
- **A sliding log at a high limit.** 10,000 timestamps per key to distinguish 10,000 from 10,040 is precision nobody benefits from.
- **Exactness that capacity doesn't care about.** The difference between 100 and 103 per minute is noise. The difference between 100 spread out and 100 in one second can matter. Choose the algorithm for burst shape, not decimal precision.
- **Shaping interactive requests.** Holding a shopper's checkout request in a queue for eight seconds is worse than a fast 429 the client can handle. Queue machines, not people.
- **Using per-caller limits as your overload defense.** Two thousand well-behaved keys, each under its limit, can still exceed your capacity together. Per-caller limits provide fairness. Protecting the service's own health is load shedding.
- **Limiting a single internal caller.** Between two of your own services, a rate limit mostly turns load into errors that the caller then retries. A concurrency limit or backpressure (9.1b) usually communicates the constraint better.

## Trade-offs

- **The algorithm is a product decision.** It determines which legitimate traffic patterns work. Choose wrong and the bug appears in a customer's checkout, not in your logs.
- **It becomes part of your API contract.** Clients tune themselves to your semantics. Switching from fixed window to token bucket changes which clients succeed, even when the configured number stays the same.
- **State scales with keys, and keys can be attacker-controlled.** Keying by IP means anyone with a botnet can create keys. Every algorithm needs an eviction story, and the sliding log needs a large one.
- **Approximations and explanations conflict.** A sliding counter can reject a customer whose own count reads 97, and a token bucket's "37.4 tokens remaining" is hard for people to reason about. Support will receive these questions.
- **Everything depends on a clock.** A clock that steps backwards can drain or overfill buckets. Clocks that disagree across machines become a correctness problem in 18.2a.
- **Retry hints are estimates.** A computed "retry after" is a lower bound under some algorithms. A client that retries at exactly that moment can be rejected again.
- **Shaping holds resources.** Every delayed request holds a connection, a socket, and sometimes a worker. A queue that's too deep turns a rate problem into a memory problem.
- **Rejections aren't free.** A limiter that does meaningful work before rejecting still spends resources on abusive traffic. Reject before anything expensive, such as the password hash in Module 4 or the database query here.
- **Per-key limits don't stop distributed abuse.** Ten thousand IPs making one request each stay under every per-key limit. That needs global baselines and bot defenses, not a better bucket.

## Alternatives

These are neighbors that solve adjacent problems. Most production systems use several together.

- **Quotas.** Long-horizon budgets (50,000 calls per month) tied to billing. They're counted from usage records, often checked asynchronously, and reset on a calendar. They answer "what did you pay for?", not "are you hurting everyone right now?" See Module 15.
- **Concurrency limits.** Cap requests in flight rather than requests per second. They correct themselves when a dependency slows down, which a rate limit doesn't (Module 10). They're the better tool for endpoints with highly variable duration.
- **Load shedding.** Reject based on the server's own health, such as queue depth, CPU, or latency, regardless of who is calling. The usual response is `503`. This is the aggregate defense that per-caller limits aren't.
- **Backpressure** (9.1b). Inside a pipeline you control, signal upstream to slow down instead of rejecting.
- **Circuit breakers** (36.3). Protection on the caller's side against a failing dependency. A breaker is about the dependency's health, not about fairness.
- **Caching and autoscaling.** Make repeated requests cheap, or add capacity. Both reduce how often limits matter, and neither addresses a caller who is actually misbehaving.
- **Accept and queue.** Return `202 Accepted` and process asynchronously (Module 10). The caller's burst becomes your backlog, on your schedule.
- **WAF and bot management.** For abuse spread across many sources, where no single key stands out.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — measure per-key usage at two resolutions.** For each key, record the p99 requests per second and the p99 requests per minute. The first number sets B and the second sets r. Without both, you're guessing at half the policy.
_Signal you're here:_ a proposed limit with a single number in it.

**Level 1 — a fixed window at the edge or gateway on the obvious targets.** Set a per-IP cap on login and password reset, plus a generous per-key ceiling that only stops runaway scripts. This is configuration, not code.
_Signal you need it:_ your first noisy-neighbor incident, or the first credential-stuffing run.

**Level 2 — per-key token buckets in application middleware, in shadow mode first.** Log every request that *would* be rejected, for a week, and check those log entries against customers you know are legitimate. Then enforce.
_Signal you need it:_ limits that need to vary by customer, bursty legitimate traffic, or boundary spikes on your graphs.

**Level 3 — shared state across instances.** With N instances and in-memory buckets, your real limit is somewhere between 1× and N× what you configured. That's 18.2a.
_Signal you need it:_ a second instance, or an autoscaler.

**Level 4 — several limits per request.** A per-key burst limit, a per-tenant sustained limit, a per-endpoint cost, and budgets set by plan (18.2b). Clear rate-limit responses to go with them (18.3).
_Signal you need it:_ one tenant spreading load across fifty keys, or an expensive endpoint that consumes a budget designed for cheap ones.

**Level 5 — specialized mechanisms where the pressure is specific.** Shaping toward downstreams that can't take bursts, GCRA where state is expensive, and load shedding alongside all of it.
_Signal you need it:_ a downstream failing on bursts you admitted legitimately, or limiter state as a line item on your infrastructure bill.

## Implementation

**TypeScript, in memory, in one process.** This keeps each algorithm's decision logic visible before 18.2a moves the state into Redis and has to make it atomic. Each limiter takes `now` as a parameter, so it's deterministic in tests. In production, pass a monotonic clock such as `performance.now()`, not `Date.now()`.

```ts
interface Decision { allowed: boolean; retryAfterMs: number }
interface Limiter  { take(key: string, now: number, cost?: number): Decision }
```

**Sliding window counter.** Delete the `prev` term and this becomes a fixed window, which is the clearest way to see what the extra counter buys you.

```ts
class SlidingWindowCounter implements Limiter {
  private state = new Map<string, { start: number; curr: number; prev: number }>();
  constructor(private limit: number, private windowMs: number) {}

  take(key: string, now: number, cost = 1): Decision {
    const start = now - (now % this.windowMs);
    let w = this.state.get(key) ?? { start, curr: 0, prev: 0 };

    if (w.start !== start) {
      // Carry the old count forward only if it was the immediately preceding window.
      const adjacent = start - w.start === this.windowMs;
      w = { start, curr: 0, prev: adjacent ? w.curr : 0 };
    }

    const elapsed  = (now - start) / this.windowMs;          // 0 … 1
    const estimate = w.prev * (1 - elapsed) + w.curr;        // fixed window: just w.curr
    this.state.set(key, w);

    if (estimate + cost > this.limit) {
      // Solve for when the previous window's weight has decayed enough.
      // If the current window alone is over budget, point at its end: a lower bound, not a promise.
      const need = w.prev > 0 ? 1 - (this.limit - w.curr - cost) / w.prev : 1;
      return { allowed: false, retryAfterMs: Math.max(0, Math.min(need, 1) * this.windowMs - (now - start)) };
    }
    w.curr += cost;
    return { allowed: true, retryAfterMs: 0 };
  }
}
```

> **Aside — this `Map` is a memory leak with an attacker-controlled key.** Every new IP adds an entry that never leaves. Real code needs a TTL sweep or an LRU bound. A bounded LRU has its own failure mode: under a flood of fresh keys, it evicts the heavy callers you most wanted to track.

**Token bucket.** The whole algorithm is the refill line.

```ts
class TokenBucket implements Limiter {
  private state = new Map<string, { tokens: number; ts: number }>();
  constructor(private capacity: number, private refillPerSec: number) {}

  take(key: string, now: number, cost = 1): Decision {
    const b = this.state.get(key) ?? { tokens: this.capacity, ts: now };   // new callers start full

    // Lazy refill: no timers, just credit the time since we last looked.
    b.tokens = Math.min(this.capacity, b.tokens + ((now - b.ts) / 1000) * this.refillPerSec);
    b.ts = now;
    this.state.set(key, b);

    if (b.tokens < cost) {
      return { allowed: false, retryAfterMs: ((cost - b.tokens) / this.refillPerSec) * 1000 };
    }
    b.tokens -= cost;
    return { allowed: true, retryAfterMs: 0 };
  }
}
```

> **Aside — two quiet failure modes.** If a request's `cost` is greater than `capacity`, it can never succeed, yet `retryAfterMs` still reports a finite wait. Validate costs against capacity when you load configuration. And if `now` ever goes backwards, which `Date.now()` can do when NTP steps the clock, `now - b.ts` goes negative and the bucket drains. That's the reason for a monotonic clock here, and the reason 18.2a has to choose a single clock for the whole fleet.

> **Aside — the race that isn't here yet.** Node runs this read-refill-decrement sequence without interruption, so there's no race within one process. Once the state lives in a shared store, the same three steps become *read, compute, write* across a network. Two instances can both read "1 token" and both admit. That's the core problem of 18.2a.

**GCRA.** The same policy as the token bucket, stored as one number.

```ts
class Gcra implements Limiter {
  private tat = new Map<string, number>();      // theoretical arrival time per key
  private readonly interval: number;            // ms per request at the steady rate
  private readonly tolerance: number;           // how far ahead of schedule a caller may run

  constructor(ratePerSec: number, burst: number) {
    this.interval  = 1000 / ratePerSec;
    this.tolerance = this.interval * burst;
  }

  take(key: string, now: number, cost = 1): Decision {
    const tat     = Math.max(this.tat.get(key) ?? now, now);   // idle time doesn't bank credit
    const newTat  = tat + this.interval * cost;
    const allowAt = newTat - this.tolerance;

    if (now < allowAt) return { allowed: false, retryAfterMs: allowAt - now };
    this.tat.set(key, newTat);                                 // safe to forget once now > newTat
    return { allowed: true, retryAfterMs: 0 };
  }
}
```

Check it against the token bucket. With rate 10/s and burst 5, five requests at the same instant push TAT forward by 100ms each, to `now + 500ms`, which is exactly the tolerance. The sixth would need TAT at `now + 600ms` and is rejected with a 100ms retry hint, which is one refill interval. Both algorithms produce the same decisions from different state.

Wiring any of these into middleware (which key, which plan, which response) is where 18.2b and 18.3 pick up.

## Further Reading

- **Stripe Engineering, "Scaling your API with rate limiters"** (Paul Tarjan). Token-bucket request limits alongside a concurrency limiter and two kinds of load shedder. The clearest public account of using several of these together.
- **Cloudflare blog, "How we built rate limiting capable of scaling to millions of domains."** The sliding window counter, and the measured error rate that justifies the approximation.
- **Brandur Leach, "Rate Limiting, Cells, and GCRA."** GCRA explained from first principles, with the redis-cell implementation.
- **NGINX documentation, `ngx_http_limit_req_module`**, together with the NGINX blog post "Rate Limiting with NGINX." A leaky bucket as a queue, and how `burst`, `nodelay`, and `delay` move it between shaping and policing.
- **AWS API Gateway documentation on request throttling**, and **Shopify's API rate-limit documentation.** Two production APIs that document their algorithms (token bucket and leaky bucket as a meter) and their burst and rate parameters.
- **AWS Builders' Library, "Fairness in multi-tenant systems"** and **"Using load shedding to avoid overload."** Where per-caller limits stop and service protection starts.
- **_Site Reliability Engineering_ (Google), ch. 21, "Handling Overload."** Per-customer limits, client-side throttling, and why a rejection still costs something.
- **_System Design Interview_, Vol. 1, Alex Xu, ch. 4.** A compact survey of these same algorithms, useful as a second explanation.
- **`golang.org/x/time/rate` package documentation.** A token bucket with a notably well-designed API.

A Go sibling would be worth writing, for two reasons this article can't show. `x/time/rate` exposes `Allow`, `Reserve`, and `Wait` on the same limiter, so policing versus shaping becomes the caller's choice per call rather than an architectural decision. And Go's shared-memory goroutines force the read-refill-decrement race into the open inside a single process, where it needs a mutex, which Node's single thread hides until the state moves to Redis. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: whenever a spec, a contract, or a vendor's documentation says "N per T," ask two questions before building anything. Per *which* T: calendar-aligned, or trailing? And how much of N may arrive at once? Those two answers are the policy. The number alone describes a volume, and says nothing about how that volume may arrive.
