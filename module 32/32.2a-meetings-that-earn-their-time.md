# Meetings That Earn Their Time

_Module 32 · Communication & Team Dynamics · 32.2a_

## The Problem

Suppose you work on a ticketing platform for small music venues and theatres: event setup, seat maps, on-sale queues for high-demand shows, payments and payouts to venues. There are 22 engineers in three squads, plus product, design and an SRE pair. After a quarter in which two planned features slipped, the engineering manager exports everyone's calendar for a month. The median engineer spends 11 hours a week in meetings; the four senior engineers spend 19. Nobody thinks 11 hours sounds outrageous. Looking at what those hours contain is more uncomfortable.

**The sync nobody can explain.** Every Thursday there's a 60-minute "Architecture Sync" with 14 invitees. The agenda field says "updates." It was created two years ago to coordinate a monorepo migration that finished eighteen months ago. Most weeks, three senior engineers debate something while eleven people half-listen with their cameras off. Occasionally a decision gets made; it's never written down. Nobody cancels the meeting because nobody is sure they're allowed to, and nobody skips it because decisions sometimes happen there.

**The decision meeting that became two.** The seat-map renderer struggles with venues over 5,000 seats, and there's a meeting to decide whether to render large maps on the server. Eight people attend. There's no pre-read, so the first 25 minutes go to explaining the problem, with three people explaining it slightly differently. The most senior engineer states his preference early; after that, the discussion is about his option. With five minutes left someone says "let's take this offline." A second meeting is booked for the following week. It goes roughly the same way.

**The meeting without the person who knew.** A cross-squad meeting agrees on how the on-sale queue will release customers to checkout for a big show. The SRE who owns the payment provider's rate limits isn't invited. The plan exceeds the provider's limit by a factor of three, which is found in a load test two days before the on-sale.

**The insight that arrived by DM.** In the seat-map meetings, a junior engineer, Mei, noticed that seat locks expire after 10 minutes while the slowest checkout step can take 12. That was a real bug and more urgent than the renderer. She didn't find a moment to say it; the conversation was moving fast between people who'd worked together for years. She mentions it to her lead in a DM afterward.

**The day with no hours in it.** Here is a staff engineer's typical Tuesday:

```
      09    10    11    12    13    14    15    16    17
       │     │     │     │     │     │     │     │     │
          ▓        ▓▓▓▓▓▓         ▓▓▓      ▓▓▓▓▓▓
       standup     design review  1:1      incident review

       meetings: 2h45        longest free block: 1h30 (lunch)
```

Two hours and forty-five minutes of meetings, which sounds like most of the day is free. But the longest stretch without one is 90 minutes, and that's lunch. The payout-reconciliation bug she's been assigned needs a few hours of holding a lot of state in her head at once. It takes three weeks, working in 60-minute pieces.

The manager's first reaction is to introduce "No-Meeting Wednesdays." Wednesdays improve. Every meeting that used to happen on Wednesday moves to Tuesday or Thursday, and those days get worse.

None of these meetings was pointless. Each had a real reason to exist. What they lacked was a clear job, the inputs and people that job needed, a structure that let everyone contribute, an ending that survived the meeting, and a budget for how much of everyone's week meetings can take in total.

## What It Is

A meeting is a **synchronous tool with a specific job**. It's the right tool when that job needs real back-and-forth, when shared understanding has to be built quickly, or when the point is the relationship itself. It's an expensive tool: an hour for eight people costs eight hours of work, plus whatever it does to the time around it.

Running effective meetings means five things:

- **Giving each meeting a job.**
- **Giving it the inputs and people that job needs,** and no more people than that.
- **Structuring it so the useful information in the room actually comes out.**
- **Ending it with an outcome that outlives it.**
- **Budgeting meetings as a whole,** so the total doesn't destroy the time the rest of the work needs.

Two neighbors are already covered. Scrum's events (28.2a) are meetings with fixed jobs in a feedback loop; everything here applies to them, but their purposes aren't reargued here. Status meetings, which usually shouldn't be meetings at all, were handled in 32.1a. This article is about everything else: design discussions, decisions, cross-team coordination, recurring syncs, and the calendar they add up to.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A recurring meeting's agenda says "updates," "sync," or nothing.
- Nobody can say what the last three occurrences of a recurring meeting decided.
- Meetings end with "let's take this offline," and nothing specific happens next.
- The first third of a decision meeting is spent explaining the problem.
- Discussion follows whoever spoke first, or whoever is most senior.
- Useful points arrive in DMs after the meeting.
- A plan made in a meeting falls apart when someone who wasn't there sees it.
- More than half the invitees don't speak.
- Senior engineers rarely have a free block longer than two hours.
- A meeting-free day made the other days worse.

## How It Works

### 1. Name the job

Most meeting problems start with a meeting whose job was never stated, so nobody could tell whether it was done. There are four jobs worth a meeting, and each needs something different:

| Job | Example | Needs | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Decide** | Server-render large seat maps? | The decider, the options written up in advance, the people with key information | A decision, with reasons and owners |
| **Solve** | Why are payouts off by a few cents? | The people who know the parts of the system, a shared screen, time | An understanding or a plan |
| **Align** | A reorg, a major incident, a change of direction | Everyone affected, room for questions | Shared understanding, questions answered |
| **Relate** | 1:1s, team retros, social time | Trust, and not being rushed | A relationship |

Informing people usually isn't a job for a meeting. A written update does it better (32.1a). The exception is news that will raise questions or emotions, like a reorganization, where people need to be able to react and hear others react. That's the Align row.

Mixing jobs is a common failure. The Architecture Sync tries to inform fourteen people, solve problems for three, and occasionally decide something, all in the same hour. None of those jobs gets what it needs.

The Relate row deserves defending. A 1:1 or a team lunch doesn't need an agenda or a measurable outcome; its purpose is a relationship that makes everything else easier, including disagreements (32.4). The problem is relationship meetings disguised as status meetings, or status meetings kept alive because they're the only time the team talks.

### 2. Write the agenda as questions

"Seat maps" is a topic. "Decide: do we render seat maps on the server for venues over 5,000 seats?" is a question, and a meeting can tell when it has answered a question. A useful agenda item has three parts: the question, the outcome wanted (decide, solve, or align), and who owns it. Add a time limit when the meeting has more than one item.

```
Seat-map rendering — Thu 30 min
Pre-read: "Large venue seat maps" (link), 2 pages. Please read before.

1. Decide: server-render maps over 5,000 seats, or optimize the
   client renderer first?                          owner: Ravi  (20 min)
2. Solve: what do we tell the two venues whose on-sales are
   next month?                                     owner: Lena  (10 min)
```

The agenda is also a test. If no item needs live back-and-forth, cancel the meeting and send a message. If the organizer can't write the question, the meeting isn't ready to happen yet.

### 3. Invite the people the job needs, and no more

For a decision meeting, three kinds of people need to be there: **whoever decides**, **whoever has information the decision depends on**, and **whoever has to carry it out** if their buy-in is uncertain. Everyone else gets the written outcome.

The on-sale meeting failed on the second kind. The question "who knows something this plan depends on?" would have surfaced the SRE, because the plan depended on payment throughput.

Size matters because a group's conversation doesn't scale linearly. Among _n_ people there are _n(n−1)/2_ pairs who might need to understand each other: 10 pairs among 5 people, 28 among 8, 91 among 14. In practice, beyond about seven or eight people a decision meeting becomes a few people talking while the rest listen. Listening is better done by reading the outcome later.

Two habits make small invite lists work. First, publish outcomes so being left out doesn't mean being uninformed. Second, make it normal to leave. Open Space events call this the "law of two feet": if you're neither contributing nor learning, go somewhere you will.

### 4. Build shared context before the discussion

The first 25 minutes of the seat-map meeting were spent building shared context. That's the most expensive way to do it, because eight people are paying for it at once and because spoken explanations drift. There are two ways to do this better:

- **A pre-read**, sent a day ahead: a short written statement of the problem, the options and a recommendation, in the proposal format from 32.1a.
- **A read-in at the start of the meeting.** Everyone reads the document silently for the first 10–15 minutes, and then the discussion starts. Amazon popularized this. It works precisely because people don't reliably read pre-reads, and it guarantees everyone discusses the same document.

Choose one and be explicit about which. The worst version is a pre-read that half the room read, with the discussion paced for the half that did.

### 5. Structure the meeting so everyone's information comes out

The seat-map meeting surfaced the senior engineer's opinion and lost Mei's bug. That's the default outcome of open discussion, not bad luck. The first opinion stated anchors everything after it. Seniority and confidence decide who speaks. Fast conversations between people who know each other well leave no gaps for anyone else. A few techniques counteract this, and they're cheap:

- **Write before talking.** Give everyone three minutes to write their view or concerns silently, then share them. This gets each person's independent view before anchoring can happen. Brainwriting is the brainstorming version.
- **Go around the room.** Ask each person in turn, especially in remote meetings, where it's hardest to find a gap.
- **Most senior speaks last.** If the person with the most authority or reputation gives their view first, the meeting becomes a discussion of that view.
- **Pair before group.** In the Liberating Structures pattern called 1-2-4-All, people think alone, then compare in pairs, then fours, then the whole group. Quieter people get to test an idea with one person before offering it to eight.
- **Separate facilitating from deciding.** When the stakes are high, the person running the conversation shouldn't be the person whose view is under discussion.
- **Park tangents visibly.** Write off-topic points in a "parking lot" list with an owner, so they're neither lost nor allowed to take over.

Problem-solving meetings have a predictable shape, which Sam Kaner's _Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making_ describes as a diamond:

```
    divergence             "groan zone"             convergence
   (many ideas)       (confusion, friction)       (narrowing, deciding)

        ◁─────────────────────◇─────────────────────▷
```

The uncomfortable middle, where ideas collide and nothing seems to progress, is a normal stage, not a sign the meeting is failing. Meetings that close early to escape it tend to reach shallow decisions. Meetings that never move past it end with "let's take this offline." Naming the stage out loud helps: "We're in the messy part. Let's give it ten more minutes, then narrow down."

### 6. End with an outcome that outlives the meeting

Reserve the last five minutes for reading back what was decided, what wasn't, who owns each next step, and by when. Then post it within a day, in the shape from 32.1a.

"Let's take this offline" is acceptable only when it becomes a specific owner, a specific question and a date: "Ravi and Lena will measure client-side render time on the two biggest venues and bring numbers on Tuesday." Without those, it just means the meeting ran out of time.

### 7. Budget the whole calendar

Every meeting in the Problem could be fixed individually and the staff engineer's Tuesday would still have no usable time in it. Meetings have to be budgeted as a whole.

**The cost of a meeting is more than its length.** Its direct cost is people × duration: an hour with 14 invitees is 14 hours. Its hidden cost is **fragmentation**, meaning what it does to the time around it. Paul Graham's essay on the **maker's schedule and the manager's schedule** makes the distinction: a manager's day is divided into hour-long slots, and a meeting takes one slot. A maker (an engineer, a designer, a writer) needs half-days, and one meeting in the middle of a half-day ruins the whole half-day. The 20 minutes before a meeting aren't usable for deep work.

The same meetings, arranged differently:

```
      09    10    11    12    13    14    15    16    17
       │     │     │     │     │     │     │     │     │
spread    ▓        ▓▓▓▓▓▓         ▓▓▓      ▓▓▓▓▓▓         longest free: 1h30
clustered ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓                               longest free: 4h45

       both days: 2h45 of meetings
```

A few policies protect the time, and each has a catch:

- **Meeting windows.** For example, meetings only happen before lunch, or only between 13:00 and 16:00. This helps makers the most. The catch is that people whose job is mostly meetings get very dense windows.
- **Meeting-free days.** They're easy to explain, but as the Problem shows, they can compress the remaining days. They work better combined with a reduction in total meetings than on their own.
- **Shorter defaults.** Make 25 or 50 minutes the calendar default, so there's a gap between back-to-back meetings.
- **Expiry dates for recurring meetings.** Every recurring meeting gets an owner and a review date; unrenewed meetings end. In early 2023 Shopify did the drastic version, deleting recurring meetings with three or more people from everyone's calendars and asking teams to re-add only the ones they found they missed.
- **Measuring focus time,** not just meeting hours. The Implementation section below shows how.

## When It's Appropriate

- **More than about ten people, or more than one team.** Small teams can fix a meeting by talking; at this size, meetings accumulate without anyone owning the total.
- **Senior engineers spending more than about a third of their week in meetings,** especially if they also own hard technical work.
- **Decisions that keep being reopened,** or that turn into series of meetings.
- **Cross-team work with many dependencies,** where the wrong invite list has real consequences, like the rate limit nobody knew about.
- **Teams with a wide range of seniority, confidence or language fluency,** where open discussion reliably loses some people's information.
- **Hybrid teams,** where some attendees share a room and others join on a screen, and the people on the screen are the easiest to lose.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Three people solving a problem at a whiteboard.** An agenda, a facilitator and a read-back would only slow them down.
- **Exploratory and social conversations.** A 1:1 whose agenda is set entirely by the manager, or a team lunch with a structure, loses the point of the conversation.
- **Incidents.** They have their own structure (an incident commander, a scribe, 24.3), and general meeting practice doesn't apply.
- **When meetings are the symptom, not the cause.** If there are too many coordination meetings because every feature touches four teams, the problem is team boundaries and ownership (Conway's law, 2.1 and 36). If decisions keep being reopened because nobody knows who decides, the problem is decision rights (32.4b). Better meetings won't fix either.
- **Heavy process for its own sake.** A mandatory template on every calendar invite quickly becomes a box people fill with "discuss."

## Trade-offs

- **Preparation moves the cost to the organizer.** Writing a question-shaped agenda and a two-page pre-read might take an hour. That's a good trade for an eight-person decision meeting, and a bad one for a quick sync between two people.
- **Structure can feel stiff.** Silent writing and going around the room feel awkward the first few times, and experienced groups sometimes resent them. They need to be used where they help, not everywhere.
- **Smaller invite lists create information gaps.** People who weren't invited may feel excluded or distrusted, and they depend entirely on the written outcome being posted. If outcomes aren't reliably posted, small meetings make things worse.
- **Clustering helps makers and costs managers.** A meeting window gives engineers afternoons but gives the people in every meeting a solid block of back-to-back calls.
- **Fewer meetings means fewer casual conversations.** Some of the value in a bad recurring meeting was that people saw each other. Cancelling it without replacing that contact weakens relationships (32.3b).
- **Deciding with the decider present makes decisions wait for their calendar.** A decision needing a busy person in the room may wait a week for a slot. Sometimes a decision by document (32.1a) is faster.
- **Measuring focus time invites gaming.** Once blocked-off "focus time" is counted, people will block their calendars defensively, whether or not they're focusing.

## Alternatives

- **Deciding in writing** (32.1a). A proposal with a comment window and a named decider, with a call only if a thread doesn't converge. Slower per decision, but it doesn't consume anyone's calendar.
- **Office hours.** A standing time when a senior engineer or a team is available for questions, instead of ad-hoc meetings and interruptions. People batch their questions, and the expert's day stays predictable.
- **Working sessions.** For a Solve job, pairing or mob programming on the actual problem (32.2b) is often better than discussing it.
- **Recorded demos and walkthroughs.** For Align jobs that don't need immediate questions, a recording can be watched at a convenient time.
- **Reshaping team boundaries.** If most meetings are coordination between teams, fewer dependencies beats better coordination.

## How to Achieve It

1. **Audit recurring meetings.** List every recurring meeting with its owner, job, attendees, and what its last three occurrences decided or produced. Cancel the ones without an owner or a job; merge the ones that overlap; give the rest review dates. This is usually the largest single gain. _Next step when:_ the remaining meetings have clear jobs, but decision meetings still sprawl.
2. **Adopt agendas as questions and posted outcomes.** No decision meeting without a question-shaped agenda and a pre-read or read-in, and every meeting ends with a read-back and a post. _Next step when:_ meetings end with decisions, but they tend to be the decisions of whoever spoke first.
3. **Add structure to decision and design meetings.** Silent writing before discussion, senior speakers last, a separate facilitator for high-stakes decisions. _Next step when:_ meetings work well individually, but people still have no time to do the work.
4. **Shape the calendar.** Agree on meeting windows or a meeting-free day, shorter defaults, and measure focus time as a team.

Signs you've gone too far: people stop talking informally because every conversation feels like it needs an agenda; decisions wait days for a correctly structured meeting; the templates are filled with "discuss."

## Implementation

Most of this is facilitation and agreement. One part benefits from a little code: measuring the fragmentation that meeting hours alone hide. A team can compute it from calendar data (a calendar API, or exported ICS files expanded with a library that handles recurrence rules). The core is one function: given a day's busy intervals, how many minutes fall in free blocks long enough for deep work?

```ts
type Interval = { start: number; end: number }; // minutes since midnight

const WORKDAY: Interval = { start: 9 * 60, end: 17 * 60 };

/** Minutes of the workday that sit in free blocks of at least `minBlock` minutes. */
function focusMinutes(busy: Interval[], minBlock = 120, day = WORKDAY): number {
  const sorted = busy
    .map(b => ({ start: Math.max(b.start, day.start), end: Math.min(b.end, day.end) }))
    .filter(b => b.end > b.start)
    .sort((a, b) => a.start - b.start);

  let cursor = day.start;
  let focus = 0;
  for (const b of sorted) {
    const gap = b.start - cursor;
    if (gap >= minBlock) focus += gap;
    cursor = Math.max(cursor, b.end); // overlapping meetings don't move the cursor back
  }
  const tail = day.end - cursor;
  return tail >= minBlock ? focus + tail : focus;
}
```

Applied to the staff engineer's Tuesday, the two arrangements from the diagram give very different answers, even though both have exactly 2h45 of meetings:

```ts
const at = (h: number, m = 0) => h * 60 + m;

const spread = [
  { start: at(9, 30), end: at(9, 45) },  // standup
  { start: at(11),    end: at(12) },     // design review
  { start: at(13, 30), end: at(14) },    // 1:1
  { start: at(15),    end: at(16) },     // incident review
];
const clustered = [{ start: at(9, 30), end: at(12, 15) }];

focusMinutes(spread);    // 0   — no free block reaches two hours
focusMinutes(clustered); // 285 — the whole afternoon (lunch included)
```

A weekly report of meeting hours alongside focus hours, as a median per team, makes the Tuesday problem visible in a way meeting hours alone can't.

> **What counts as "busy" decides what you measure.** Exclude declined events and "tentative" holds nobody attends, or fragmentation looks worse than it is. Decide explicitly how to treat blocked-off "focus time" events: counting them as meetings hides exactly the time you're trying to measure, and counting them as free means anyone can raise their score by blocking their calendar. Treating them as free and also reporting how much time is blocked defensively is usually the honest option; a rising amount of defensive blocking is itself a signal that the calendar is under pressure.

> **Report teams, not individuals.** A manager's calendar legitimately looks like a manager's schedule; ranking people by focus hours would penalize them for their job, and invite people to game the number. The useful comparisons are a team against its own history, and makers against the rough goal the team agreed on (say, at least two two-hour blocks a day).

## Further Reading

- **Paul Graham, "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule"** (2009). The short essay behind the fragmentation argument.
- **Steven G. Rogelberg, _The Surprising Science of Meetings_** (2019). A summary of research on meeting size, length, facilitation and how attendees experience them.
- **Sam Kaner et al., _Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making_.** The diamond model and a large toolkit of facilitation techniques.
- **Liberating Structures** (liberatingstructures.com). A library of small, well-described meeting structures, including 1-2-4-All.
- **Patrick Lencioni, _Death by Meeting_** (2004). Argues for separating meetings by job and frequency, told as a fable.
- **Andy Grove, _High Output Management_** (1983). The chapter on meetings, and the distinction between process-oriented and mission-oriented meetings.
- **Priya Parker, _The Art of Gathering_** (2018). On giving any gathering a specific, disputable purpose; broader than work meetings, and useful for them.
- **Harrison Owen, _Open Space Technology: A User's Guide_.** The source of the "law of two feet."
- **Frederick P. Brooks Jr., _The Mythical Man-Month_.** The communication-paths argument, originally about team size and schedules.
