# Remote Teams: Replacing What the Office Did by Accident

_Module 32 · Communication & Team Dynamics · 32.3b_

## The Problem

Suppose you work on a platform for independent music teachers: lesson booking, invoicing parents, practice tracking for students, and a marketplace where new students find teachers. There are 34 engineers. The company went fully remote in 2020 and later became "office optional," with an office in Manchester. About 15 engineers come in most days; the rest live elsewhere in the UK and rarely visit. Everyone shares a time zone, so the problems of 32.3a don't apply. Over a year:

**The new hire who never joined.** Jonas joins remotely as a backend engineer. The written onboarding is good (31.4a): he has a working environment on day one and ships a small fix in his first week. Three months in, he's technically productive and knows four people. He doesn't know who to ask about payments. When he asks in the wrong channel, he gets a one-line reply pointing him elsewhere, which he reads as irritation. He stops asking in public and works things out alone, slowly. He leaves after seven months. His exit interview says he never felt part of the team.

**Decisions in the kitchen.** Three office engineers, talking over lunch, agree to switch the library that generates invoice PDFs. The remote engineers find out from the pull request, which already rewrites the templates one of them was halfway through changing. In hybrid meetings, six people sit around a table and eight appear as tiles on a screen. The remote people can't hear side comments, can't see the whiteboard, and can't find a gap in the conversation to speak.

**The promotions nobody meant to skew.** In the last promotion cycle, five of the six engineers promoted come to the office regularly, though office regulars are under half of engineering. Nobody intended this. When asked, one manager says honestly: "I just saw more of their work."

**Tool sprawl.** There are 140 chat channels. Sales and support use a different chat tool from engineering. One team runs its standups in a separate community app. There are three video tools, and every notification is on by default. A notice about a production database migration, posted in `#general` between a birthday message and a lunch photo, is missed by the two teams it affects.

**The surveillance proposal.** After a feature slips, a director proposes activity-monitoring software that records keyboard activity and takes periodic screenshots, "to understand how remote people spend their time." It isn't adopted, but word gets around. Engineers start answering messages instantly to look present, and some keep a mouse-jiggler running to keep their status green. A senior engineer leaves. Meanwhile, the real cause of the slip goes unnoticed: two remote engineers were stuck on a payment-provider problem for a week. Neither said anything, because saying "I'm stuck" in a channel felt like admitting they couldn't cope.

**Nobody noticed.** A quiet engineer works later and later, turns her camera off, and posts less. In an office, someone might have noticed she looked exhausted. Remotely, the first sign anyone registers is her resignation letter, citing burnout.

The underlying issue: an office provides several things **for free, as side effects**. People overhear what's going on, get to know people outside their team, see decisions being made, notice when a colleague is struggling, and learn how things work just by being around. None of that was ever designed. Remote work removes those side effects entirely. Hybrid work is harder still, because it removes them for some people and not others. A remote team has to provide these things **deliberately**, or go without them.

## What It Is

A **remote team** does most of its work without sharing a physical space. A **hybrid team** has some people sharing a space some of the time. It helps to distinguish three stances:

- **Remote-first:** work is designed around the remote participant. Everything that matters happens in channels open to everyone, and the office, if there is one, is a place to meet, not where decisions are made.
- **Remote-friendly:** the office is the center, and remote people are accommodated. This is where the kitchen decision and the promotion skew come from.
- **Fully co-located:** everyone in one place, where most of this article doesn't apply.

The central practice is **replacing the office's side effects with deliberate mechanisms**. That covers how people find out what's happening, how they get to know each other, how they're onboarded, how trust is built, and how struggles get noticed. The tool setup that carries all of this also has to be treated as infrastructure.

Three neighbors cover related ground. Time-zone distance is 32.3a. This article assumes people share working hours, and its problems exist even then. Writing and decisions in writing are 32.1a, and meeting practice is 32.2a. This article is about the social layer those rest on.

## Recognizing the Problem

- New hires are technically productive but, months in, know only a few people outside their team.
- Remote people learn about decisions from PRs or announcements after the fact.
- In hybrid meetings, the people in the room do most of the talking.
- Promotions, high-visibility projects or praise go disproportionately to people who are physically present.
- Important announcements are missed because they're in a noisy channel or the wrong tool.
- Different teams use different tools for the same purpose.
- People are stuck for days before anyone finds out.
- Green-dot status, instant replies or camera use are treated as signs of work.
- Burnout or disengagement is discovered at resignation.
- Cross-team collaboration has quietly dropped, and teams have become islands.

## How It Works

### 1. List what the office did, and replace each on purpose

Every item in the Problem is one of the office's side effects going missing:

| What the office did by accident | Remote replacement, done deliberately |
|---|---|
| Overhearing what others are working on | Working in the open: public channels, draft PRs, written weekly notes, demos |
| Getting to know people outside your team | Cross-team connections by design: onboarding introductions, pairing across teams, random coffee |
| Seeing decisions being made | Decisions don't count until posted (32.1a) |
| Noticing a colleague struggling | Regular 1:1s, explicit norms for asking for help, managers asking directly |
| Learning how things work by being around | Structured onboarding with a buddy and a people map |
| The meeting room as the shared space | The video call as the shared space: everyone joins the same way |

Researchers have measured what happens when nothing is replaced. A study of more than 60,000 Microsoft employees during the shift to firm-wide remote work (Yang et al., _Nature Human Behaviour_, 2022) found collaboration became more siloed. People worked more within their own groups and formed fewer connections across groups, and communication shifted toward asynchronous channels. Those cross-group connections are what sociologist Mark Granovetter called **weak ties**: acquaintances rather than close colleagues. They're how information travels between parts of an organization, and they're exactly what an office generates by accident.

### 2. Make hybrid symmetric

Hybrid is the hardest arrangement, because the side effects still exist for the people in the office and are missing for everyone else. The result is two classes of employee. A few rules restore symmetry:

- **One remote, all remote.** If anyone joins a meeting remotely, everyone joins from their own laptop, even people in the same office, using headphones. Everyone gets the same view, the same audio, the same chance to speak, and the same shared digital whiteboard. A room full of people around one microphone is the problem this rule fixes.
- **Decisions happen where everyone can see them.** Lunch conversations are fine; lunch decisions aren't final until posted where the remote people will read them, with time to object. This is the rule from 32.1a, and hybrid teams need it most.
- **Office time for what it's good at.** If people come to the office, schedule collaboration for those days: workshops, planning, difficult conversations, social time. Don't expect people to commute to do solo work on video calls they could have joined from home.
- **Check for proximity bias.** Managers see more of the people they sit near, and that shows up in who gets interesting projects, praise and promotions. Base assessments on written evidence of impact, like design docs, PRs, incident write-ups and peer feedback, rather than impressions. Then compare outcomes by work location each cycle. In the Problem, a check at the end of the cycle would have made the five-of-six pattern impossible to miss.

### 3. Treat tools as infrastructure

In an office, the room is a shared default: everyone knows where the meeting is. Remotely, the tools are the room, and a team with four chat tools and three video tools has several rooms with no map. Treat the tool setup like any other infrastructure: owned, minimal, and configured deliberately.

- **One tool per job.** One chat tool, one video tool, one home for documents (29.3a), one tracker. Every exception needs a reason, like a customer-facing tool support must use. Two chat tools for the same job guarantee that someone is always in the wrong one.
- **Channel conventions.** Use prefixes that explain a channel's purpose from its name, such as `#team-`, `#proj-`, `#help-`, `#incident-` and `#social-`. Make `#announce-` channels restricted to a few posters, so notices like the migration warning aren't buried. Archive channels that go quiet.
- **Sensible notification defaults.** Announcements notify; social channels don't. Suggest defaults to new people rather than leaving them with everything on.
- **Status as information, not surveillance.** A status line like "focus time until 14:00" or "at the dentist, back 11:30" helps colleagues. Being online, or "green," is not evidence of work, and treating it as such creates the presence theatre in the Problem.
- **Write the map down.** A short "how we communicate" page covering which tool for what, channel naming, response-time norms (32.1a) and where announcements go. Give it to every new person on day one, and give it an owner.

### 4. Onboard people into the team, not just the codebase

Jonas's onboarding solved the technical problem and not the social one. Good remote onboarding treats both as the job:

- **A buddy who isn't the manager.** Someone on the team whose explicit job for the first six weeks is being the person it's safe to ask anything. That includes the "stupid" questions and "who do I ask about payments?"
- **A people map.** A list of perhaps ten to fifteen people across teams, each with a reason: "Priya owns payments; meet her before you touch invoicing." Schedule short introductory calls in the first month. This is the weak-tie network, built on purpose.
- **Pairing in the first weeks** (32.2b), with rotating teammates, so the new person learns how people work, not just what the code does.
- **An early, real contribution**, followed by a gradual move into more central work, planned rather than hoped for.
- **Check-ins at 30, 60 and 90 days** that ask about connection as well as productivity. "Who would you ask if you were stuck on X?" is a revealing question. If the answer is "no idea" at 60 days, something is missing.
- **In-person time early, if the company ever gathers.** Meeting people in person in the first few months accelerates everything after.

"Time to first PR" measures the technical half of onboarding. The social half needs its own questions.

### 5. Build trust from visible work, not monitoring

Managers of remote teams often worry about not being able to _see_ work. The instinct is to measure presence: activity, online status, cameras. That backfires in two ways. It measures the wrong thing, since presence isn't output and people quickly learn to produce presence. And it signals distrust, which damages the trust it was meant to create. Research reported in _Harvard Business Review_ in 2022 found that monitored employees were _more_ likely to break rules, as if the monitoring had shifted responsibility for their behavior onto the monitor.

What works instead is making **work** visible, which suits both sides:

- **Working in the open:** draft PRs early, discussion in public channels rather than DMs, short written weekly notes on progress and blockers.
- **Demos:** regular show-and-tell of what's been built, including half-finished work.
- **Managing by outcomes and commitments:** agreeing what will be done and by when, then following up on that, not on hours.

Trust between people grows from reliability over time. Doing what you said, when you said, visibly, is how a remote colleague earns it. New teams can start with a working assumption of trust and adjust from experience, rather than making people prove themselves first.

### 6. Make it safe to say "I'm stuck"

The two engineers stuck for a week weren't hiding. In an office, someone would have seen them frowning at the same screen for days and asked. Remotely, the only way anyone learns someone is stuck is if they say so, and saying so in writing, in a public channel, feels more exposing than a hallway comment.

Amy Edmondson's term for the underlying condition is **psychological safety**: the shared belief that you won't be punished or humiliated for asking a question, admitting a mistake or raising a concern. Google's Project Aristotle, a study of what distinguished its effective teams, found it was the most important factor. Remote teams need it more, and have fewer natural ways to build it. A few things help:

- **Make asking for help an explicit norm, with a threshold.** For example: "if you've been stuck for half a day, say so in your team channel." A rule makes asking a normal expectation rather than a confession.
- **Leaders go first.** When a tech lead posts "I've spent two hours on this and I'm stuck, can someone look?", everyone else learns it's safe.
- **Respond to questions well,** especially from new people. The one-line redirect Jonas got was efficient and did lasting damage. "Good question, the payments folks in #help-payments will know. I'll tag Priya" costs ten more seconds.
- **Blameless reviews** of incidents and slips (24.3), so bringing up problems doesn't lead to blame.

### 7. Build relationships on purpose

Relationships are what let people disagree well (32.4), ask for help, and assume good intent in a terse message. Offices build them without trying. Remote teams need a mix of small regular mechanisms and occasional bigger ones:

- **Random coffee.** Regularly pair people, preferably across teams, for a 20–30 minute unstructured call. Opt-in, low-pressure, and sustained over months, this rebuilds weak ties at a steady rate. The Implementation section shows a pairing algorithm.
- **Team rituals that aren't about status:** demos, a weekly show-and-tell, a retrospective (28.2a) that has room for how people are doing, social channels for hobbies and pets.
- **Gathering in person, with a budget.** Many remote companies bring teams or the whole company together once or twice a year; Automattic's annual Grand Meetup is a well-known example. Spend the time on relationships, planning and difficult conversations, not on heads-down work that could be done anywhere.
- **Variety and opt-in.** Not everyone likes virtual games or video socials. Offer several kinds of connection and let people choose. Mandatory fun works against its purpose.

### 8. Notice people

With no office to see people in, noticing is a deliberate job, and mostly a manager's job.

- **Regular 1:1s** that include how someone is doing, their workload, and whether they feel connected. Ask directly; don't try to infer it from activity data.
- **Norms that protect boundaries.** Agree on working hours and respect them. Keep notifications after hours to real emergencies (on-call exists for those). Make it normal to log off. Some countries now legislate this: Portugal, for example, restricts employers from contacting employees outside working hours.
- **Pay attention to changes over time,** gently and in conversation: someone going quiet, working late regularly, dropping out of social channels. These aren't metrics to monitor; they're prompts for a manager to ask a human question.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any team where a meaningful share of people work remotely,** even in one time zone.
- **Hybrid teams in particular,** where the office's side effects reach some people and not others.
- **Teams that are hiring.** Every new person is an onboarding test of whether these mechanisms exist.
- **Teams that grew or reorganized remotely,** where people may never have met their colleagues in other teams.
- **Signs of siloing:** fewer cross-team conversations, more duplicated work, surprise at what other teams are doing.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A small, fully co-located team.** The office still provides these side effects. Deliberate replacements would be ceremony.
- **Early-stage teams of five or six who all know each other well.** Relationships already exist; the main needs are the tools map and decisions in writing.
- **Structured socializing as a substitute for fixing work.** Virtual social events won't fix disengagement caused by unclear priorities, overload or a poor manager. Rituals help relationships; they don't replace good management.
- **Heavy rituals imposed on everyone.** Daily video socials, mandatory cameras, compulsory team games. Past a light baseline, more is worse.

## Trade-offs

- **Deliberate mechanisms take time.** Buddies, people maps, random coffee, demos and meetups all compete with delivery work, and their payoff is slow and hard to measure.
- **Symmetry costs the office people something.** "One remote, all remote" means people in the same office join from their own laptops, which feels absurd to them. Posting every lunch decision feels bureaucratic. The people who lose the most convenience are often the most influential.
- **Written visibility has a performative side.** Weekly notes and public channels can turn into signaling rather than informing (32.1a).
- **Consolidating tools means someone gives up a tool they prefer,** and some teams will resent it.
- **In-person gatherings cost money and travel,** and are harder for people with caring responsibilities or disabilities. They need planning for inclusion.
- **Relationship-building can't be forced.** Some people will opt out of every social mechanism. That's their right, and it means some weak ties will never form.
- **The remote replacements remain partial.** Even done well, remote teams tend to have fewer spontaneous cross-team connections than co-located ones. The goal is to make the loss small and deliberate, not zero.

## Alternatives

- **Return to the office,** fully or for set days. This restores the side effects for everyone who comes, at the cost of hiring reach, commuting, and losing the people who left for remote work. Studies of hybrid arrangements, such as Bloom and colleagues' randomized trial at Trip.com (_Nature_, 2024), found two home days a week reduced attrition without harming performance or promotions. That suggests the choice doesn't need to be all or nothing.
- **Anchor days.** Everyone in a team comes in on the same days, so office time is actually shared. Without coordination, hybrid often means people commute to sit on video calls with colleagues at home.
- **Co-located hubs.** Remote-first overall, with small clusters of people in a few cities who meet regularly.
- **Remote but same-team co-location.** Each team is either entirely in one office or entirely remote. This avoids within-team asymmetry, but not the asymmetry between teams.

## How to Achieve It

1. **Fix the tools map.** Consolidate to one tool per job, set channel conventions and an announcements channel, choose notification defaults, and write the "how we communicate" page. This is concrete, quick, and removes daily friction. _Next step when:_ information reliably reaches people, but hybrid meetings and decisions still favor the office.
2. **Make hybrid symmetric.** Adopt "one remote, all remote," require posted decisions, and schedule office days around collaboration. Add a proximity-bias check to the next performance and promotion cycle. _Next step when:_ remote and office people are equal in how work happens, but new people still take months to feel part of the team.
3. **Rebuild onboarding around people:** a buddy, a people map, pairing in the first weeks, and connection-focused check-ins. _Next step when:_ new people integrate well, but existing teams are drifting into silos.
4. **Add relationship mechanisms and norms:** random coffee across teams, demos, a threshold for asking for help, and regular in-person gatherings if the budget allows.

Throughout, watch for the overcorrection: rituals nobody wants, performative updates, and monitoring disguised as "visibility."

## Implementation

Most of this is organizational. One mechanism is easy to automate and benefits from a little thought: random coffee pairing. A naive version pairs people uniformly at random. That mostly pairs people within the same large team and repeats pairs, which works against the purpose. A better version prefers pairs across teams and across locations, and avoids repeating recent pairs.

```ts
type Person = { id: string; team: string; site: string }; // site: "manchester" | "remote" | …
type History = Map<string, number>; // pair key → times paired recently

const pairKey = (a: string, b: string) => [a, b].sort().join("|");

/** Higher is better: cross-team and cross-site pairs score up, repeats score down. */
function score(a: Person, b: Person, history: History): number {
  let s = 0;
  if (a.team !== b.team) s += 2;   // the weak ties the office used to create
  if (a.site !== b.site) s += 1;   // office ↔ remote, against the hybrid split
  s -= 3 * (history.get(pairKey(a.id, b.id)) ?? 0);
  return s;
}

function shuffle<T>(xs: T[], rng: () => number): T[] {
  for (let i = xs.length - 1; i > 0; i--) {
    const j = Math.floor(rng() * (i + 1));
    [xs[i], xs[j]] = [xs[j], xs[i]];
  }
  return xs;
}

/** Greedy pairing: each person, in random order, takes their best-scoring remaining partner. */
function pairUp(optedIn: Person[], history: History, rng = Math.random): Person[][] {
  const pool = shuffle([...optedIn], rng);
  const groups: Person[][] = [];
  while (pool.length > 1) {
    const a = pool.shift()!;
    let best = 0;
    for (let i = 1; i < pool.length; i++) {
      if (score(a, pool[i], history) > score(a, pool[best], history)) best = i;
    }
    groups.push([a, ...pool.splice(best, 1)]);
  }
  if (pool.length === 1) groups.at(-1)?.push(pool[0]); // odd one out makes a three
  return groups;
}
```

After each round, increment the history count for every pair in each group, and decay or expire old entries after a few months so people can meet again eventually. Post the groups in the social channel and suggest a time; nothing more is needed.

> **Greedy is good enough.** The random order combined with "best remaining partner" doesn't find the best possible set of pairs. The last few people get whoever is left. Optimal matching algorithms exist, but the difference is invisible to the people drinking the coffee, and the random order keeps rounds from being predictable. This is a case where the simple version is the right one.

> **Opt-in, and record only the pairing.** Only include people who've opted in, and make opting out a single click. Store who was paired, never whether they actually met or what they discussed. The moment the tool starts tracking attendance, it becomes monitoring, and it'll produce the same presence theatre as the green dots.

> **Other constraints fit in the score.** Teams across time zones can add a penalty for pairs with little working-hours overlap, using the overlap calculation from 32.3a. The scoring function is the place to express what "a useful connection" means for your organization.

## Further Reading

- **GitLab, _The Remote Playbook_ and the all-remote section of the GitLab Handbook** (handbook.gitlab.com). The most detailed public description of running a large remote company, including onboarding, informal communication and in-person meetups.
- **Tsedal Neeley, _Remote Work Revolution_** (2021). A research-based guide to trust, productivity and leadership in remote teams, including swift trust.
- **Amy C. Edmondson, _The Fearless Organization_** (2018). Psychological safety: what it is, how it's built, and why it matters for learning and speaking up.
- **Google re:Work, "Guide: Understand team effectiveness."** The write-up of Project Aristotle's findings.
- **Longqi Yang et al., "The effects of remote work on collaboration among information workers"** (_Nature Human Behaviour_, 2022). The Microsoft study on siloing and weak ties under firm-wide remote work.
- **Nicholas Bloom, Ruobing Han and James Liang, "Hybrid working from home improves retention without damaging performance"** (_Nature_, 2024). A randomized trial of hybrid work.
- **Mark Granovetter, "The Strength of Weak Ties"** (_American Journal of Sociology_, 1973). The original paper on why acquaintances matter for information flow.
- **Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, _Remote: Office Not Required_** (2013). A short, opinionated case for remote work, including its pitfalls.
