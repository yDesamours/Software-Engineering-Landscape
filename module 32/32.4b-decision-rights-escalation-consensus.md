# Decision Rights, Escalation and Consensus: Who Decides When We Don't Agree

_Module 32 · Communication & Team Dynamics · 32.4b_

## The Problem

Suppose you work on a platform schools use for trips and activities: parents pay for trips and sign consent forms, schools manage places and medical information, and cancelled trips are refunded. There are 40 engineers in six teams: Payments, Consent & Forms, Trips, Parent App, School Admin and Platform. The tech leads form an architecture guild that meets fortnightly. Nobody would describe the company as dysfunctional. Over two quarters:

**The decision nobody owned.** The public API, which school management systems integrate with, needs a versioning policy. The guild discusses it at four meetings over two months and publishes a "recommendation." Two teams follow it; one continues with URL versioning; one adds date-based versions in a header. A school integration partner, now dealing with three schemes, asks which one is official. Nobody can say, because nobody was ever given the right to decide.

**Everyone can say no.** A Platform engineer proposes adopting OpenTelemetry (19.3) across all services. Because it affects everyone, the process is that all six team leads must sign off. Five do within two weeks. The sixth, overloaded with a launch, doesn't reply. The proposal waits ten weeks, not because anyone objected, but because a process that needs everyone's agreement gives each person a veto, including the power to block by silence.

**Escalation as a weapon.** Payments and Consent & Forms disagree about who owns refunds when a trip is cancelled. After one exchange, the Payments lead messages the VP of Engineering, describing the other team as "blocking the refund work." The VP, hearing one side, assigns refunds to Consent & Forms. The Consent & Forms lead learns about it from the VP's announcement. Three weeks later, in a different dispute, Consent & Forms escalates first. Escalation has become a race to get to the VP first.

**Escalation that never happened.** Meanwhile, Trips and School Admin disagree about changes to a shared database table. Each team works around the other for six weeks, with duplicated columns and a sync job. Neither escalates, because escalating feels like admitting failure, or like complaining about colleagues. The director finds out at the quarterly review, when both teams report the same blocker.

**The opinion that ended a discussion.** In a Slack thread about the Parent App's API, the CTO writes: "Have we thought about GraphQL for this?" The discussion stops. The team starts a GraphQL spike the next day. A month later, when it comes up again, the CTO is surprised: it was a question, not an instruction.

None of these teams lacked good judgment or goodwill. Each failure came from the same gap: for each kind of decision, nobody had defined **who decides**, **how they decide**, and **what happens when people can't agree**. Without those, decisions went to whoever was most persistent, most senior, first to the VP, or simply fastest, and were made again whenever someone new objected.

## What It Is

Three connected ideas:

- **Decision rights** define who has the authority to make which decisions, and who must be consulted or informed. The core rule is that each decision has exactly one decider: a person, or a role such as "API owner," never a group of equals.
- **Decision method** is how the decider reaches the decision. The options range from deciding alone, through consulting people, to proceeding only if nobody has a reasoned objection.
- **Escalation** is a defined, normal path for moving a stuck decision to someone with authority over everyone involved, taken jointly and early rather than as a last resort.

**Rough consensus** is a particular method, developed by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF). It aims for agreement without unanimity: objections must be addressed, but not everyone has to agree, and nobody holds a veto.

This article is the structure around 32.4a. That article is about making disagreement productive: finding the real question, settling facts with evidence, committing properly. This one is about what happens when that process doesn't produce agreement, and about deciding in advance who holds the pen. The written record of the result is an ADR (2.5). How the decision is communicated is 32.1a.

## Recognizing the Problem

- People ask "who decides this?" and get different answers, or none.
- Guilds, committees or working groups "recommend," and teams choose whether to follow.
- Proposals that need everyone's approval wait for the slowest or least interested person.
- Decisions get reopened whenever someone new joins the discussion.
- Senior people's comments are treated as decisions, whether or not they're meant to be.
- Escalations come from one side, framing the other side as the problem.
- Disputes go unescalated for weeks, while teams build workarounds.
- The same kind of dispute (ownership, shared resources, standards) keeps coming back.
- Decisions made by leaders get reversed because they were missing information.

## How It Works

### 1. Write down who decides what

Several frameworks exist for this. They differ in vocabulary more than in substance:

- **RACI:** Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed. The most widely known, and the vaguest about the decision itself, because "accountable" and "responsible" are easy to confuse.
- **DACI:** Driver (runs the process), Approver (decides), Contributors (give input), Informed. Popular in product and engineering; Atlassian's team playbooks use it.
- **RAPID:** from Bain & Company. Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide. Its distinctive feature is **Agree**: a role with a veto, which should be limited to things like legal, security or compliance.

All three rest on the same rule: **exactly one decider**. "The six team leads" isn't a decider; it's a committee that can always fail to decide. Everyone else is either **consulted**, meaning their input is sought and considered but they can't block, or **informed**, meaning they're told the outcome. Being consulted doesn't mean approving. That distinction alone would have unblocked OpenTelemetry.

You don't need a matrix for every decision. You need one for the **kinds** of decision that cross boundaries and recur. For the trips platform:

| Decision | Decides | Must be consulted | Informed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design inside one service | The owning team | Anyone whose API calls change | Via ADR (2.5) |
| Public API changes and policy | API owner (in Platform) | Integration support, Product | All engineering, partners |
| Cross-cutting standards (observability, versioning) | Principal engineer, after an RFC | All tech leads | All engineering |
| Security or compliance exceptions | Security (has a veto) | Requesting team | Leadership |
| Which team owns a capability | Lowest common manager of the teams | Both tech leads | Affected teams |
| Taste (naming, formatting) | Whoever owns the code; else the linter (31.2) | — | — |

The underlying principle is sometimes called **subsidiarity**: decisions are made at the lowest level that has both the necessary information and all the consequences. A choice whose effects stay inside one service belongs to that team. A choice that affects every team, or every external partner, goes to a role that spans them. The versioning policy failed because it belonged to the second category and was handled by a guild that had no authority at all.

### 2. Choose a method, and say which one

Once the decider is known, they still choose how to decide. The methods trade speed against how much input is gathered and how committed people feel to the result:

```
fast, little buy-in                                               slow, broad buy-in
◀──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────▶
decide     consult,     advice        consent          rough          unanimous
alone      then decide  process       (no reasoned     consensus      consensus
                                       objection)      (objections
                                                        addressed)
```

- **Decide alone.** Right for reversible, low-impact decisions within the decider's own area.
- **Consult, then decide.** The decider collects input from the people who know and the people affected, then decides and explains. This is the right default for most technical decisions.
- **Advice process.** Anyone can make a decision, even one affecting others, provided they first seek advice from everyone meaningfully affected and from people with relevant expertise. They don't have to follow the advice. Andrew Harmel-Law describes using this for architecture decisions without a central architecture group. It spreads decisions widely, and depends on high trust and on the advice actually being sought.
- **Consent.** From sociocracy. A proposal goes ahead unless someone raises a **reasoned objection**, meaning a reason it would cause harm or set the group back, not a preference for something else. The test is "good enough for now, safe enough to try."
- **Rough consensus.** Described in the next section.
- **Unanimous consensus.** Everyone must agree. It gives everyone a veto, so it should be reserved for decisions where each person's genuine agreement is required, which is rare.
- **Voting.** Fine for taste-level choices among equals. For anything else, it lets numbers override expertise and gives the minority no reason to commit.

The most important habit is **stating the method at the start**. "I'll decide on Friday, after hearing from each team lead" and "we'll proceed on Friday unless someone raises a reasoned objection" set very different expectations, and both are far better than leaving people to guess. Victor Vroom and Philip Yetton's work in the 1970s formalized this idea: how much to involve people should depend on who has the information and how much the decision needs their commitment.

### 3. Consent and rough consensus: objections instead of agreement

Both consent and rough consensus are built on one idea: **it's the absence of serious objections that matters, not the presence of universal agreement.** RFC 7282, "On Consensus and Humming in the IETF," makes the point that a lack of disagreement is more important than agreement, and that counting heads is not the goal.

Three things make this work:

- **Objections must be reasoned.** "I'd rather use Jaeger" is a preference. "The OpenTelemetry Node SDK has no supported instrumentation for our queue library, so we'd lose tracing on the job system" is an objection. The person running the process distinguishes the two, and only objections are allowed to block.
- **Objections must be addressed, not necessarily accommodated.** Every objection gets a considered answer. Sometimes the proposal changes to integrate it; sometimes the objection is explained and set aside. What matters is that nobody's concern goes unanswered. That's the difference from the rate-limit concern in 32.4a, which was simply cut off.
- **Someone judges when it's rough enough.** In the IETF, a working group chair judges whether consensus has been reached, sometimes by asking people to hum rather than vote so that nobody's position is individually counted. In a company, the decider or facilitator does this. They then own that judgment, and it can be escalated if people think it was wrong.

Consent also needs a time limit, or it becomes unanimity by another name. "Objections by Friday; silence counts as consent" is the lazy consensus from 32.1a. Under that rule, the OpenTelemetry proposal would have gone ahead in two weeks, and the overloaded lead would still have had a real chance to object.

### 4. Separate opinions from decisions

When the most senior person in a discussion speaks, their words carry weight whether they intend it or not. The term **HiPPO**, the "highest-paid person's opinion," names the effect. The fix isn't for senior people to stay silent; their knowledge is often valuable. It's for them to **label what kind of statement they're making**:

- **"Just a question"** or **"a thought, ignore if not useful."** Input, with no expectation.
- **"A suggestion, your call."** Input the decider can weigh.
- **"A strong recommendation."** Input the decider should take seriously and explain if they don't follow.
- **"A decision."** It's decided, and it's the speaker's to decide.

"Have we thought about GraphQL? (just a question; the Parent App team decides)" would have saved a month. Teams can make these labels a shared convention, so that everyone uses them, not only senior people.

Two related tools make delegation explicit:

- **Jurgen Appelo's seven levels of delegation** (from Management 3.0) run from "I'll tell you" through "I'll consult you" and "we'll agree together" to "you decide and don't need to tell me."
- **Susan Scott's decision tree** uses leaf, branch, trunk and root decisions to mark which decisions someone makes alone, which they report afterwards, and which need discussion first.

Either gives a manager and a team a quick way to agree, for each kind of decision, how much freedom the team has.

### 5. Escalate together, early, and to the right place

Escalation is a normal part of decision-making, not a failure. When two people or teams with equal authority can't agree, the only way to reach a decision is to involve someone with authority over both. Andy Grove described this in _High Output Management_ as bringing in the "peer-plus-one." The trips platform's escalations went wrong in both directions: one was taken unilaterally as a weapon, and the other was never taken at all. A few rules fix both:

- **Escalate jointly.** Both sides escalate together, with one shared document: the question, the options, each side's best case (written by that side, or steelmanned by the other, 32.4a), what's been tried, the decision needed, and by when. A joint escalation can't be used as a weapon, because the other side wrote half of it.
- **Escalate early, on a trigger, not an emotion.** Agree in advance what triggers an escalation. Examples: a time box expiring (32.4a), a dispute blocking a committed date, or workarounds starting to appear. Trips and School Admin's six weeks of duplicated columns should have been an escalation in week one.
- **Go to the lowest common authority.** That's the lowest person or role with authority over both sides, usually their shared manager, not the most senior person available. Skipping levels is sometimes necessary, but it shouldn't be the default.
- **Make it ordinary.** Leaders can say outright that timely joint escalations are welcome and are a sign of good judgment. The teams in the Problem avoided escalating because it felt like complaining. That belief costs more than any escalation.

```
  disagree ──▶ 32.4a: find the layer, the crux, the evidence
                   │
                   ├── converged ──────────────────────────────▶ decide, record, commit
                   │
                   └── trigger fires (time box, blocked date, workaround)
                          │
                          ▼
                 joint escalation doc ──▶ lowest common authority
                 (both sides' best case)        │
                                                ├── decides this case, explains why
                                                └── if it's a recurring type:
                                                    fixes the decision rights too
```

The person receiving an escalation has obligations too. They should hear both sides together, not one at a time. They should decide or clarify promptly and explain their reasoning. And they should ask whether this escalation is a one-off or a symptom. If they keep receiving the same _kind_ of escalation, the real fix is a missing row in the decision-rights table.

### 6. Most recurring disputes are ownership disputes

The refund dispute wasn't about refunds; it was about who owns a capability that sits between two teams. Disputes like this recur, in slightly different forms, until the ownership question is answered explicitly. So the useful output of an ownership escalation is not only a decision about this refund feature. It's an entry in the service catalog or ownership map (29.3a) saying which team owns refunds and which interfaces the other team relies on. Once that's written down, the next refund question is a design question within one team, not a dispute between two.

The same applies to shared resources like the database table Trips and School Admin both changed. Shared ownership usually means no ownership. The long-term fix is either a single owner that others request changes from, or splitting the resource so each team owns its own part.

### 7. The decider's obligations

Holding decision rights comes with obligations. A decider who doesn't meet them loses the team's trust in the whole arrangement:

- **Gather the input** from everyone the table says must be consulted, and anyone else with relevant knowledge.
- **Decide within the agreed time.** A decider who doesn't decide creates the same limbo as having no decider at all.
- **Explain the reasoning,** including why the dissenting view didn't prevail (32.4a).
- **Record it** in an ADR (2.5), with the alternatives, the dissent and any revisit triggers.
- **Communicate it** where affected people will see it (32.1a).
- **Revisit when a trigger fires,** without defensiveness.

The last obligation is to **decide with enough information**. Leaders far from the work, like the VP deciding the refund dispute, often lack context. Hearing both sides together, and giving decisions to the people closest to the information where possible (subsidiarity again), is how decisions avoid being reversed a month later.

### 8. Keep the map current

A decision-rights table describes an organization at one moment. Teams split and merge, new kinds of decision appear, and people change roles. Keep it short, owned by someone specific (often an engineering director or principal engineer), and reviewed when the organization changes. Link it from team charters and the "how we communicate" page (32.3b). When a new kind of dispute appears, add a row, rather than handling it from scratch each time.

## When It's Appropriate

- **More than one team,** or any decision that affects several teams. Within a single small team, people usually know who decides.
- **Recurring cross-team disputes,** especially about ownership, shared resources or standards.
- **Groups that advise but can't decide,** like the guild, whose recommendations are followed unevenly.
- **Processes that need everyone's sign-off,** where proposals wait on the slowest approver.
- **Visible HiPPO effects,** where senior comments redirect work unintentionally.
- **After a reorg,** when old understandings of who decides no longer match the structure.
- **Regulated environments** that need clear records of who approved what (the four-eyes controls in 32.2b).

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A single small team** with a clear lead and good relationships. Informal agreement works, and a formal table would be ceremony.
- **Decisions entirely within one team's area.** They don't need a row in the table; the default rule that owners decide covers them.
- **Very early startups,** where everything changes monthly and the founders decide most things anyway. Write the table once there are several teams.
- **Exhaustive matrices.** A RACI chart with a hundred rows won't be read, maintained, or followed. Cover the recurring, cross-boundary decisions and leave the rest to the default.
- **Formal consent or consensus processes for routine choices.** They're valuable for decisions that need broad commitment, and slow for everything else.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity can feel like hierarchy.** Naming one decider tells everyone else they aren't deciding, and some people who felt they had an equal say will feel diminished, even if the old equality was mostly illusory.
- **One decider concentrates risk.** A decider with poor judgment, or poor information, makes poor decisions efficiently. Consultation, recorded reasoning and revisit triggers reduce this risk; they don't remove it.
- **Broad methods are slow.** Consent and rough consensus take facilitation and time. The advice process relies on people actually seeking advice, which erodes under deadline pressure.
- **Veto roles tend to grow.** Once security or legal has an "Agree" veto, more topics tend to get routed through it. Keep vetoes narrow and explicit.
- **Escalation paths depend on the managers.** Joint, early escalation only works if the people receiving escalations respond promptly and fairly. One bad experience teaches people to avoid escalating again.
- **Tables go stale.** A decision-rights map that doesn't match the current organization is worse than none, because people cite it to justify decisions nobody really gave them the right to make.
- **Codified rights can be gamed.** If the process can be satisfied by labeling a decision as the kind you're allowed to make, some people will label decisions that way.

## Alternatives

- **Strong hierarchy.** A technical leader decides all significant cross-team questions. It's fast and clear, but concentrates both workload and judgment in one person, and scales poorly.
- **Architecture review boards.** A standing group with real authority over defined kinds of decision. It gives the guild what it lacked, at the cost of becoming a bottleneck and seeming remote from teams.
- **The advice process everywhere.** Decentralized decisions with required consultation, as described above. It suits high-trust organizations, and works less well where trust or shared context is low.
- **Market-style autonomy.** Teams choose independently, within a small set of supported options (the paved road from 32.4a). It avoids many decisions by making consistency optional, at the cost of divergence.
- **Changing team boundaries.** Many cross-team decisions exist because ownership is fragmented. Reshaping teams so fewer decisions cross boundaries (Conway's law, 2.1) removes decisions instead of structuring them.

## How to Achieve It

1. **Write the table for the disputes you've actually had.** List the last few quarters' cross-team disputes, stalled proposals and escalations, and give each kind of decision one decider, a consulted list and an informed list. A single page is enough. _Next step when:_ people know who decides, but decisions are still slow or contested.
2. **Name the method up front, and adopt labels.** Every proposal says how it will be decided and by when. Senior people label comments as questions, suggestions, recommendations or decisions. Replace sign-off from everyone with consent within a time box. _Next step when:_ decisions happen on time, but disputes between teams still fester or get escalated unilaterally.
3. **Agree on an escalation protocol:** joint escalation documents, triggers based on time and impact, going to the lowest common authority, and an explicit statement from leaders that escalation is welcome. _Next step when:_ escalations work, but the same kinds keep recurring.
4. **Turn recurring escalations into rights and ownership.** Update the table and the ownership map after each new kind of escalation, review the table when the organization changes, and, where useful, enforce parts of it in tooling.

## Implementation

For decisions made in code, GitHub and GitLab already provide decision rights as configuration: a `CODEOWNERS` file combined with branch protection means changes to a path need approval from the owning team. That's the cheapest possible version of the table, and worth using before anything custom.

Decisions recorded as ADRs (2.5) can be checked the same way. Each ADR states what kind of decision it is. A CI step then checks the ADR against the decision-rights table, now stored as data, and checks that an accepted ADR has been approved by someone in the deciding role.

```yaml
---
title: Public API versioning via a date-based header
status: accepted              # proposed | accepted | superseded
scope: public-api
consulted: [integrations-support, product]
revisit_if: "more than two partners can't send custom headers"
---
```

```ts
import { readFile } from "node:fs/promises";
import matter from "gray-matter";
import { Octokit } from "@octokit/rest";

// The decision-rights table, as data. GitHub teams stand in for roles.
const RIGHTS = {
  "public-api":         { decider: "api-owners",        consult: ["integrations-support", "product"] },
  "cross-cutting":      { decider: "principal-eng",     consult: ["tech-leads"] },
  "security-exception": { decider: "security",          consult: [] },
  "team-ownership":     { decider: "eng-directors",     consult: ["tech-leads"] },
} as const;
type Scope = keyof typeof RIGHTS;

const octokit = new Octokit({ auth: process.env.GITHUB_TOKEN });
const [org, repo] = process.env.GITHUB_REPOSITORY!.split("/");
const pull_number = Number(process.env.PR_NUMBER);

async function checkAdr(path: string): Promise<string[]> {
  const { data: adr } = matter(await readFile(path, "utf8"));
  const rule = RIGHTS[adr.scope as Scope];
  if (!rule) return [`${path}: unknown scope "${adr.scope}"; say what kind of decision this is`];

  const problems: string[] = [];
  const consulted = new Set<string>(adr.consulted ?? []);
  for (const group of rule.consult) {
    if (!consulted.has(group)) problems.push(`${path}: ${adr.scope} decisions must consult ${group}`);
  }

  if (adr.status === "accepted") {
    const { data: reviews } = await octokit.pulls.listReviews({ owner: org, repo, pull_number });
    const approvers = new Set(reviews.filter(r => r.state === "APPROVED").map(r => r.user?.login));
    const { data: deciders } = await octokit.teams.listMembersInOrg({ org, team_slug: rule.decider });
    if (!deciders.some(d => approvers.has(d.login))) {
      problems.push(`${path}: accepted ${adr.scope} decisions need approval from @${org}/${rule.decider}`);
    }
  }
  return problems;
}
```

Run it on pull requests that add or change files under `docs/adr/`, and fail the check if any problems are returned. The versioning ADR from the Problem couldn't be merged as "accepted" without an approval from the API owners. Just as important, a guild recommendation couldn't masquerade as an accepted decision.

> **The check enforces the process, not the quality.** It confirms that the right person approved and that the right groups are listed as consulted. It can't tell whether the consultation was real. The `consulted` list is self-declared. What the check does is make skipping consultation a deliberate misstatement rather than an oversight, which is usually enough. Linking each consulted group to where its input was given, such as a comment thread or a meeting note, makes it checkable by humans.

> **The table in code must match the table on the page.** If the organization changes and nobody updates `RIGHTS`, the check will enforce yesterday's structure, and people will work around it. Keep the table in one place, whether that's this file or a page this file reads, give it an owner, and treat a failing check as a prompt to ask "is the rule still right?", not only "who needs to approve?"

> **Leave an exception path.** Urgent decisions sometimes can't wait for the deciding role. Allow a documented override (for example, an `emergency: true` field that requires a follow-up approval within a week) rather than letting people disable the check.

## Further Reading

- **Paul Rogers and Marcia Blenko, "Who Has the D? How Clear Decision Roles Enhance Organizational Performance"** (_Harvard Business Review_, 2006). The RAPID framework and the case for one decider.
- **Atlassian Team Playbook, "DACI: A decision-making framework."** A practical template for running a decision with named roles.
- **Pete Resnick, RFC 7282, "On Consensus and Humming in the IETF"** (2014). A short, careful explanation of rough consensus: why objections matter more than agreement, and why counting isn't the point.
- **Andrew Harmel-Law, "Scaling the Practice of Architecture, Conversationally"** (martinfowler.com, 2021). The advice process applied to software architecture, with decision records and an advice forum instead of a central architecture group.
- **Sociocracy 3.0** (sociocracy30.org). Consent-based decision-making, including how to distinguish objections from preferences.
- **Andy Grove, _High Output Management_** (1983). The chapter on decision-making: free discussion, a clear decision, full support, and bringing in the "peer-plus-one" when peers can't agree.
- **Victor H. Vroom and Philip W. Yetton, _Leadership and Decision-Making_** (1973). The original model for choosing how much to involve people in a decision.
- **Jurgen Appelo, _Management 3.0_** (2011). The seven levels of delegation, and the "delegation poker" exercise for agreeing them.
- **GitHub Docs, "About code owners."** Decision rights for code, enforced by the platform.

It's usually a missing piece of structure: no ending (32.1a), no job for the meeting (32.2a), no shared hours (32.3a), no safe way to say "I'm stuck" (32.3b), no shared question (32.4a), or no one who decides (here). Learn to notice which piece is missing, and you can usually fix the group without replacing anyone in it.
