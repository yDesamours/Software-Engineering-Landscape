# Written by Default: Asynchronous Communication That Closes Its Loops

_Module 32 · Communication & Team Dynamics · 32.1a_

## The Problem

Suppose you work on software for independent gyms: memberships, class bookings, card payments and direct debits, and integrations with the door-access hardware at the gym entrance. There are 13 engineers in two squads, a product manager, a designer and a support lead. Most sit in one office; four work remotely, all within an hour's time difference. Nobody would call this a distributed team. Over one quarter, four things go wrong.

**The call that decided two different things.** On a Tuesday, five people on a call agree to "sunset the v1 door-access API by the end of June." Two of them leave believing it means no _new_ gyms on v1 from June. Three believe v1 is _switched off_ in June. Nothing is written down. The two Access-squad engineers who weren't on the call spend the next three weeks building a v1 feature for a large chain, because nobody told them. Six weeks later, the argument about what was actually decided takes longer than the original call.

**"hey."** The tech lead, Priya, counts her messages for one week. She gets about 63 direct messages and mentions a day, and a third of them open with "hey" or "quick q" and then wait for her to reply before saying anything. The team's median reply time to her is four minutes, and people are quietly proud of how responsive everyone is. Her longest uninterrupted stretch that week is 35 minutes. The booking-engine refactor she owns slips by a month. The same question, where to find the staging database credentials, reaches her by DM nine times that quarter, from nine different people.

**The design doc that died of comments.** An engineer proposes replacing the nightly billing batch with billing each member on their own renewal date. He posts the doc with "thoughts welcome." Over five weeks it collects 112 comments. Two separate threads argue the same question about retries without either noticing the other. The head of finance, whose month-end reporting depends on the batch, never sees the doc. Nobody knows who is supposed to decide, so nobody does. The doc goes quiet, and its tired author builds half of it on a branch anyway.

**Green, green, green, red.** Each squad sends leadership a weekly status line. For eight weeks the payments migration reads "on track 🟢." The engineer doing the work has known for three weeks that the payment provider's sandbox can't create direct-debit mandates, so the direct-debit flow can't be tested end to end. The template has one line and a colour, and reporting red feels like confessing. The problem surfaces a week before launch, and a partner gym chain's launch slips with it.

After a painful retrospective, the head of engineering declares the team "async first": no meetings except the daily standup, everything in writing. Within a month the opposite failures appear. A question about a feature-flag name, which needed two sentences each way, takes two days across three threads. A disagreement about on-call pay escalates in a document's comment thread into something personal. Forty new documents are written; most get no views after their first week.

The team's problem was never that it was synchronous or asynchronous. Each message went through whichever channel was closest to hand, and nobody asked what the message needed to do: who needs it, how soon, how long it has to survive, and how it ends.

## What It Is

**Asynchronous communication** is any exchange where the sender and the receiver don't need to be present at the same time: a written proposal, a thread, a status update, a ticket comment, a posted decision. **Async-first** (or "written by default") is the practice of making durable, self-contained writing the default for anything other people will need later or can respond to on their own schedule, and choosing live conversation deliberately for what it does well. The word that matters is _default_, not _only_. The overcorrection at the end of the Problem is what happens when it becomes "only."

A neighbor worth separating: **documentation** (29.3a and 31.4) describes the system and has to stay true as the system changes. Async communication moves work between people: questions, proposals, decisions, status. Most of it is meant to expire. The two meet at the moment of decision. The conversation that reaches a decision is communication; the record it leaves behind, an ADR (2.5) or a decision-log entry, is documentation. This article is about the conversation and how it ends; where the record lives is covered in 29.3a.

(And "async" here has nothing to do with `async`/`await`, though the analogy to a callback nobody ever invokes turns out to be closer than it sounds.)

## Recognizing the Problem

Signs you're paying for having no deliberate approach:

- "What did we decide about X?" gets different answers from different people.
- The same question reaches the same person by DM more than a couple of times a month.
- Someone back from a week off needs a full day to catch up, or catches up by asking around.
- Proposals stay "open" for weeks and are never rejected, only abandoned.
- Status meetings are mostly people reading updates aloud.
- The person who owns the hardest technical work rarely gets an hour without interruption.
- Status reports go from green straight to red, with no amber in between.
- Decisions are made by whoever happened to be in the room, and part-time, remote, or out-of-office people find out afterwards.

Signs you've overcorrected:

- Simple yes/no questions wait a day or more.
- Disagreements harden in comment threads instead of resolving.
- The number of documents grows while nobody reads them.

## How It Works

### 1. Choose the channel from the message

Before sending anything that matters, four questions decide the channel:

- **How soon** does it need a response?
- **How much back-and-forth** will it take to converge? A factual question converges in one exchange; a design disagreement might take ten.
- **Who needs it later**, and how long must it survive?
- **How much emotional weight** does it carry?

The first two decide sync versus async; the third decides whether it must be written at all:

```
                          needs to be found later?
                          no                     yes
                   ┌──────────────────────┬───────────────────────────┐
  converges in one │ chat thread          │ written: doc, ticket,     │
  or two exchanges │ (answer, move on)    │ posted decision           │
                   ├──────────────────────┼───────────────────────────┤
  needs real       │ call                 │ call, THEN write it down  │
  back-and-forth   │                      │ (what was decided and why)│
                   └──────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┘

  urgent (production down, customer blocked) → interrupt: page or phone,
                                               never "URGENT" in a chat
  emotionally loaded (feedback, conflict)   → talk first, write the agreement after
```

Each failure from the Problem is a message in the wrong cell. The door-access call was a bottom-right conversation handled as bottom-left: it converged live and was never written down. The billing doc was a bottom-right conversation handled as top-right: it needed a live conversation to resolve the retry question and never got one. The "hey" messages were top-left questions delivered as interruptions.

### 2. Write messages that need no reply to be understood

Synchronous conversation is cheap per round trip; you can ask "which job?" and hear back in two seconds. Asynchronously, each round trip might cost hours. So an async message should aim to need **zero follow-up questions** before someone can act on it. Put the ask first (sometimes called BLUF, "bottom line up front"), then the context, what you've already tried, what shape of answer you need, and by when.

Before:

> hey
> _(4 minutes later)_ quick q about the renewal job
> _(9 minutes later)_ is it safe to rerun?

After:

> Is it safe to rerun the renewal job for gym 2231 today? It failed at 03:10 on a provider timeout after charging about 40 of 310 members ([log](#)). I think the idempotency keys cover a rerun, but I'm not sure they survive a worker restart. I need an answer by 14:00 to charge before the gym's direct-debit cutoff. If nobody's sure by then, I'll rerun only the 270 members with no charge attempt.

The second version takes a minute longer to write and saves three round trips. It also contains a **fallback**, which makes silence safe: if nobody answers, the work still moves. And it's posted in `#billing`, not in a DM, so the next person with the same question finds it. Defaulting questions to a team channel is how the staging-credentials question gets answered once instead of nine times. Keep DMs for things that really are personal.

### 3. Every ask has an owner, a deadline, and a default

The characteristic failure of async communication isn't disagreement; it's that nothing ends. A synchronous meeting has a built-in ending, because it's over at 15:00. A thread or a doc has none unless you give it one. Three things make an ending explicit:

- **Who decides.** One named person, not "the team."
- **By when.** A date, not "thoughts welcome."
- **What happens on silence.** "I'll go ahead with option B on Thursday unless someone objects."

The last one is **lazy consensus**, the decision rule the Apache Software Foundation's projects run on: a proposal passes unless someone objects within the stated time. It works well for reversible, low-stakes decisions in teams where everyone knows silence counts as consent. It fails in two situations. The first is irreversible decisions, where silence is too weak a signal. The second is when the affected people aren't reading, like the head of finance who never saw the billing doc. For those, name the people who must actively approve ("@finance-lead needs to sign off"), and don't treat their silence as consent.

It also matters to separate the people being **consulted** from the person **deciding**. Comments are input, not votes. The decider can go against the majority of comments, but should say why. What to do when consulted people disagree strongly, and how decision rights get assigned in the first place, is the subject of 32.4.

### 4. Give proposals a lifecycle

For decisions that cross team boundaries or are hard to reverse, a written proposal is the standard tool. Companies call it an RFC, a design doc, or (at Oxide) an RFD. What makes it work isn't the template; it's the lifecycle:

```
 draft ──────▶ open for comment ──────▶ decided ──────▶ recorded
 (author)      fixed window,            named decider   ADR / decision log,
               named reviewers          writes outcome  linked from the work
                    │
                    │ a thread goes two rounds without converging
                    ▼
               short call ──▶ outcome written back into the doc
```

A few rules carry most of the weight:

- **A fixed comment window**, stated at the top: "open until Wed 13 May." Rust's RFC process uses a "final comment period" in the same way: a last, announced window for objections before a decision.
- **Named reviewers**, including anyone whose work the change affects outside engineering.
- **Comments get resolved**: accepted, rejected with a reason, or explicitly deferred. An unresolved comment is an open loop.
- **An escalation rule**: when a thread has gone back and forth twice without converging, the people in it get on a call, and the outcome is written back into the doc. This is where async stops being the right tool (cell bottom-right in the grid), and the rule prevents the 112-comment spiral.
- **The outcome is announced where affected people are**, not only edited into the doc.

The document itself should be shorter than people expect: the problem, the constraints, the options (including doing nothing), a recommendation, what would change the recommendation, and the open questions. Two to four pages is typical. Amazon's practice of starting meetings with a narrative memo of at most six pages is the best-known version of this discipline.

Not every decision deserves this. Jeff Bezos's distinction between **one-way doors** (hard to undo) and **two-way doors** (easy to undo) is a good sizing rule. A new lint rule is a two-way door and needs a message with a default, not a proposal. Deprecating a public API that customers' hardware depends on is a one-way door.

### 5. Synchronous decisions still end in writing

Calls are often the fastest way to converge. The rule isn't "don't decide in meetings"; it's that **a decision made live isn't finished until it's posted**, within a day, where the people who weren't there will see it. A short fixed shape is enough:

```
Door-access API v1 — decided Tue 12 May (call: Priya, Tom, Anja, Kev, Sam)

Decided:
- From 1 June, no new gyms are onboarded to v1.
- Existing v1 gyms keep working at least until 31 December.
NOT decided:
- The v1 switch-off date. Anja will propose one by 5 June.
Why: v2 covers every v1 feature except PIN pads; 38 gyms still use PIN pads.
Affects: Access squad (no new v1 features), onboarding, support macros.
Objections: reply in this thread by Fri 15 May.
```

The "NOT decided" line is doing real work. It's exactly the point the five people on the original call understood differently. Writing a decision down is what exposes that there were two decisions, and that only one of them was made. The two engineers who weren't on the call would have seen this post the next morning and not started the v1 feature.

Where the record lives afterwards (an ADR for architecturally significant choices, per 2.5, or a lighter decision log for product and process choices) is covered in 29.3a.

### 6. Write status for the reader's decision

A status update exists because someone has to decide something: whether to worry, move a date, add help, or warn a customer. A colour and "on track" gives them nothing to decide with. A useful update answers four questions: what changed since last time, how confident you are in the date and why, what risks exist and what would trigger a change, and what you need.

```
Payments migration — week 6 of 9. Target: 24 June. Confidence: medium (was high).

Changed: card payment flows done and tested against the provider sandbox.
Risk:    the sandbox can't create direct-debit mandates, so we can't test
         direct debit end to end. If the provider can't enable it by 29 May,
         direct-debit gyms slip about two weeks; card-only gyms are unaffected.
Need:    someone with the provider relationship to push our support ticket (link).
```

Amber has to be a normal, unremarkable state, not an admission. Reports that stay green outside while the work is red inside are common enough to have a name, "watermelon status," and they are nearly always a problem of incentives rather than honesty. If reporting amber gets someone questioned in front of leadership, they'll stop reporting it. Early bad news has to be treated as useful, because it's the only kind of bad news there's still time to act on.

Once updates are written this way, a status meeting can be replaced with the written updates plus a short meeting only about the ambers, which is where the discussion is actually needed.

### 7. Agree on response times

Async only works if people know when replies will come. Otherwise everyone watches every channel continuously, which is synchronous communication with worse tools. A team working agreement makes the expectations explicit, for example:

| Channel | Expected response |
|---|---|
| Chat mention in a team channel | Same working day |
| Pull request review request | Within one working day (29.2) |
| Proposal comment window | Whatever the proposal states |
| Production issue, blocked customer | Page or phone on-call; never chat |

This is what lets Priya close chat for three hours in the morning. The norm must actually be honoured, though. If the people who reply within four minutes are the ones praised in reviews, the written agreement says one thing and the real incentive says another, and the real incentive wins.

## When It's Appropriate

- **More than about eight people, or more than one team.** A hallway reaches the people nearby; it doesn't reach a second squad. In the door-access case, 5 of 16 people heard the decision.
- **Anyone works different hours.** Part-time staff, parents with fixed pick-up times, people in other time zones (32.3). Every decision made in a room excludes them by default.
- **Roles that depend on long focus.** If someone's work needs two-hour blocks, and the typical interruption rate gives them 35 minutes, the lost output is large and invisible.
- **Decisions with long lives.** API deprecations, data retention, pricing rules. Someone will ask "why did we do this?" in a year, and the answer shouldn't depend on who still works there.
- **Decisions that affect people outside engineering's meetings**, like finance and billing.
- **Regulated work** where an auditor may ask who approved a change and on what basis.
- **Frequent handovers**: on-call rotations, shifting squad membership, people covering for each other's leave.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Three to five people working closely together.** Overhearing works, feedback is instant, and writing everything down is overhead. Write down only the decisions that will outlive the week.
- **Incidents.** Incident response is synchronous by nature: a call, an incident channel, and a scribe (24.3). The written timeline is a by-product, not the medium.
- **Early exploration.** When an idea is still fragile, fast talk beats a document. A doc written too early can freeze a half-formed idea into a position people then defend.
- **Anything personal or emotionally loaded.** Feedback, conflict, compensation, performance. Text strips tone and gives people time to compose a harder reply. The on-call-pay thread should have been a conversation, with the agreement written up afterwards.
- **Small, reversible decisions.** A proposal process for a lint rule is process theater. Size the process to reversibility and blast radius.
- **"No meetings" as a policy.** It produces exactly the overcorrection in the Problem: slower answers to simple questions, and conflict moved into the worst possible medium.

## Trade-offs

- **Latency.** Each async round trip can cost hours. A decision that needs five exchanges takes a week in writing and fifteen minutes on a call. Self-contained messages and the escalation rule reduce this, but it never goes away.
- **The cost moves to the writer.** A good async message takes ten minutes instead of ten seconds. That's a good trade when the message has many readers or a long life, and a bad one otherwise.
- **Tone gets lost, and disagreement hardens.** Written disagreement reads harsher than it was meant, and people defend positions they've written down more than ones they've said aloud.
- **It shifts who gets heard.** Confident, fluent writers gain influence, and people writing in a second language can lose it. At the same time, people who don't speak up in meetings often contribute more in writing. The net effect depends on the team, and it should be watched rather than assumed.
- **More things to find.** More writing means more to search, link and eventually archive (29.3a).
- **Lazy consensus can railroad.** When people are overloaded, silence isn't agreement; it's absence. Decisions can pass that nobody affected actually read.
- **Weaker relationships.** Fewer casual conversations mean less rapport, which makes written disagreement harsher still. This loop is one of the main subjects of 32.3.
- **Performative writing.** Once writing is visible and valued, some of it will be written to look busy rather than to inform.

## Alternatives

- **Synchronous by default, with a note-taker.** Meetings for everything, but every meeting produces written decisions. This works for small co-located teams. The costs are calendar load and excluding whoever wasn't there.
- **Co-location and overhearing.** Very effective within one room. It doesn't reach a second room.
- **Recorded video walkthroughs.** Asynchronous and good at carrying tone or showing a demo, but not skimmable, searchable, or commentable line by line. A good complement for demos; a poor medium for decisions.
- **The tracker as the channel.** All discussion happens in tickets. This keeps context next to the work, but works badly for decisions that cut across many tickets.
- **Standups and team rituals** (28.2a). Synchronous status is fine when the audience is the team itself. Written status matters when the audience is outside it.

## How to Achieve It

Each step fixes one visible pain and makes the next one visible.

1. **Post every live decision in writing within a day**, in a fixed shape (decided, not decided, why, affects, how to object). This costs almost nothing and fixes the most expensive failure. _Next step when:_ "what did we decide?" questions become rare, and the remaining complaint is interruptions.
2. **Write a working agreement**: which channel for what, expected response times, questions in team channels rather than DMs, a real urgency path, messages that stand on their own. _Next step when:_ interruptions drop, but decisions that cross teams still stall or get abandoned.
3. **Give cross-team and hard-to-reverse proposals a lifecycle**: a named decider, a comment window, required reviewers, and a call after two rounds without convergence. _Next step when:_ proposals end reliably, but leadership is still surprised by slipping dates.
4. **Replace status reporting with written updates** that state confidence and risk, and turn status meetings into short meetings about the ambers only.

Watch for the signal that you've gone too far: trivial questions waiting days, documents with no readers, disagreements that fester in threads. The response isn't to abandon the practice but to move those specific conversations back to calls.

## Implementation

Most of this is habit and agreement rather than tooling. One piece benefits from automation: making sure proposals actually end. If proposals live in a repository as Markdown with a little front matter, a weekly job can find the open loops. The same check works against a wiki's or document tool's API, as long as each proposal records an owner, a date and a state.

```yaml
---
title: Bill each member on their renewal date
status: open            # draft | open | decided | withdrawn
decider: priya
required: [finance-lead, billing-squad]
opened: 2026-05-06
decide_by: 2026-05-13
---
```

```ts
import { readdir, readFile } from "node:fs/promises";
import matter from "gray-matter";

type Proposal = {
  title: string;
  status: "draft" | "open" | "decided" | "withdrawn";
  decider?: string;
  decide_by?: Date; // gray-matter parses YAML dates into Date objects
};

async function findOpenLoops(dir: string, today = new Date()): Promise<string[]> {
  const problems: string[] = [];

  for (const file of await readdir(dir)) {
    if (!file.endsWith(".md")) continue;
    const { data, content } = matter(await readFile(`${dir}/${file}`, "utf8"));
    const p = data as Proposal;

    if (p.status === "open" && !p.decider)
      problems.push(`${p.title}: open, but nobody is named to decide`);
    if (p.status === "open" && p.decide_by && p.decide_by < today)
      problems.push(`${p.title}: decision was due ${p.decide_by.toDateString()}`);
    if (p.status === "decided" && !/^## Decision/m.test(content))
      problems.push(`${p.title}: marked decided, but the outcome isn't written down`);
  }
  return problems;
}

const problems = await findOpenLoops("proposals");
if (problems.length > 0) {
  await fetch(process.env.SLACK_WEBHOOK_URL!, {
    method: "POST",
    headers: { "Content-Type": "application/json" },
    body: JSON.stringify({ text: `Proposals that need an ending:\n• ${problems.join("\n• ")}` }),
  });
}
```

Run it on a weekly schedule in CI and post to a shared channel. Two asides on what this does and doesn't do:

> **It checks that loops close, not that decisions are good.** A proposal can be "decided" badly and still pass. The check exists to catch the billing-doc failure, where nothing was ever decided at all, which is the failure that's otherwise invisible.

> **Name proposals, not people.** The message lists titles, not whose proposals are late. Turning this into a per-person scoreboard invites the same gaming 31.1 describes for code metrics: people will mark things decided to clear the list. And if half the proposals are always overdue, the fault is usually the process, not the people. Either the windows are too short, or proposals are being used for two-way-door decisions that didn't need one.

## Further Reading

- **GitLab Handbook, "Asynchronous communication" and "All-remote" sections** (handbook.gitlab.com). The most detailed public description of a company running on async-first, including where they deliberately choose synchronous communication.
- **Cal Newport, _A World Without Email_** (2021). Names the "hyperactive hive mind," the constant-reply workflow in the "hey" example, and argues for structured processes instead.
- **Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, _It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work_** (2018). Basecamp's case for calm, written, low-interruption work, including its costs.
- **Apache Software Foundation, "Lazy consensus"** (community.apache.org). The decision rule in its original setting, with the conditions under which it works.
- **Oxide Computer Company, "RFD 1: Requests for Discussion"** (rfd.shared.oxide.computer). A proposal process described in detail, including states and when to write one.
- **The Rust RFC process** (github.com/rust-lang/rfcs). Final comment periods and explicit decision-making teams, at the scale of a large open-source project.
- **Malte Ubl, "Design Docs at Google"** (industrialempathy.com). What a design doc contains, and when it isn't worth writing one.
- **Jeff Bezos, Amazon 2015 and 2017 shareholder letters.** The one-way/two-way door distinction (2015) and the six-page narrative memo (2017).
- **Barbara Minto, _The Pyramid Principle_.** The origin of "answer first, then support it," the structure behind self-contained messages.
- **nohello.net.** A one-page explanation of why "hey" followed by silence costs the reader a round trip.
- **Michael Nygard, "Documenting Architecture Decisions"** (2011), for the record side of decisions. See also 2.5 and 29.3a.

No sibling article is proposed: nothing here depends on a language.

---

The habit to take from this: before you send anything that matters, ask two questions. _How does this end?_ and _Who will need it after it ends?_ If you can't answer the first, you're starting a loop nobody will close. If the answer to the second is "somebody," write it where they'll find it.
