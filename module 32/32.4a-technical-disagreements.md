# Technical Disagreements: Arguing About the Right Thing

_Module 32 · Communication & Team Dynamics · 32.4a_

## The Problem

Suppose you work on a marketplace for secondhand furniture: listings, search, buyer–seller messaging, payments held in escrow until delivery, and a delivery-quote service that calls several courier APIs and calculates routes. There are 14 engineers. They're capable and they care, and over two quarters they have five disagreements that go badly.

**Three weeks of database arguments.** A new listings service is being built. One group wants MongoDB, because listing attributes vary a lot by category: a sofa has fabric and seat count, a wardrobe has door count and hanging depth. The other group wants PostgreSQL, because listings interact with escrow payments and reporting. The discussion runs across two Slack channels and a design doc for three weeks, with each side restating its case more forcefully. It's finally settled by a two-day experiment. PostgreSQL's JSONB columns handle the varying attributes, with indexed category filters running well within the target at a million listings. In hindsight, the argument was never about databases. It was about two unstated questions: how varied the attributes would really become, and whether listings needed to share transactions with payments. Both were answerable in days.

**Ninety comments on a naming convention.** A PR introducing a convention for React hook names collects ninety comments in a week, more than the escrow design review received.

**The rewrite decided by seniority.** Dev, a senior engineer, argues for rewriting the delivery-quote service from Node.js in Go, because route calculation is CPU-heavy and slow. Sam and two others argue against. The discussion stays on performance, where Dev is most confident. After a tense meeting, the tech lead sides with Dev, the most senior person in the room. Sam's group stops arguing and disengages. Every time the rewrite hits a problem, someone says "as we said." The decision is relitigated in three retrospectives in a row.

**Commit, before anyone disagreed.** In a planning meeting about moving delivery booking to a new courier's API, an engineer starts raising a concern about the courier's rate limits. The manager cuts in: "Let's disagree and commit, we need to move." The rate limits cause a day of failed bookings during the launch.

**The consensus nobody believed.** The design for event-driven delivery booking passes review with no objections. Afterwards, it turns out at least four reviewers privately doubted it: two worried about ordering, one about debugging, one thought it was over-engineered. Each assumed the others were convinced. The design struggles in production exactly where the doubts were.

The team's problem wasn't that it disagreed. Disagreement is how a team finds out what it doesn't know. The problem was _how_ it disagreed. It argued positions instead of the reasons behind them. It had no shared criteria for judging options, and no way of settling factual questions with evidence. The size of each argument had nothing to do with its stakes, and nothing marked an ending people could commit to. And it had no reliable way to hear doubts people didn't volunteer.

## What It Is

Resolving a technical disagreement well means turning a conflict between **positions** ("MongoDB," "Go") into questions that can actually be answered. Some are answered by **evidence**, some by **explicit priorities**, and some by **a named decider**. Then the disagreement is closed in a way people can commit to, even if they still disagree.

Three neighbors:

- **Decision rights, escalation and consensus** (32.4b) cover _who_ decides when a disagreement doesn't converge, and how that's set up. This article is about making the disagreement productive before and while that happens.
- **Architecture Decision Records** (2.5) are where the result, and the dissent, are written down.
- **Interpersonal conflict**, meaning personality clashes or a strained working relationship, is a management problem. Technical and personal disagreements often feed each other, and some of the techniques here help with both. But a framework won't fix a relationship that's broken.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The same technical argument recurs across weeks, channels or retrospectives.
- People restate their positions more forcefully rather than adding new information.
- Nobody can say what evidence would settle the question.
- Trivial topics get more debate than consequential ones.
- Disputes are settled by who's most senior, most persistent or most confident.
- People go quiet in discussions and complain afterwards.
- Decisions are reopened every time something goes wrong.
- Reviews pass without objections, and doubts surface only after problems appear.
- "Disagree and commit" is said more often by managers than by people who disagree.

## How It Works

### 1. Find out what kind of disagreement it is

Most technical disagreements are really about one of four things, and each is resolved differently:

| Layer | Sounds like | Resolved by |
|---|---|---|
| **Goals** | "We need to ship by March" vs "We need this to last five years" | The person who owns the goal: product, leadership, the roadmap |
| **Facts and predictions** | "JSONB will be too slow" vs "It'll be fine" | Evidence: a spike, a benchmark, production data |
| **Priorities** | "Performance matters most" vs "Team familiarity matters most" | Making the weights explicit, then a decider (32.4b) |
| **Taste** | "Hooks should be named `useX`" vs "`withX`" | Picking one and moving on: a formatter, an owner, or a coin |

Most bad arguments come from treating one layer as another. The database debate was mainly about facts and predictions (how slow, how varied), argued as if it were about taste or principle. The naming debate was taste, argued as if it were principle. The Go rewrite mixed all four. The Implementation section shows how to separate them.

A useful habit: early in any disagreement, ask "is this about goals, facts, priorities or taste?" Just asking the question changes the argument.

### 2. Ask for interests, not positions

Roger Fisher and William Ury's _Getting to Yes_ distinguishes a **position** (what someone says they want) from an **interest** (why they want it). "MongoDB" is a position. "I don't want a schema migration every time we add a furniture category" is an interest. "PostgreSQL" is a position. "Escrow and listings must change together atomically, and finance needs SQL reporting" is an interest.

Positions often conflict when interests don't. Here, all four interests were satisfied by one option neither side had proposed. The simplest question that surfaces interests is: **"What problem does your option solve for you?"**

### 3. Agree on criteria before comparing options

When options are compared before criteria are agreed, each side argues from the criteria that favor its option. That's why the rewrite debate stayed on performance: it was the criterion where Go looked best. Fixing it takes a short step before any comparison:

1. **Hard constraints.** Anything an option must satisfy, or it's out: "escrow and listing state change atomically."
2. **Criteria to weigh.** Performance, team familiarity, migration cost, operational fit, hiring.
3. **Only then, score the options.**

Two things happen. Some options fail a hard constraint and the argument ends there. And for the rest, it becomes visible where people actually disagree: on the scores, which are facts, or on the weights, which are priorities.

### 4. Find the crux: what would change your mind?

For each person, ask: **"What would you need to see to change your mind?"** The answer tells you what kind of disagreement you have:

- **A specific fact** ("if category filters ran under 50 ms at a million listings, I'd accept Postgres"). Go and get that fact.
- **A change in priorities** ("if the roadmap gave us three spare months, I'd accept the rewrite"). Take it to whoever owns the priority.
- **Nothing.** Then it's a matter of taste, or someone isn't really open. Either way, stop debating facts and move to a decision.

The Center for Applied Rationality called a structured version of this **double crux**: both people look for a fact that would change _both_ their minds, and investigate that together. Even without the formality, the question alone often finds the real issue in minutes. In the database debate, it would have surfaced the two answerable questions in the first week.

### 5. Settle facts with evidence, and agree on the rule first

When the crux is a fact, get evidence rather than arguing. There are several kinds, depending on the question:

- **A spike:** a short, time-boxed, throwaway experiment that answers one specific question (the term comes from Extreme Programming). It's how the database question was settled.
- **A benchmark** with realistic data and load (22.5), not a microbenchmark that flatters one side.
- **Production data** about how the current system actually behaves.
- **A reversible experiment**, such as a feature flag (30.2b) or a partial rollout, where the real system provides the answer.
- **Prior art:** how other teams, or the wider industry, have fared with each option.

The crucial step comes _before_ the spike: **agree on the question and the decision rule in advance.** For example: "If JSONB category filters run under 50 ms at the 95th percentile with a million listings, we use PostgreSQL. If not, we look at a document store." Without a rule agreed upfront, both sides reinterpret the result to fit their existing view: "50 ms is fine" against "but 50 ms under _real_ load won't be." With a rule, the result settles the matter.

### 6. Size the argument to the stakes

Some arguments deserve three weeks; most don't. Two things set the right size. The first is **reversibility**, the one-way and two-way doors from 32.1a. The second is **cost of being wrong**: how much work, risk or customer impact a wrong choice would cause.

**Time-box the rest.** Say "we'll discuss until Friday; if we haven't converged, Ana decides." Name the fallback decider up front (32.4b), so the time box has a real ending.

Beware of **bikeshedding.** C. Northcote Parkinson's "law of triviality" observes that a committee will spend more time on a bike shed than on a nuclear reactor, because everyone understands bike sheds well enough to have an opinion. The naming-convention PR is a bike shed; the escrow design is the reactor. For taste-level decisions, the best move is usually to take them out of human debate:

- **Automate them.** A formatter or lint rule (31.2) ends the discussion once, for everyone.
- **Let the owner decide.** Whoever owns the code or the area picks.
- **Value consistency over the optimum.** "Either is fine; we'll do what the existing code does."

### 7. Commit, properly

"Disagree and commit" is useful when it describes the end of a real process, and corrosive when it's used to skip one. Done properly, it has four parts:

1. **Every view has been heard,** including the concern someone was about to raise. The rate-limit concern wasn't heard; that wasn't disagree-and-commit, it was an interruption.
2. **The right person decides** (32.4b) and explains the reasoning, including why the dissent didn't prevail.
3. **The dissent is recorded,** in the ADR's alternatives and consequences, with the dissenter's reasoning in their own words if they want.
4. **Everyone commits to making it succeed,** dissenters included. That means working on it in good faith, not waiting for it to fail.

Two additions stop the endless relitigating that followed the rewrite decision:

- **Revisit triggers.** Agree in advance what would reopen the decision: "if the rewrite isn't handling production traffic by the end of Q3," or "if p95 route calculation in Node is still above 800 ms after the caching change." Until a trigger fires or genuinely new information appears, the decision stands, and the retro isn't the place to reopen it.
- **No "I told you so."** If a trigger fires, the dissent was valuable information, and recording it made it usable. Gloating turns the next dissenter silent.

### 8. Surface the disagreement people don't voice

The event-driven design passed review because nobody objected, and nobody objected because each person assumed everyone else was convinced. Social psychologists call this **pluralistic ignorance**. Jerry Harvey's **Abilene paradox** is its best-known illustration: a family takes a trip none of them wanted, because each thought the others did. Quiet reviews aren't evidence of agreement. A few techniques make doubts visible:

- **Premortem.** Proposed by Gary Klein: before committing, everyone imagines it's six months later and the project has failed, then writes down why. Because failure is taken as given, doubts become contributions instead of objections. Do this in writing first, then share.
- **Silent writing before discussion** (32.2a), so each person's concerns are captured before anyone else's view anchors them.
- **An assigned critic.** Rotate a "red team" role in design reviews, whose job is to find the strongest objection. It separates the objection from the person, so raising it costs nothing socially.
- **Seniors speak last** (32.2a).

Healthy disagreement depends on psychological safety (32.3b). A team that argues openly, and where people change their minds without losing face, is usually in better shape than one that never argues.

### 9. Keep the argument about the idea

A few habits help keep technical arguments from becoming personal:

- **Restate the other side first.** Anatol Rapoport's rule, popularized by Daniel Dennett, is to restate the other person's position so well that they say "I wish I'd put it that way," before criticizing it. It proves you've understood, and it often shows you didn't.
- **Talk about the option, not the person.** "This design has an ordering problem" rather than "you haven't thought about ordering."
- **Say how confident you are.** "I'm fairly sure, from the last time we did this" and "this is a hunch" are both useful to hear, and they're different.
- **Write both sides in one document.** Two options side by side, preferably written by both advocates or by someone neutral, is easier to compare than a thread of rebuttals. But written threads harden positions (32.1a). After two rounds without progress, get on a call.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Decisions with long-lived consequences:** data stores, service boundaries, languages, public APIs.
- **Arguments that keep recurring,** or that have lasted more than a week without convergence.
- **Teams where seniority or confidence reliably wins,** and quieter people disengage.
- **Cross-team decisions,** where people don't share context and assume bad faith more easily.
- **Designs that pass review too easily** for their size.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Small, reversible decisions.** Pick one, move on, and change it later if it's wrong.
- **Taste.** Automate it or give it to an owner. Criteria, cruxes and spikes for a naming convention would be a bike shed of their own.
- **Incidents and emergencies.** The incident commander decides (24.3). Debate happens in the review afterwards.
- **One clear owner.** If the decision plainly belongs to one person or team and they have the context, input is welcome but a structured process isn't needed.
- **When the conflict is really personal.** If two people disagree about everything, the problem is probably the relationship, and it belongs with their manager, not in a decision matrix.

## Trade-offs

- **Time.** Criteria, cruxes and spikes take time upfront. For large decisions this is far less than three weeks of circular argument; for small ones it's more than the decision is worth.
- **Spikes cost days, and can survive.** A spike's code has a way of ending up in production. Keep spikes on a separate branch and delete them afterwards.
- **Criteria can be gamed.** People propose criteria, or weights, that favor the option they already want. Agreeing on criteria before options are named helps; it doesn't make anyone neutral.
- **Compromise can be worse than either option.** Merging two positions to keep both sides happy can produce the worst of each: half the listings in one database, half in the other. Sometimes one side has to lose.
- **Disagree-and-commit can suppress.** Even done well, it asks dissenters to put effort into something they doubt. If it's used too often, or only in one direction, people stop dissenting at all.
- **Surfacing dissent takes energy.** Premortems and assigned critics can feel negative, and teams can overdo them, questioning everything.
- **Revisit triggers need tracking.** A trigger nobody checks doesn't prevent relitigating; it just delays it.

## Alternatives

- **Authority decides.** A tech lead or architect settles technical disputes. It's fast and clear, but depends heavily on that person's judgment, and can silence people when it's the only mechanism.
- **Voting.** Quick and feels fair, but lets the majority overrule expertise, and the minority has no reason to commit.
- **Consensus or consent.** Decide only when everyone agrees, or when nobody has a reasoned objection. Covered in 32.4b.
- **Defer, or build both.** Put an interface in front and postpone the choice. This preserves options, at the cost of building and maintaining an abstraction that may never be needed.
- **Let teams choose within guardrails.** Where consistency doesn't matter much, let each team decide for its own area, within a small set of supported options, sometimes called a paved road.

## How to Achieve It

1. **Change the design-doc template.** Add a section for hard constraints and criteria before options, and ask each option's advocate to answer "what would change your mind?" It costs nothing and reshapes most arguments. _Next step when:_ arguments are clearer, but factual questions still drag on.
2. **Use spikes with decision rules agreed in advance,** and time-box discussions with a named fallback decider. _Next step when:_ decisions get made, but they're reopened whenever something goes wrong.
3. **Record dissent and revisit triggers in ADRs,** and treat reopening a decision as something that needs a trigger or new information. _Next step when:_ decisions stick, but they're mostly shaped by the loudest voices, or reviews pass too easily.
4. **Add premortems for large decisions,** silent writing in design reviews, and a rotating critic. Move taste decisions to formatters and owners.

## Implementation

Most of this is conversation, but one step benefits from a little arithmetic: working out _where_ two people actually disagree. In the rewrite debate, suppose the team first agreed on scores for each option, drawing on a spike and a benchmark, and then asked Dev and Sam each to weight the criteria. The scores are facts; the weights are priorities. With both written down, a few lines of TypeScript can show which criterion's weighting drives the disagreement.

```ts
type Criterion = "throughput" | "familiarity" | "migrationCost" | "opsFit" | "hiring";
type Weights = Record<Criterion, number>;

// Agreed scores, 1–5, higher is better (so migrationCost: 5 = cheap migration)
const scores = {
  "keep-node":  { throughput: 3, familiarity: 5, migrationCost: 5, opsFit: 4, hiring: 4 },
  "rewrite-go": { throughput: 5, familiarity: 2, migrationCost: 1, opsFit: 4, hiring: 3 },
} satisfies Record<string, Weights>;
type Option = keyof typeof scores;

const criteria = Object.keys(scores["keep-node"]) as Criterion[];

/** How far option x beats option y under a person's weights (negative: y wins). */
const margin = (x: Option, y: Option, w: Weights) =>
  criteria.reduce((m, c) => m + w[c] * (scores[x][c] - scores[y][c]), 0);

/**
 * The gap between two people's margins splits exactly into one term per criterion:
 * (their weight difference) × (the options' score difference). The biggest terms
 * are where the disagreement actually lives.
 */
function whereWeDisagree(x: Option, y: Option, a: Weights, b: Weights) {
  return criteria
    .map(c => ({ criterion: c, contribution: (a[c] - b[c]) * (scores[x][c] - scores[y][c]) }))
    .filter(r => r.contribution !== 0)
    .sort((p, q) => Math.abs(q.contribution) - Math.abs(p.contribution));
}

const dev: Weights = { throughput: 0.5, familiarity: 0.1, migrationCost: 0.1, opsFit: 0.1, hiring: 0.2 };
const sam: Weights = { throughput: 0.2, familiarity: 0.3, migrationCost: 0.3, opsFit: 0.1, hiring: 0.1 };

margin("rewrite-go", "keep-node", dev); //  0.1 → Go, barely
margin("rewrite-go", "keep-node", sam); // -1.8 → Node, clearly
whereWeDisagree("rewrite-go", "keep-node", dev, sam);
// migrationCost 0.8, throughput 0.6, familiarity 0.6, hiring -0.1
```

The result reframes the debate in two ways. First, the argument had been almost entirely about throughput, but the largest single source of disagreement is how much each person cares about **migration cost**. That's really a question about the roadmap (can the team afford three months on a rewrite this year?), a goal-level question for whoever owns the roadmap, not something engineers can settle by arguing. Second, even by Dev's own weights, Go wins by only 0.1. Small changes in his weights or in any score would flip it, which is worth knowing before committing to a one-way door.

> **Use the matrix to locate the disagreement, not to make the decision.** Weighted scoring produces precise-looking numbers from rough judgments. A score of 3 against 4 isn't a measurement, and nobody's priorities are really 0.3 rather than 0.35. Treat the output as a map of where to talk ("so it's really about migration cost?"), then decide with judgment. If people start adjusting scores to win, the matrix has stopped being useful.

> **Scores should be settled first, with evidence.** The split between scores and weights only works if the scores are agreed. If Dev and Sam also disagree on the throughput scores, that's a factual disagreement, and it goes back to a benchmark with a decision rule (section 5) before any weighting.

## Further Reading

- **Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton, _Getting to Yes_.** Positions versus interests, and objective criteria. The foundation for most of sections 2 and 3.
- **Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen, _Difficult Conversations_.** The personal side of disagreement: why conversations go wrong, and how to separate intent from impact.
- **Center for Applied Rationality, "Double Crux"** (the write-up on LessWrong). A structured technique for finding what would change both people's minds.
- **Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem"** (_Harvard Business Review_, 2007). A short description of the technique.
- **Jerry B. Harvey, "The Abilene Paradox: The Management of Agreement"** (_Organizational Dynamics_, 1974). The classic account of false consensus.
- **C. Northcote Parkinson, _Parkinson's Law_** (1958). The source of the law of triviality, the origin of "bikeshedding."
- **Daniel C. Dennett, _Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking_** (2013). Includes Rapoport's rules for criticizing an opposing view.
- **Jeff Bezos, Amazon 2016 shareholder letter.** A short passage on "disagree and commit," as a way to move fast on decisions where people can't reach agreement.
- **Kent Beck, _Extreme Programming Explained_.** The origin of spikes as a practice.

No sibling article is proposed: nothing here depends on a language.

---

The habit to take from this: the next time you find yourself restating your position more firmly, stop and ask two questions: _Is this about goals, facts, priorities or taste?_ and _What would change my mind?_ If you can't answer the second, the argument won't end by itself, and the next useful step is a decision, not a better sentence.
