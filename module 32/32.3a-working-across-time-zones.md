# Working Across Time Zones: Designing Around the Hours You Share

_Module 32 · Communication & Team Dynamics · 32.3a_

## The Problem

Suppose you work on an expense-management product: corporate cards, receipt capture and matching, approval workflows, accounting exports. Engineering has 26 people across three sites: Bengaluru (10), Lisbon (9) and Toronto (7). Customers are mostly in Europe and North America, with a growing number in India. The company grew into three sites by hiring where good people were, and nobody designed how the sites would work together. Over one year:

**Blocked by sleep.** Anand, in Bengaluru, picks up a ticket to add a new receipt format. The matching service is owned in Toronto. At 16:00 his time he posts a clear, self-contained question. It's 06:30 in Toronto. The answer arrives at 20:00 his time the next day, when he's offline. He reads it the following morning, and it raises a second question. A ticket he'd estimated at one day takes four, and three of those days were spent waiting for someone to wake up.

**The meeting that's always at night for the same people.** The weekly all-engineering meeting is Wednesday at 11:00 Toronto time. That's 16:00 in Lisbon, and 20:30 or 21:30 in Bengaluru depending on the season. It has been at that time for eighteen months. Bengaluru attendance drifts down. The Bengaluru engineers who attend rarely speak, with dinner and family around them. Architecture decisions get made there without them. Two senior engineers who leave mention in their exit interviews that they felt like a satellite office.

**The hour nobody was on call.** On-call follows the sun: each site covers part of its own day, and Toronto takes an evening and a short night shift. The Lisbon shift ends at "14:00 Lisbon time." Toronto's shift was set up in the on-call tool as "from 14:00 UTC" by someone in winter, when Lisbon time _is_ UTC. At the end of March, Lisbon moves to summer time, and its shift now ends at 13:00 UTC. For seven months there is an uncovered hour every day. Nobody notices until a card-authorization alert at 13:20 UTC goes unanswered for forty minutes, and cards are declined for a large customer's sales team.

**"End of day Friday."** A product manager in Toronto asks for a fix "by end of day Friday." Bengaluru finishes at 17:00 Friday their time, which is 07:30 in Toronto, and feels done early. Toronto merges a related change at 16:30 on Friday and deploys it before leaving for the weekend. That's 02:00 Saturday in Bengaluru. The change breaks receipt capture for rupee-denominated receipts. Indian customers start hitting it on Saturday morning. Toronto is asleep, and Bengaluru is off for the weekend. It's fixed on Monday.

**Follow-the-sun development.** For an urgent feature, the lead tries round-the-clock development: Lisbon works on it, hands over to Toronto, which hands over to Bengaluru. Each handover note says roughly "see the PR." Each site spends the first two or three hours of its day reconstructing what the last one was thinking, and undoes some of it. After a week, the feature is further behind than if one site had built it.

The company had treated time zones as a scheduling inconvenience: find a slot, send the invite. It's actually a structural constraint on who can talk to whom, and when. None of these failures would be fixed by more meetings or better tools. Each of them needed the structure (who owns what, who decides what, when things happen) to be designed around the hours people actually share.

## What It Is

Working across time zones means designing team structure, ownership, decision rights, rituals, handoffs and schedules around the **overlap**: the hours in which people are both working. Three terms carry most of the idea:

- **Overlap window:** the hours two people, teams or sites share. It can be several hours, one hour, or none.
- **Handoff:** passing work, or responsibility for a system, from one zone to another at the end of a working day.
- **Follow-the-sun:** a model where work or responsibility passes from zone to zone around the clock, so something is always progressing or always covered.

The general practice of communicating asynchronously is 32.1a. Time zones make that practice mandatory rather than a choice, and they add problems async writing alone doesn't solve: where to draw ownership lines, how to share out the inconvenient hours fairly, and how schedules break when clocks change. Remote teams that share a time zone have a different set of problems, covered in 32.3b.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Simple tickets take days because every question waits for someone to wake up.
- The same people are always at meetings outside their working hours.
- One site hears about decisions after they're made.
- Handovers between sites cost hours of reconstruction.
- "EOD," "tomorrow," or "Friday" in a message has caused a missed expectation.
- On-call rotas, recurring meetings or scheduled jobs misbehave for a few weeks in March or around the end of October.
- A change deployed at the end of one site's day breaks something at the start of another site's day, and nobody who can fix it is awake.
- People regularly work early mornings or late nights to cover the gap, and it's become normal.

## How It Works

### 1. Map the overlap

The first step is knowing, precisely, what the overlap is. Drawn in UTC for a normal working day of 09:00 to 17:00 local time:

```
UTC        00  02  04  06  08  10  12  14  16  18  20  22  24
           │   │   │   │   │   │   │   │   │   │   │   │   │
Bengaluru         ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓                              03:30–11:30, all year (no DST)
Lisbon W                     ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓                   09:00–17:00 winter
Lisbon S                   ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓                     08:00–16:00 summer
Toronto W                              ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓         14:00–22:00 winter
Toronto S                            ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓           13:00–21:00 summer

overlap          winter        summer
Bengaluru–Lisbon 2h30          3h30
Lisbon–Toronto   3h            3h
Bengaluru–Toronto none         none
```

This picture explains most of the Problem. Bengaluru and Toronto share no hours at all, so any work that needs them to talk many times a day will crawl. Lisbon is a hub with a few hours toward each side. And the overlap changes with the seasons, because India has no daylight saving time and Europe and North America change their clocks on different dates.

Overlap sets what's possible:

| Overlap | What works |
|---|---|
| 4+ hours | Close to a same-zone team. Pairing and live discussion are easy. |
| 1–3 hours | A daily window for live work; everything else asynchronous. |
| None | Fully asynchronous. Ownership should be split so that little needs to cross. |

### 2. Draw ownership lines along the overlap

This is the most important decision, and it's easy to never make it explicitly. If two people need to exchange questions many times a day to get their work done, they need to share working hours. Work that crosses a zone boundary should be designed to need few, well-defined exchanges.

In practice there are three common shapes:

- **Teams within a zone.** Each team sits in one zone, or in zones with at least four hours of overlap. It owns complete areas of the product, and its dependencies on teams in other zones go through stable interfaces: APIs, contracts, documented events. This is Conway's law (2.1) used deliberately. The system's boundaries follow the communication boundaries because the organization chose that.
- **A hub zone.** One site overlaps the others and coordinates between them. Lisbon is naturally in that position. It works, but hub people spend much of their day coordinating, and that load needs to be recognized and shared, not assumed.
- **Mixed teams across zones.** Possible with enough overlap, and good for spreading knowledge. With little or no overlap, every piece of work pays the waiting cost from the Problem.

Ownership alone isn't enough; **decision rights** have to follow. Anand waited three days partly because every decision about receipt matching belonged to Toronto. If the Bengaluru team is allowed to decide within agreed limits, such as adding a new receipt format that follows the existing parser interface, most of those questions become decisions that can be reviewed later rather than blockers. The general question of who decides what is 32.4b. The time-zone version is simple: a decision that has to wait for another zone to wake up should be delegated unless there's a strong reason not to.

### 3. Spend the overlap on what needs it

A few shared hours a day is a scarce resource, and it tends to get filled with whatever is scheduled first, often status meetings. Protect it for the things that genuinely need people at the same time:

- **Conversations that are going nowhere asynchronously** (the escalation rule from 32.1a).
- **Pairing across sites** (32.2b), especially for knowledge transfer.
- **Handovers** that benefit from a live conversation.
- **Relationships:** a few minutes of unstructured conversation, so the other site is made of people, not message senders.

Status goes in writing. Some people choose to shift their hours to widen the overlap, for example working 11:00–19:00. That should be voluntary, recognized, and reversible, not quietly expected of whichever site is smallest.

### 4. Hand work over so the next zone can decide

When work does cross zones, the handover note is the entire interface. "See the PR" failed because the receiving site had the code but not the reasoning. A handover that works answers:

```
Handover: rupee receipt capture — Lisbon → Bengaluru, Tue 17:00 Lisbon

State:     parser handles printed INR receipts; handwritten ones still fail (3 of 20 samples).
Done:      currency detection, amount extraction, tests in receipts/inr.test.ts.
Next:      date formats (DD-MM-YYYY vs DD/MM/YY); sample set in fixtures/inr/.
Unsure:    whether GST lines should be split out — asked finance, no answer yet.
You can decide without me:
           - date-parsing approach, any library already in the repo
           - skip handwritten receipts for this release if they take > half a day
Wait for me on:
           - anything that changes the receipt schema (other services read it)
Reachable: Lisbon overlap tomorrow 08:00–11:30 UTC.
```

The "you can decide without me" section does the most work. It turns tomorrow's questions into today's permissions. Without it, the receiving site either waits, or decides and gets overruled, which was exactly the undoing in the follow-the-sun attempt.

Follow-the-sun _development_, meaning one task continuously passed around the world, rarely pays off. Every handover costs context, and design-heavy work doesn't split cleanly across people who can't talk. Research on global software development, notably by Erran Carmel and colleagues, finds it hard to make work outside narrow conditions. It works much better for work made of **independent units**: support tickets, test runs, operational tasks, separate small tickets. For a single feature, it's usually better for one site to own it and the others to review asynchronously.

### 5. Ask questions that survive the night

32.1a covered self-contained messages. Across zones, add one more habit: **branch the question**, so the work can continue whatever the answer is.

> Should rupee receipts store GST as a separate line? If yes, I'll add a `taxLines` field (schema change, needs your review). If no, I'll keep the total only. If I don't hear back by my morning, I'll go with "no" for now, since it's additive to change later, and flag it in the PR.

Also: **know the other zone's hours, and ask before they end.** A question sent an hour before Toronto logs off gets answered today. The same question sent an hour after doesn't. Showing local time and working hours in chat profiles makes this effortless.

### 6. Say times in a zone, and know which zone a schedule belongs to

"EOD," "tomorrow morning," and "Friday" mean different moments in each zone. Always attach a zone to a deadline, or use UTC. For deadlines across many zones, some fields use **Anywhere on Earth** (AoE, UTC−12): a deadline of "Friday AoE" hasn't passed while it's still Friday anywhere on the planet, so nobody loses a day to arithmetic.

Schedules need a more careful rule: **define each recurring schedule in the zone it belongs to.**

- A shift or meeting that belongs to a person's working day is defined in that person's local zone, so it follows their clock through daylight saving time.
- A system job that has nothing to do with anyone's day (a nightly export, a certificate renewal) is defined in UTC.

The uncovered on-call hour came from mixing the two in one chain. The Lisbon shift was defined in Lisbon time and the Toronto shift in UTC. The two were adjacent in winter and an hour apart in summer.

Daylight saving time has several traps worth knowing:

- **North America and Europe change on different dates.** The US and Canada change on the second Sunday of March and the first Sunday of November. The EU changes on the last Sunday of March and the last Sunday of October. So for about three weeks in March and one week around the end of October, the offset between Toronto and Lisbon is an hour different from the rest of the year. A meeting defined in one zone moves by an hour for the other.
- **Many zones have no DST at all.** India is one, so its overlap with everyone else shifts twice a year.
- **The southern hemisphere changes in the opposite direction.** Parts of Australia move their clocks forward when Europe moves back, so a European–Australian overlap can change by two hours.
- **Rules change.** Countries adopt and abolish DST; Brazil abolished it in 2019. Rely on the IANA time zone database through your language's date library, never on fixed offsets.

Weekends and public holidays differ as well. Not every country's weekend is Saturday and Sunday, and a site's national holidays are a working day elsewhere. A shared calendar of each site's holidays prevents "why is nobody answering?" days.

### 7. Share the inconvenient hours fairly

With no hour that's reasonable for everyone, a meeting will always be inconvenient for someone. The Problem's failure wasn't the inconvenient time; it was that the same people bore it permanently. There are several ways out:

- **Rotate the slot.** Alternate between a time that's good for Bengaluru and Lisbon and a time that's good for Lisbon and Toronto, so the inconvenience is shared.
- **Split the meeting by region.** Run it twice, once per overlap window, with the same material and a combined written summary.
- **Make it asynchronous with a live option.** Post a recording or written briefing, collect questions in writing, and answer them in the next regional session.
- **Move decisions out of the meeting** (32.1a), so missing it doesn't mean missing the decision.

It's worth tracking who attends outside their working hours and how often. The numbers usually surprise the people who never have to.

### 8. On-call across zones

On-call is where time zones stop being about convenience and start being about sleep and incidents. There are two basic models:

- **Follow-the-sun.** Each site covers its own daytime, and nobody is paged at night. This needs sites spread widely enough to cover 24 hours (the Google SRE book describes two sites around eight hours apart each taking roughly 12-hour shifts). It also needs knowledge of every system spread across all sites, and a disciplined handover at each boundary: open incidents, ongoing risks, recent deploys, anything unusual.
- **Single-zone rotation.** One team covers all 24 hours, including nights. The knowledge requirements are simpler, but it costs sleep, and it needs to be compensated and limited.

Many companies run a hybrid, like the one in the Problem: daytime coverage by site, with one site also covering the gap.

Two rules prevent the silent failures. **Generate the schedule from one definition**, with each shift in its own zone (section 6). And **verify coverage for every day of the year**, not just for the week the rota was set up. That second rule is mechanical, so it's what the Implementation checks.

### 9. Deploy while the owners are awake

A deploy carries risk for the hours after it, and someone who can fix it needs to be awake for those hours. Deploying at the end of your own day hands that risk to someone asleep, or to a site that doesn't own the code.

A few rules help:

- **Deploy when the owning team has several working hours left.**
- **Be careful with changes that affect customers whose day is about to start.** The rupee bug hit Indian customers at the start of their Saturday.
- **Include deploy state in on-call handovers.**
- **Freeze windows should cover the people who'll handle problems,** not just the office the rule was written in: "no deploys after 15:00 local on Fridays" means different things at each site.

Progressive delivery (30.2b) reduces the risk from any single deploy. It doesn't change who's awake when something goes wrong.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any team whose working hours overlap by less than about four hours.** Below that, the waiting costs in the Problem start to appear.
- **Several sites,** especially when some pairs of sites share no hours at all.
- **On-call across zones,** or customers across zones that you support around the clock.
- **Teams spanning zones with different DST rules,** where schedules and rotas can silently shift.
- **Remote hiring that reaches new zones.** The first hire in a new zone is the moment to design this, not after the tenth.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A team spread across one or two neighboring zones,** such as Lisbon and Berlin, or New York and Chicago. With six or more hours of overlap, most of this reduces to the async habits of 32.1a and remembering to say which zone a time is in.
- **Reorganizing ownership for one person.** A single engineer five hours away from an otherwise co-located team doesn't need their own team boundary. They need a protected overlap, well-written handovers, and a fair share of meeting times.
- **Follow-the-sun on-call for a small service.** If a service pages once a month, a simple single-zone rotation with occasional night calls may cost less than the knowledge-spreading and handover discipline follow-the-sun needs.
- **Follow-the-sun development of a single feature.** As described in section 4, it usually costs more than it gains.

## Trade-offs

- **Slower decisions across zones,** however well structured. Delegation and branched questions reduce the delay; they don't remove it.
- **Ownership by zone creates silos by zone.** If Toronto owns receipt matching, Bengaluru will know little about it, and the system's architecture will increasingly reflect the org chart. That's fine while the boundaries are stable, and painful when they need to move.
- **The hub site pays a coordination tax.** Lisbon ends up in more meetings and more handovers than anyone else.
- **Shifted hours cost people's personal lives.** Early starts and late finishes to widen the overlap are useful and easy to over-rely on.
- **Fair rotation means everyone has some inconvenient meetings,** including the people who didn't before, who may resent it.
- **Follow-the-sun on-call needs knowledge spread,** and that costs deliberate cross-site pairing and documentation (32.2b, 29.3a).
- **More process around time:** zone-tagged deadlines, holiday calendars, handover templates, schedule checks. Each is small, but together they're a real overhead that single-zone teams don't pay.

## Alternatives

- **Hire within a band of time zones.** Many remote companies hire only within a range, for example UTC−3 to UTC+3, deliberately trading hiring reach for overlap.
- **Co-locate by function.** Put whole functions in one place, with clear interfaces between places, which is the ownership approach from section 2 taken to its limit.
- **Shift hours at one site.** One site works partly offset hours, recognized and compensated, to create a shared window.
- **Two sites instead of three.** Two sites about eight hours apart give follow-the-sun on-call with a single, clean handover in each direction.
- **Buy coverage.** For some functions, like first-line support or overnight operations monitoring, a provider in another zone can cover the hours more cheaply than stretching your own teams.

## How to Achieve It

1. **Make zones visible.** Put local time and working hours in chat profiles, attach a zone to every deadline, and keep a shared calendar of each site's public holidays. This costs almost nothing and removes a whole class of misunderstandings. _Next step when:_ nobody misreads times any more, but work between sites still stalls.
2. **Map the overlap, and fix meeting fairness.** Draw the overlap for each pair of sites in both seasons. Protect the overlap hours from status meetings, and rotate or split the meetings that are always inconvenient for the same people. _Next step when:_ meetings are fair, but tickets that cross sites still take days.
3. **Redraw ownership and decision rights around the overlap,** and adopt handover notes that include "you can decide without me." _Next step when:_ work flows between sites, but schedules and on-call are still set up by hand.
4. **Generate rotas and recurring schedules from one definition,** each item in its own zone. Verify coverage for the whole year in CI, and adopt deploy-timing rules.

## Implementation

Most of this is structure and habit. The piece that code does better than people is checking schedules across daylight-saving changes, because the failure appears only on certain dates and is invisible on the day someone sets the schedule up. TypeScript with Luxon, which uses the IANA zone database through the platform's `Intl` support, is enough.

The core is one function: turn a daily span defined in its own zone into a real interval on a given date.

```ts
import { DateTime, Interval } from "luxon";

type DailySpan = { name: string; zone: string; from: string; to: string }; // "HH:mm", local to `zone`

function spanOn(s: DailySpan, isoDate: string): Interval {
  const day = DateTime.fromISO(isoDate, { zone: s.zone });
  const [fh, fm] = s.from.split(":").map(Number);
  const [th, tm] = s.to.split(":").map(Number);
  const start = day.set({ hour: fh, minute: fm });
  let end = day.set({ hour: th, minute: tm });
  if (end <= start) end = end.plus({ days: 1 }); // overnight span, e.g. 22:00–03:00
  return Interval.fromDateTimes(start, end);
}
```

With that, checking an on-call rota is a matter of asking, for every day of the year, which parts of the UTC day no shift covers. Here's the rota from the Problem, exactly as it was configured:

```ts
const rota: DailySpan[] = [
  { name: "Bengaluru",     zone: "Asia/Kolkata",  from: "08:30", to: "14:30" },
  { name: "Lisbon",        zone: "Europe/Lisbon", from: "08:00", to: "14:00" },
  { name: "Toronto",       zone: "UTC",           from: "14:00", to: "22:00" }, // set up in winter
  { name: "Toronto night", zone: "UTC",           from: "22:00", to: "03:00" },
];

function uncoveredOn(isoDate: string): string[] {
  const start = DateTime.fromISO(isoDate, { zone: "UTC" });
  const day = Interval.fromDateTimes(start, start.plus({ days: 1 }));
  const yesterday = start.minus({ days: 1 }).toISODate()!;
  // include yesterday's shifts: an overnight shift that started yesterday covers this morning
  const shifts = rota.flatMap(s => [spanOn(s, yesterday), spanOn(s, isoDate)]);
  return day.difference(...shifts).map(
    i => `${i.start!.toUTC().toFormat("HH:mm")}–${i.end!.toUTC().toFormat("HH:mm")} UTC`,
  );
}

function coverageReport(year: number): string[] {
  const report: string[] = [];
  let d = DateTime.fromObject({ year, month: 1, day: 1 }, { zone: "UTC" });
  let runStart = "", runGaps = "";
  for (; d.year === year; d = d.plus({ days: 1 })) {
    const gaps = uncoveredOn(d.toISODate()!).join(", ");
    if (gaps !== runGaps) { // group consecutive days with the same gaps
      if (runGaps) report.push(`${runStart} → ${d.minus({ days: 1 }).toISODate()}: uncovered ${runGaps}`);
      [runStart, runGaps] = [d.toISODate()!, gaps];
    }
  }
  if (runGaps) report.push(`${runStart} → ${year}-12-31: uncovered ${runGaps}`);
  return report;
}
```

Run against 2026, it prints one line, which is exactly the incident:

```
2026-03-29 → 2026-10-24: uncovered 13:00–14:00 UTC
```

Changing the Toronto shifts to `zone: "America/Toronto"` with their local times makes the report empty. Run in CI whenever the rota definition changes, and once a month anyway, this catches the problem the day it's introduced instead of seven months later.

The same `spanOn` function produces the overlap table from section 1 for any date, by intersecting each pair of people's working spans. Computing it for a few dates through the year shows the seasonal shifts directly. Between Lisbon and Toronto, for instance, the overlap grows to four hours during the three weeks in March when North America has changed its clocks and Europe hasn't.

> **Test the dates that matter, not today.** A schedule check that runs only for the current week passes on the day the rota is set up, and that's the day it's least likely to be wrong. The useful dates are the DST transitions in every zone involved, and the weeks in between when some zones have changed and others haven't. Checking every day of the year is cheap enough that there's no reason to pick.

> **Use zone names, never offsets.** `"Europe/Lisbon"` knows about summer time and any future rule change; `"+00:00"` is only right half the year. The same goes for storing schedules: store the zone name and the local time, and compute UTC when needed. A schedule stored as a UTC time has already lost the information about which clock it was meant to follow.

## Further Reading

- **GitLab Handbook, all-remote guides** (handbook.gitlab.com), especially the sections on time zones and asynchronous work. A detailed account from a company spread across most time zones.
- **Erran Carmel, _Global Software Teams_** (1999), and **Carmel, Espinosa and Dubinsky, "'Follow the Sun' Workflow in Global Software Development"** (_Journal of Management Information Systems_, 2010). The foundational work on distributed development, and a careful look at when follow-the-sun works.
- **Betsy Beyer et al. (eds.), _Site Reliability Engineering_** (Google, 2016), chapter "Being On-Call." Multi-site on-call, shift lengths and the limits on on-call load.
- **Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, _Remote: Office Not Required_** (2013). The short, opinionated case for remote work, including the recommendation to keep some overlap.
- **Erin Meyer, _The Culture Map_** (2014). Sites in different countries often differ in more than time: in how directly people disagree, and how decisions get made. Useful context for the next articles.
- **The IANA Time Zone Database** (iana.org/time-zones), and **Luxon's documentation on time zones and DST** (moment.github.io/luxon). Where the rules actually live, and how one library exposes them.
- **"Falsehoods programmers believe about time,"** the widely shared lists by Noah Sussman and others. Short reminders of how many assumptions about clocks turn out to be wrong.

No sibling article is proposed. The time-handling code would look much the same in any language with an IANA-aware date library, such as `java.time` or Go's `time` package.

---

The habit to take from this: when you work with someone in another zone, picture their clock before you act. Is this the right moment to ask, to deploy, or to schedule, for _them_? And when you set up anything that recurs, ask whose clock it follows. Most time-zone failures are one of those two questions, unasked.
