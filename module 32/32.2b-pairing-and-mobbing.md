# Pairing and Mobbing: When Several People on One Problem Pays Off

_Module 32 · Communication & Team Dynamics · 32.2b_

## The Problem

Suppose you work on software that small breweries use to run production: recipes, brewing batches, tank and stock tracking, and the monthly alcohol-duty return to the tax authority. There are nine engineers, one of them in Montreal, five hours behind everyone else. Everyone works alone on their own ticket and opens a pull request when it's done. Over two months:

**The module only one person understands.** Oksana wrote the duty calculation four years ago. It handles rates by strength band, relief for small producers, and duty on beer lost or spilled after it was declared. Every change to it for the last two years has been hers, or reviewed by her. The government announces new rates and a change to small-producer relief, effective in six weeks. Oksana's three-week holiday falls right in the middle. Raj takes the change. It takes him nine days to do what she estimates would have taken her one, most of it spent working out _why_ the code does what it does. He gets the relief taper wrong for breweries near the threshold, and the error reaches two customers' returns before Oksana is back.

**The review that came too late.** Tom spends a week on batch transfers between tanks and opens a 1,400-line pull request. The first review round is about naming. In the second round, two days later, the reviewer raises the real objection: transfers should be recorded as events so stock can be reconstructed at any date, which the duty return needs. That's a redesign. The PR goes through four rounds over six days, and everyone is frustrated with everyone.

**The new hire and the starter tickets.** Mei joins and gets "good first issues" to do alone. She spends two days fighting the local environment and a third trying to understand what "hectolitres of pure alcohol" means in the code, and doesn't want to ask, because everyone looks busy. Six weeks in, she still hasn't touched anything central.

**The one thing that went well.** A customer's stock doesn't match their physical count after a tank transfer. Four people get on a call, one of them sharing their screen, and pass control around every ten minutes. In two hours they find a rounding bug that occurs only when a transfer is split across two tanks. Three of the four say afterwards that they'd never have found it alone, and that they learned more about the stock model in those two hours than in the previous month.

After a retrospective, the lead reads about Extreme Programming and decides: from now on, everyone pairs, all day, in fixed pairs. Three weeks later:

- **Two people are visibly exhausted.**
- **In one pair, the senior engineer always has the keyboard** and the junior watches.
- **Small changes now take two people,** like bumping a dependency or fixing a typo in an email template.
- **Raj, who is hard of hearing,** finds eight hours of video calls draining in a way nobody noticed.
- **The Montreal engineer can only pair for three hours a day,** because that's the overlap, so he's left out of most of it.
- **The fixed pairs have created new silos,** except now each silo is two people instead of one.

The team didn't discover that pairing works or that it doesn't. It discovered that working together is a tool with specific benefits and specific costs. It had been using solo work where working together would have been better, and then switched to using working together where solo work would have been better.

## What It Is

**Pair programming** is two people working on the same task at the same time, at one screen, shared either physically or remotely, exchanging roles frequently. It isn't one person helping another for five minutes, and it isn't two people splitting a task and working in parallel.

**Mob programming**, now often called **ensemble programming**, extends this to a group of three or more, often a whole team. The group works on one thing, with one person typing and the role rotating every few minutes. Woody Zuill, whose team popularized it, describes it as the whole team working on the same thing, at the same time, in the same space, at the same computer.

Two neighbors are easy to confuse with these:

- **Code review** (29.2) is feedback _after_ the code is written. Pairing gives the same feedback _while_ it's being written. They answer the same need at different times, which is why the "review that came too late" is a pairing problem as much as a review problem.
- **Swarming**, from Kanban, means several people working on one item, but on separate parts of it in parallel. It shares the goal of finishing one thing before starting another, but not the screen.

## Recognizing the Problem

- An area of the codebase has one person who changes it or reviews every change to it.
- Estimates depend heavily on who takes the ticket.
- Fundamental design objections arrive in code review, after the work is done.
- Pull requests go through three or more review rounds.
- New hires take months to work on core parts of the system.
- People stay stuck for hours or days before asking for help.
- Hard bugs get solved quickly when several people happen to look together, but that only ever happens by accident.

Signs of overcorrection:

- **People are drained by the end of the day.**
- **Small, obvious changes take two people.**
- **One person in a pair does all the typing.**
- **Some people are consistently excluded** by hours, time zone, or how they work best.
- **Nobody has time to think alone.**

## How It Works

### 1. Why it works, when it works

Four mechanisms account for most of the benefit.

**Feedback arrives in seconds instead of days.** In Tom's pull request, the objection that mattered came six days after the design decision it was about. In a pair, the same objection arrives before the first line of the design is written:

```
solo + PR review
  design ─── build (5 days) ─── PR ── review ── rework (4 rounds, 6 days) ── merge
  ▲                                     ▲
  decision                              objection  ← days later, after the building

pair
  design ── objection ── build ─────────────── merge
  ▲         ▲
  decision  seconds later, before the building
```

**Tacit knowledge moves.** What Raj couldn't reconstruct from Oksana's code wasn't _what_ it did. It was _why_: which tax rules each branch implements, which edge cases came from real customer incidents, which parts are safe to change. That kind of knowledge rarely ends up in documentation (29.3a, 31.4a), because the person holding it doesn't know which parts others lack. It transfers when someone watches the expert work and asks questions at the moment they come up.

**Focus.** Two people rarely both drift off into email at the same time, and a partner will ask "is this rabbit hole worth it?" out loud.

**Fewer waiting states.** Mob programming in particular removes the waits between people: for a review, for an answer, for someone to get back from a meeting. Everyone who could answer is already there.

The costs are just as real. Two people's time goes into one task, and the work is more tiring than solo work. Research reflects both sides. In an early university study by Cockburn and Williams, pairs spent roughly 15% more total effort than individuals and produced roughly 15% fewer defects. A 2009 meta-analysis by Hannay and colleagues found that the effects vary with the task and the people. Pairing tended to help quality on complex tasks and speed on simpler ones, but at a consistent cost in total effort, and results varied widely between studies. The honest summary: pairing buys quality, knowledge spread and fewer delays, at the price of more person-hours. Whether that's a good trade depends on the work.

### 2. Styles

**Driver and navigator.** The classic division. The driver types and thinks about the current line and function. The navigator thinks one level up: the overall approach, what's coming next, what could go wrong. Swap every 15 to 30 minutes, or at natural breakpoints. The typical failure is a navigator who drifts into their phone, or a driver who types silently so the navigator can only follow.

**Strong-style.** Llewellyn Falco's rule: _for an idea to go from your head into the computer, it must go through someone else's hands._ The person with the idea navigates; the other person types. This matters most when experience is uneven. The instinct is for the expert to drive and the novice to watch, which is the "watch the master" failure that had the junior engineer staring at a screen for three weeks. Strong-style reverses it. The expert navigates, explaining intentions ("we need the relief rate for this band; it's in `rates.ts`, look for the taper"), and the novice types. The novice has to understand each step well enough to perform it, and the expert has to put the reasoning into words, which is exactly the knowledge Raj was missing.

**Ping-pong.** Paired with test-driven development (21.2). A writes a failing test; B makes it pass, then writes the next failing test; A makes that pass, and so on. The rhythm keeps both people typing and thinking, and it doesn't drift into one person driving. It needs code that's reasonably easy to test.

**Unstructured.** Two experienced peers often just talk and pass the keyboard as it suits them. That's fine, but it tends to drift toward one person driving. A timer is a cheap correction.

The combination of experience matters:

| Pair | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Expert + expert | Hard problems, risky changes, design | Shared blind spots; little knowledge spread |
| Expert + novice | Spreading knowledge, onboarding | "Watch the master" — use strong-style, novice types |
| Novice + novice | Learning by doing, building confidence | Getting stuck; set a time limit before asking for help |

### 3. Mob (ensemble) programming

In a mob, one person types, and the rest of the group navigates using strong-style: the typist types what the group decides and doesn't add their own ideas while typing. The typist rotates on a short timer, often every 4 to 10 minutes, so nobody watches for long and everyone stays engaged. Someone may facilitate, keeping discussion moving and making sure quieter people are heard, as in 32.2a.

Mobbing is the extreme of the trade-off: the highest cost in person-hours, and the fastest feedback and knowledge spread. That makes it a good fit for a particular kind of work:

- **Hard investigations and incidents,** like the stock-count bug, where several partial understandings of the system combine into a complete one.
- **Work that sets conventions** others will follow: the first endpoint in a new service, the first use of a new library.
- **Changes that touch everyone's area** and would otherwise need a round of reviews from every owner.
- **Onboarding several people at once.**
- **High-uncertainty work** where the team would otherwise spend days in meetings discussing an approach.

Some teams, including Zuill's, mob all day. More teams mob for specific work, or for a regular session each week. Both work. The Open Space "law of two feet" from 32.2a applies: people step out when they're neither contributing nor learning, and step back in later.

### 4. Choosing between solo, pair and mob

The decision depends mainly on two things: how hard or uncertain the work is, and how concentrated the knowledge it needs is.

```
                                   knowledge the work needs is…
                         spread across the team          held by one or two people
                     ┌──────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────┐
  routine, low       │ SOLO + async review          │ PAIR for transfer             │
  uncertainty        │ (dependency bump, copy fix)  │ (strong-style, the non-expert │
                     │                              │  types)                       │
                     ├──────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┤
  hard, risky,       │ PAIR of peers                │ MOB                           │
  uncertain          │ (design + build together)    │ (the duty-rate change, the    │
                     │                              │  stock-count bug)             │
                     └──────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────┘
```

Some triggers override the grid: someone stuck for more than about an hour, a new team member, or an incident. Solo work is right more often than enthusiasts admit. That includes reading and research, prototyping to see whether an idea works at all, the first rough exploration of a problem, and anyone who genuinely thinks better alone before discussing. A common pattern is to explore alone, then pair to build.

The duty-rate change sits in the bottom right. Had Oksana, Raj and Mei mobbed on it for two days before her holiday, the change would likely have shipped correctly and three people would understand the duty module afterwards.

### 5. Rotation

Pairing spreads knowledge only if pairs change. Fixed pairs just turn one-person silos into two-person silos. There are two common rotation patterns:

- **Rotate every day or two, with one person staying.** The person who stays carries the context of the task; the person joining brings fresh eyes and takes knowledge back to their next pair.

  ```
  day 1       day 2        day 3
  Oksana+Raj  Raj+Mei      Mei+Tom      ← the task continues;
                                          knowledge spreads one person per day
  ```

- **Frequent rotation.** Arlo Belshee's experiment with "promiscuous pairing" rotated pairs as often as every 90 minutes. He found the team did better with constant fresh eyes than with deep continuity, because the newcomer's "beginner's mind" kept exposing assumptions. Few teams rotate that often, but the result is a useful counterweight to the instinct that continuity always beats freshness.

A **knowledge map** helps direct rotation: which areas of the system have only one or two people who can work in them? Those areas get deliberate pairing. The Implementation section builds a rough version from git history.

### 6. Keeping it sustainable

Pairing is more intense than solo work: there's no drifting, no pause to check messages, and constant talking. Most teams that pair heavily find four to six hours a day is the realistic maximum. The rest goes to email, reading, reviews, meetings and solo thinking. Some ways to keep it sustainable:

- **Regular breaks,** on a timer if needed. Pomodoro-style 25-minute blocks with short breaks work well for many pairs.
- **Pair by default for certain kinds of work, not for all work.** Use the grid rather than a blanket rule.
- **Adapt for people, instead of excluding them.** For Raj: captions, a shared text side-channel, shorter sessions, and in-person pairing where possible. For people who need time alone to think: alternate paired and solo stretches on the same task. For the Montreal engineer: use the three-hour overlap for pairing (32.3a), and hand over the rest of the day through clear notes.
- **Let the typing be shared.** If one person always drives, strong-style or ping-pong fixes it better than a reminder does.

### 7. Remote pairing

Remote pairing works, and some people prefer it: each person has their own keyboard, screen and chair. The tools fall into three groups:

- **Screen sharing with remote control** (Tuple, Pop, or conferencing tools with control handover). This is closest to sitting together. It depends on low latency.
- **Shared editors** (VS Code Live Share, JetBrains Code With Me). Each person has their own editor view and cursor, and edits appear live for both. Terminals and debuggers are shared with some limits.
- **Handover through git.** The typist commits work in progress to a shared branch and the next person pulls and continues on their own machine. The `mob` command-line tool (from remotemobprogramming.org) automates this handover for mob rotations. This is the most robust option across flaky networks, because each person uses their own full environment.

Audio quality matters more than video. A good headset does more for a remote pair than a better camera.

### 8. Pairing, code review and compliance

If code was written by two people, does it also need a pull request review? Many pairing teams say no: the pair _was_ the review. They record both people with a `Co-authored-by:` trailer on the commit and merge after CI passes. That matches the feedback-timing argument above.

Two caveats. First, if a regulation or audit framework requires evidence that someone other than the author reviewed a change (the "four-eyes principle" in many SOX and SOC 2 control sets), whether pairing counts depends on your auditors. Many teams keep a lightweight PR anyway, approved by the pair's second person, because it produces the evidence automatically. Second, a pair can share blind spots, especially if the two people always pair together. A risky change may still warrant a short review from someone outside the pair.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Knowledge held by one or two people,** especially before holidays, departures or reorgs. The duty module is the textbook case.
- **Onboarding.** A new hire pairing with rotating teammates for their first few weeks, typing in strong-style, typically reaches core work much sooner than one working alone on starter tickets.
- **Risky or hard-to-reverse changes:** payments, tax, data migrations, security-sensitive code.
- **Design-heavy work** where a late objection would mean redoing it.
- **Incidents and hard bugs,** which usually benefit from a mob.
- **Recurring PR ping-pong** between the same people or on the same area.
- **Teams building shared conventions,** such as a new service or a new framework in the codebase.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Routine, well-understood changes:** dependency updates, copy changes, configuration. One person plus CI plus a quick review is enough.
- **Exploration and research,** where the first step is reading, trying things out and thinking. A pair tends to make it a conversation too soon.
- **As a blanket mandate.** A rule that everyone pairs all day produces the overcorrection in the Problem: fatigue, excluded people, two people on trivial changes.
- **When the team has little overlap in working hours.** With under two hours of overlap, pairing has to be rare and deliberate; handover notes (32.3a) and good async review carry the rest.
- **As a substitute for writing things down.** Pairing spreads knowledge to the people who were there. Decisions and system knowledge that others will need later still belong in writing (32.1a, 29.3a).

## Trade-offs

- **More person-hours per task.** Two or more people on one task costs more total effort, even when it finishes the task sooner. The work gets spent differently, and anyone counting tickets per person will see what looks like halved productivity.
- **Fatigue.** Pairing all day is more tiring than solo work, and it's easy to underestimate until people are burned out.
- **Uneven cost across people.** It's harder for people who are hard of hearing, who work outside the core hours, who need quiet to think, or who find constant social contact draining. Unadapted, pairing can quietly push those people to the edges of the team.
- **Power dynamics.** Seniority can turn a pair into one person working and one person watching. Structure (strong-style, timers) counteracts it, but only if someone watches for it.
- **Less solo deep thinking.** Some problems need one person to stare at them for an hour in silence. Pairing crowds that out if it's the only way of working.
- **Scheduling.** Pairs need two calendars to line up; mobs need several. Meetings (32.2a) fragment pairing time even more than solo time.
- **Invisible to history.** Unless pairing is recorded in commits, git shows only the typist. Knowledge maps, authorship metrics (31.1) and review evidence all get the wrong answer.

## Alternatives

- **Async code review** (29.2). The default for most teams. It's cheaper in person-hours, and better for people who can't overlap in time, at the cost of later feedback. Small PRs and early "draft PR" check-ins shrink the delay.
- **Design review before building.** A short written proposal (32.1a) or a 20-minute design conversation before starting catches the kind of objection that sank Tom's PR, without pairing on the implementation.
- **Desk checks.** A 10-minute walk through a change with a teammate before opening the PR. A small, cheap step toward pairing.
- **Documentation and runbooks** (29.3a, 31.4a). They spread explicit knowledge, and they reach people who weren't there, but they rarely carry the "why" as well as working together does.
- **Shadowing.** The novice watches the expert on real work, then the expert watches the novice (reverse shadowing). A lighter onboarding version of strong-style pairing.
- **Swarming.** Several people on one item, on separate parts in parallel. It finishes items faster without sharing a screen, but spreads less knowledge.

## How to Achieve It

1. **Pair on specific triggers.** Start with onboarding, stuck for more than an hour, changes to areas one person knows, and risky changes. Record pairs with `Co-authored-by:` trailers from the start. _Next step when:_ people are pairing, but the sessions often turn into one person working and one watching.
2. **Learn the styles.** Use a timer to swap roles, strong-style for knowledge transfer with the less experienced person typing, and ping-pong where the code is testable. _Next step when:_ pairs work well, but knowledge still stays in the same small groups.
3. **Rotate deliberately,** guided by a knowledge map: one person stays with the task, the other rotates every day or two, and single-person areas get targeted pairing. _Next step when:_ knowledge spreads, but hard problems and incidents still take too long.
4. **Add mobbing for specific work:** incidents, hard bugs, cross-cutting changes, first-of-a-kind work, or a regular weekly session.

Signs you've gone too far: people are drained, trivial work takes two people, some people are regularly left out, and nobody has time to think alone. Pull back to the triggers, rather than dropping the practice.

## Implementation

The code worth writing here isn't pairing tooling; it's a rough knowledge map. The question is: _which areas of the system have been worked on by only one person recently?_ Those are where Oksana-style risk is building and where pairing should be aimed. Git history can answer it roughly, but only if pairing is recorded. A commit made by a pair shows only the typist as the author, so without `Co-authored-by:` trailers, the history makes every pair look like one person working alone.

```ts
import { execFileSync } from "node:child_process";

const RS = "\x1e", US = "\x1f", GS = "\x1d"; // record, field, group separators

/** For each area (top-level folders to `depth`), who has committed or co-authored changes to it. */
function peopleByArea(since = "90 days ago", depth = 2): Map<string, Set<string>> {
  const log = execFileSync("git", [
    "log", `--since=${since}`, "--no-merges", "--name-only",
    // author email, then co-author trailers, then (after GS) the changed files
    `--format=${RS}%ae${US}%(trailers:key=Co-authored-by,valueonly,separator=%x1f)${GS}`,
  ], { encoding: "utf8" });

  const areas = new Map<string, Set<string>>();
  for (const entry of log.split(RS).slice(1)) {
    const [header, files] = entry.split(GS);
    const [author, ...coAuthors] = header.split(US);
    const people = [
      author,
      ...coAuthors.filter(Boolean).map(c => c.match(/<(.+)>/)?.[1] ?? c.trim()), // "Name <email>" → email
    ].map(p => p.toLowerCase());

    for (const file of files.split("\n").filter(Boolean)) {
      const area = file.split("/").slice(0, depth).join("/");
      const set = areas.get(area) ?? new Set<string>();
      people.forEach(p => set.add(p));
      areas.set(area, set);
    }
  }
  return areas;
}

for (const [area, people] of peopleByArea()) {
  if (people.size <= 1) console.log(`${area}: only ${[...people][0]} in the last 90 days`);
}
```

On the brewery codebase before any pairing, this prints what everyone already suspected:

```
src/duty: only oksana@… in the last 90 days
src/excise-export: only oksana@… in the last 90 days
src/recipes: only tom@… in the last 90 days
```

Run weekly, it shows whether rotation is actually spreading knowledge, which is a more honest measure than counting pairing sessions.

> **Touched isn't the same as understood.** A person who co-authored one commit in `src/duty` shows up as a second person who knows it. They may not. This is a first approximation, meant to find the areas with obviously too few people, not to certify that an area is safe. A mob commit that lists five co-authors credits everyone who was on the call, including anyone who wasn't paying attention.

> **Emails aren't identities.** People commit with work and personal addresses, and co-author trailers get typed by hand. Map known aliases to one person before trusting the counts, or a single person will look like two.

> **Keep it about areas, not people.** The same caution as 31.1 applies. The useful output is "this area has one person," not a ranking of who pairs the most. Once it becomes a ranking, people will add co-author trailers to commits they barely watched.

## Further Reading

- **Birgitta Böckeler and Nina Siessegger, "On Pair Programming"** (martinfowler.com, 2020). The best practical overview: styles, pitfalls, remote pairing, and the costs.
- **Kent Beck, _Extreme Programming Explained_ (2nd ed., 2004).** Pairing in its original context, as one practice among several that support each other.
- **Llewellyn Falco, "Llewellyn's strong-style pairing"** (2014). The short post that defined strong-style.
- **Woody Zuill and Kevin Meadows, _Mob Programming: A Whole Team Approach_.** How the practice started and how a team does it full-time.
- **Maaret Pyhäjärvi, _Ensemble Programming Guidebook_.** A practical guide to running ensembles, including facilitation and roles.
- **Arlo Belshee, "Promiscuous Pairing and Beginner's Mind"** (Agile 2005). The frequent-rotation experiment.
- **Alistair Cockburn and Laurie Williams, "The Costs and Benefits of Pair Programming"** (2000), and **Jo E. Hannay et al., "The effectiveness of pair programming: A meta-analysis"** (_Information and Software Technology_, 2009). An early optimistic study and a later, more cautious synthesis.
- **remotemobprogramming.org.** A guide to remote mobbing, and the `mob` tool for handing over through git.
- **GitHub Docs, "Creating a commit with multiple authors."** The `Co-authored-by:` trailer convention.

No sibling article is proposed: nothing here depends on a language.

---

The habit to take from this: next time something takes you or a teammate twice as long as it should, ask which of two things is missing. Is it someone to think with, or someone who already knows? The first is a reason to pair. The second is a reason to pair with that specific person, before they go on holiday.
