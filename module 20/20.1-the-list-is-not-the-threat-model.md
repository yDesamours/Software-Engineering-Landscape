# The List Is Not the Threat Model

_Module 20.1 — Security Best Practices_

## The Problem

A B2B analytics product, mid-size, doing well enough that a prospect's procurement team now sends a security questionnaire before signing. One line item: _"Does the application address the OWASP Top 10?"_

The engineering lead does the responsible thing. She pulls up the list, turns each entry into a Jira ticket, and the team works through them. They add a Content-Security-Policy header (XSS). They switch the last few string-concatenated queries to parameterized ones (Injection). They add `helmet` and set the security headers (Misconfiguration). They upgrade the four dependencies flagged by the scanner (Vulnerable Components). They turn on the WAF their cloud provider offers, because it advertises "OWASP Top 10 coverage." Ten tickets, ten green checkmarks. She writes "Yes" on the questionnaire, honestly.

Four months later they have an incident. A customer's analyst, on the free tier, changes the `workspace_id` in an API request from their own to a number one higher, and pulls back another company's dashboards. No injection. No XSS. No vulnerable dependency. No misconfiguration in any scanner's sense. Every one of the ten tickets is still closed and still correct.

The bug is that `GET /api/workspaces/{id}/dashboards` checks that you're logged in and never checks that the workspace is _yours_. It is the single most common serious vulnerability in web applications, it sits at the very top of the same list they worked through, and the checklist approach walked straight past it — because this bug isn't a header you can set or a library you can swap. It's a missing sentence in the authorization logic of one endpoint, and there are three hundred endpoints.

Here is the thing the incident revealed, stated plainly:

```
   what the checklist treats the Top 10 as        what it actually is
   ─────────────────────────────────────────      ──────────────────────────────
   ten bugs to find and fix                        ten FAMILIES of failure
   a task with a done state                        a map of where to keep looking
   fixable with headers and libraries              mostly fixable only in your
                                                     own domain logic
   "we did it" ✓                                   "we designed against it" — ongoing
```

The team answered a question the list wasn't asking. And the gap between those two readings is not a detail — it is the entire difference between a security program and a compliance artifact.

## What It Is

The **OWASP Top 10** is a periodically-updated, data-driven awareness document listing the ten most critical categories of web application security risk. It is produced by the Open Worldwide Application Security Project, a nonprofit, from an analysis of real vulnerability data — the 2025 edition drew on more than 175,000 CVE records mapped to 248 underlying weakness types (CWEs) — combined with a practitioner survey for risks that don't show up well in scan data.

Three words in that definition are doing all the work, and missing any one of them produces the checklist error.

**Categories, not bugs.** Each entry is a _family_ — "Broken Access Control" is not a vulnerability, it's a bin holding dozens of distinct CWEs, from the missing ownership check above to path traversal to privilege escalation through a manipulated JWT. You cannot "fix" a category. You can only design and test against the failure mode it names.

**Risk, not exploit.** The ranking weighs how often a category appears, how exploitable it is, and how much damage it does when exploited. A category with few reported instances but catastrophic impact can outrank a common-but-shallow one. So the ordering is not "fix these first" in your system — it's "here is where the industry's damage concentrates."

**Awareness document, not standard.** This is the one OWASP itself is most insistent about, and the one most often ignored. The Top 10 is a starting point — a shared vocabulary and a floor. It is explicitly _not_ a checklist, not a certification, and not sufficient as a security program. The document that _is_ meant to be tested against, item by item, is the **ASVS** (Application Security Verification Standard), which is far longer and organized for exactly the verification job people try to force the Top 10 to do.

The neighbour worth disambiguating immediately, because it is now on the same page: the Top 10 is a *web application* risk list. There is a separate **OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications**, and the 2025 web list points to it rather than absorbing it — prompt injection lives there, not in the Injection category here. If your product has an AI feature, "we cover the OWASP Top 10" now silently means two lists, and Module 39.7 is where the second one lives.

## Recognizing the Problem

You are reading the list as a checklist — the failure this article is about — if:

- Your evidence of "addressing" a category is a header, a library, or a WAF rule, for a category whose real instances live in business logic.
- The work had a done state. Someone closed the epic.
- A scanner's clean report is treated as coverage. Scanners find the categories that look the same in every application (injection, headers, known-CVE dependencies) and are nearly blind to the ones that depend on what _your_ app is supposed to allow (access control, business-logic flaws, insecure design).
- Nobody can point to a test that asserts user A cannot read user B's data.
- "OWASP Top 10 compliant" appears in your own marketing or an upstream vendor's, as if it were a certification. It isn't one; there is no such certification.
- The list you worked from is from 2021 (or earlier), and nobody noticed the 2025 edition moved things, added two categories, and folded SSRF into another.
- Your threat model _is_ the Top 10 — rather than the Top 10 being a prompt that helped you build a threat model for your specific system.

The tell underneath all of these: security was treated as a property you can attain, rather than a property you have to keep spending to maintain.

## How It Works

### The list is a lens, applied per-endpoint

The correct use is mechanical and unglamorous. You do not ask "have we addressed Injection?" at the level of the application. You walk your actual attack surface — every endpoint, every input, every trust boundary — and at each one you ask the ten questions the categories represent:

```
  for each endpoint / input / trust boundary:
      A01  can a caller reach data or actions that aren't theirs?
      A02  is anything relying on a default, or an unset hardening control?
      A03  do we trust a dependency, build step, or artifact we didn't verify?
      A04  is data that should be protected in transit/at rest actually protected?
      A05  does untrusted input reach an interpreter (SQL, shell, template)?
      A06  did we design this flow without thinking about how it's abused?
      A07  can someone become a user they aren't (auth, session, recovery)?
      A08  can someone tamper with code or data we later trust (updates, deserialization)?
      A09  if this is attacked, would we see it — and would anyone be alerted?
      A10  what happens when this fails? Does it fail open?
```

The list's value is that it is a _memory aid against your own blind spots_. Left to intuition, a team checks for the failure modes it happens to think of. The Top 10 is the industry's aggregated memory of what teams reliably forget — and running all ten past each endpoint is how you catch the workspace-id bug, because A01 is the first question and "is this workspace theirs?" is what it forces you to ask.

### Why most of it can't be bought

Split the ten by where the fix actually lives, and the checklist illusion dissolves:

```
  fixable with configuration / libraries          fixable only in YOUR domain logic
  (bounded, has a done-ish state)                 (unbounded, per-feature, ongoing)
  ───────────────────────────────────             ────────────────────────────────
  A02  security headers, TLS config               A01  who is allowed to do what
  A03  pin & verify dependencies                  A06  designing a flow against abuse
  A04  use the right algorithms                   A07  your specific auth & recovery
  A05  parameterize / encode output               A09  what YOUR system should alert on
                                                   A10  how YOUR failures behave
```

The left column is real work, but it converges — you can get it done and mostly keep it done with automation. The right column never converges, because it is defined entirely by what your application is _supposed_ to permit, and only you know that. Every new feature adds new access-control decisions, new abuse cases, new failure modes. This is why "we addressed the Top 10 in Q2" is a category error: half the list is not a task, it's a standing obligation that grows with the product.

### The list moves, and the moves are the signal

The 2025 edition (the first revision since 2021) is worth reading not just for its current ordering but for _what changed_, because the deltas encode where the industry's pain migrated:

- **A01 Broken Access Control** stayed at #1, and **SSRF was folded into it.** Server-side request forgery used to be its own entry; the data said it's another instance of "the server can be made to reach something it shouldn't," so it moved in. The message: access control is not just about users reaching data — it's about your _server_ reaching resources on an attacker's behalf too (the webhook and link-preview problem from 17.1c).
- **A02 Security Misconfiguration** jumped from #5 to #2. As applications shifted their behaviour into cloud permissions, IaC templates, and config files, every unset flag became a live vulnerability. Configuration _is_ the application surface now.
- **A03 Software Supply Chain Failures** is new at #3, expanding the old "Vulnerable and Outdated Components." It had the _fewest_ raw occurrences but the _highest_ average exploit and impact scores — a small number of catastrophic events (the dependency you didn't audit, the compromised build step). This is the category most invisible to a "check my code" mindset, because the vulnerable code isn't yours.
- **Injection fell from #3 to #5.** Not because injection was solved, but because a generation of frameworks made parameterized queries and auto-escaping the default. This is the one category where the tooling genuinely moved the needle — evidence that the buyable fixes, when they become defaults, work.
- **A09 gained "Alerting"** — now _Security Logging and Alerting Failures_. The explicit point (which Module 19.3b was built around): logs nobody is paged on are expensive storage, not a detective control.
- **A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions** is new at #10 — failing open, swallowed errors, logic that does the wrong thing when something abnormal happens. The security framing of what Module 19.2a's fail-open routers and Module 18's "was this request processed?" were circling.

If your mental model of web security is the 2021 list, it is not wrong so much as _four years stale_ in exactly the places that moved most.

## When It's Appropriate

The Top 10 is the right tool when:

- **You need a shared vocabulary.** A team, a questionnaire, a pentest report, and a compliance auditor can all say "A01" and mean the same thing. That interoperability is genuinely valuable and is most of what the list is for.
- **You're bootstrapping a security practice** and need a defensible, industry-endorsed starting point for _what to look for_. Starting from the Top 10 beats starting from whatever the loudest engineer read last.
- **You're scoping a review or a threat-modeling session** and want a checklist of _prompts_ (not of fixes) to run past each part of the system.
- **You're triaging** and want a common severity intuition — a finding in A01 or A03 generally deserves faster attention than one in a low-impact category, absent other information.

The rest of Module 20 is organized to follow the list's spirit while respecting where the depth actually needs to go: the injection family and output encoding, the auth and session material (much of it already built in Module 4), secrets management (Module 3.4), the headers-and-config layer, and the design-level thinking that A06 demands. Each is a full article because each category is a family, not a line item.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **As a verification standard.** If your actual job is "prove this application meets a security bar," the Top 10 is too coarse and too short. Use the ASVS, which was built for that and has the granularity the Top 10 deliberately omits.
- **As your entire threat model.** A payments system, a healthcare product, and a multiplayer game have wildly different threats, and none of their most important risks are guaranteed to be on a general-purpose top-ten list. The list is a floor, not a model of _your_ adversary.
- **As a WAF procurement justification.** "OWASP Top 10 coverage" on a product datasheet almost always means the buyable half — it can pattern-match injection and some misconfiguration. It cannot know whether workspace 41 belongs to the caller. Buying the WAF is fine; believing it closed A01 is the checklist error wearing a purchase order.
- **For LLM-specific risk.** If the concern is prompt injection, training-data poisoning, or agent tool-abuse, this list is the wrong one — you want the LLM Top 10 (Module 39.7).
- **As a stick.** Turning all ten into mandatory tickets for a team that has no access-control test in place produces ten closed tickets and the same breach. The effort is better spent on the two or three categories that actually map to _your_ system's crown jewels.

## Trade-offs

Even used correctly, leaning on the Top 10 has costs worth naming:

- **It shapes attention toward the common, away from the specific.** By construction it reflects aggregate industry data. Your application's worst risk may be a domain-specific business-logic flaw that appears in no CWE bin — a discount that can go negative, a state machine that can be run backwards. The list will never prompt you to look there; only thinking about your own system will.
- **The ranking invites mis-prioritization.** #1 in the industry is not #1 in your system. Injection sitting at #5 does not mean your one unparameterized query matters less than a theoretical misconfiguration. The numbers describe a population, not your codebase (the same "aggregate is not your instance" error Module 16.3 warned about with metrics).
- **It creates a compliance surface that can be gamed.** The moment "addresses the Top 10" appears in a contract, the incentive shifts from being secure to being able to _say_ the sentence. The checklist reading is not a mistake of stupidity; it's the path of least resistance the contract language creates.
- **It goes stale between editions.** Four years passed between 2021 and 2025. A team that internalized one edition as _the_ list carries its blind spots — and the gaps between editions are exactly where new attack surface (supply chain, cloud config, LLMs) shows up first.
- **Ten is a memorable number, not a complete one.** Things fall off the bottom or get merged not because they stopped mattering but because ten is the format. SSRF didn't become less dangerous when it merged into A01.

## Alternatives

Not competitors so much as the other instruments in the same toolbox — reach for these when the Top 10's job isn't the job you have:

- **OWASP ASVS.** The verification standard. When you need per-requirement testable criteria, this is what the Top 10 is not.
- **OWASP API Security Top 10.** A parallel list specifically for APIs, where the access-control and object-level authorization failures (BOLA/BFLA — the workspace-id bug's proper names) dominate even more than in the web list. If your product is API-first, this is arguably the more relevant list.
- **OWASP LLM Top 10.** The AI-application counterpart (Module 39.7).
- **STRIDE / threat modeling.** A _generative_ technique rather than a list — you derive the threats specific to your design instead of matching against a fixed set. The right tool for A06 (Insecure Design), and complementary: threat-model the system, use the Top 10 to check you didn't miss a common category.
- **The CWE Top 25 Most Dangerous Software Weaknesses.** More granular, weakness-level rather than category-level, maintained by MITRE. Useful when you want the specific defect names underneath the OWASP bins.
- **Your own incident history.** The most relevant risk list you will ever have is the categories of thing that have actually bitten _you_. Weight it above any general list once you have enough of it.

## How to Achieve It

You don't "adopt the Top 10" in one step; you move from the checklist reading to the threat-model reading incrementally.

**The minimal first step:** pick the one category that maps to your worst-case breach — for most SaaS products that's A01, "a customer reads another customer's data" — and write a single automated test that asserts it can't happen for your most sensitive endpoint. One test. It converts the category from a slogan into something with a pass/fail.

**The path from there:** make that test a pattern. Every new endpoint that touches tenant data gets a "wrong tenant gets 404" test as part of its definition of done (the Module 4.3 permission-declaration idea, enforced in tests). Now A01 is being _maintained_, not _checked once_.

**Then widen, cheaply, on the buyable half:** turn on the automation that covers the convergent categories — dependency scanning in CI (A03), a security-header check (A02), SAST for injection patterns (A05). These have a done-ish state; get them running and mostly forget them.

**The signal it's time for the next step:** when the buyable half is automated and green, and you're still finding issues in review, that's the cue to invest in the unbuyable half — a real threat-modeling practice for new designs (A06), and deciding what your system should alert on (A09). Those are the categories that never finish, and they're where a maturing program spends its time.

The maturity ladder, in one line each:

```
  L1  the list is a slogan in a questionnaire answer
  L2  one test asserts your worst category can't happen
  L3  that test is a pattern every new endpoint inherits
  L4  buyable categories are automated in CI and stay green
  L5  unbuyable categories (design, alerting) have a standing practice, not a ticket
```

Most teams that think they're at L4 are at L1 with good tooling — green scanners over an application nobody has threat-modeled.

## Further Reading

- **OWASP Top 10:2025** (owasp.org/Top10) — read the Introduction and the "how the categories were built" methodology before the categories themselves. The methodology is what teaches you to read it as data rather than doctrine.
- **OWASP Application Security Verification Standard (ASVS)** — the standard the Top 10 is repeatedly mistaken for. Skim the table of contents once; it recalibrates your sense of how much the Top 10 leaves out.
- **OWASP API Security Top 10** — the API-specific list, where BOLA and BFLA (the object- and function-level authorization failures) are the headline items. If you ship APIs, read this alongside the web list, not instead of it.
- **OWASP LLM Top 10** — the AI-application risk list, for anything with a model in the request path. Cross-referenced from Module 39.7.
- **MITRE CWE Top 25** — the weakness-level view underneath the OWASP categories, when you need the specific defect names.
- **Adam Shostack, _Threat Modeling: Designing for Security_** — for the generative alternative to a fixed list, and the right foundation for the A06 (Insecure Design) work this module gets to later.
- Within this map: the access-control machinery in **Module 4.3** (RBAC, row-level security, the tenant-isolation bug), secrets in **3.4**, dependency and supply-chain integrity in **3.3**, logging and alerting as detective controls in **19.3b**, fail-open behaviour in **19.2a**, and the server-side-request-forgery case (now part of A01) in **17.1c**.

---

The habit to carry out of this article, and into the rest of the module: **a security list tells you where other people got hurt, not where you will.** Its job is to interrupt your blind spots, not to define your work. The moment a list has a "done" checkbox next to it, someone has stopped thinking — and the workspace-id bug is always in the half of the list that no checkbox can cover. Read every entry in the coming articles the same way: not "here is a bug to eliminate," but "here is a question to keep asking of every endpoint you will ever write."
