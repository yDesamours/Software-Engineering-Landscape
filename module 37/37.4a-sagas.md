# Sagas: Consistency Across Services Without a Distributed Transaction

_Module 37.4a — Event-Driven Architecture_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a vacation-rental platform with request-to-book listings. A booking touches four services, each with its own database:

- **calendar** holds the dates so nobody else can book them
- **payments** authorizes the guest's card, and captures the money once the booking is confirmed
- **hosts** asks the host to accept, and the host has 24 hours to answer
- **operations** schedules cleaning, and notifications sends the confirmation

In a monolith this would be one transaction. Here it can't be, and the team has discovered what that means one incident at a time.

**Dates held for bookings that don't exist.** The first version calls the services in sequence from the booking service: hold dates, then authorize the card. When the card is declined, the booking service returns an error to the guest. Nobody releases the dates. After three months, 1,100 listings show dates as unavailable that nobody booked, and hosts are asking why their calendars are full and their income isn't.

**A flow nobody can see.** To decouple the services, the team switches to events. `BookingRequested` makes calendar hold dates, `DatesHeld` makes payments authorize, `PaymentAuthorized` makes hosts notify the host, and each failure event makes someone else undo something. It works, mostly. But the flow now exists only across five codebases. Support can't answer "where is my booking?" A booking whose `HostDeclined` event was dropped by a consumer bug stays `PENDING` for eleven days, with the card authorization quietly expiring underneath it. Adding "instant book" for some hosts means changing four services and coordinating their deploys.

**A cleaner at an empty house.** Operations listens for `PaymentAuthorized` and schedules cleaning for the checkout date, to make scheduling easier. When the host declines the booking, the dates and the authorization are released, but nothing tells operations. A cleaner turns up at a property whose next guest never existed. The cleaning service had acted on a booking that was only halfway through.

**An undo that isn't one.** A host cancels two days after a booking was confirmed. The team's "rollback" refunds the card. But the capture has already settled, so the guest sees a charge and a refund on their statement, the platform pays processing fees twice, and the currency conversion costs the guest €11. The confirmation email can't be unsent either. What looked like a rollback was a set of new actions that partly make up for the old ones.

## What It Is

A **saga** is a sequence of local transactions, each committed in one service's own database, where each step that can be undone has a **compensating transaction**. If a later step fails, the saga runs the compensations for the completed steps, in reverse order, to reach an acceptable end state. The idea comes from Garcia-Molina and Salem's 1987 paper on long-lived database transactions, and it applies directly to services.

Three kinds of step matter:

- **Compensatable steps** can be semantically undone: release the dates, void the authorization.
- **The pivot** is the step after which the saga won't turn back: capturing payment and confirming the booking. If the pivot succeeds, the saga must finish. If it fails, the saga compensates.
- **Retriable steps** come after the pivot. They must eventually succeed, so they're retried until they do: schedule cleaning, send the confirmation.

And two ways to coordinate them:

- **Choreography:** each service reacts to the others' events. Nobody is in charge; the flow emerges from the subscriptions.
- **Orchestration:** one coordinator, usually owned by the service responsible for the business outcome, sends commands to each participant, records the saga's state, and decides what happens next, including compensation and timeouts.

Neighbors:

- **A distributed transaction** (two-phase commit) makes all steps commit or none, and holds locks across services while it decides. A saga gives up that isolation: other transactions can see its intermediate states. That trade is what lets each step commit on its own, and it's the source of the cleaning incident.
- **Compensation is not rollback.** A rollback makes it as if nothing happened. A compensation is a new business action that makes up for an earlier one: a refund, a release, an apology email. Its effects are visible, and sometimes costly.
- **Workflow engines** (Temporal, AWS Step Functions) are one way to build an orchestrator: they persist its state and timers for you. They're covered in 37.4b. This article builds the orchestrator from parts this module already has.

## Recognizing the Problem

- One user action updates several services or databases, and failures leave some updated and others not.
- There's a cleanup job for "orphaned" holds, reservations or authorizations.
- Records sit in `PENDING` indefinitely, and support resolves them by hand.
- Nobody can say, for a given order or booking, which steps have happened and which haven't.
- Adding a step to a business process means changing several services and coordinating deploys.
- A service acts on something that was later cancelled, because it saw it partway through.
- "Rollback" code issues refunds, credits or apologies, and nobody has written down what each one costs.
- A step can time out with an unknown outcome, and the code assumes it failed (36.3a).

## How It Works

### Order the steps around the pivot

```
 hold dates ──► authorize card ──► host approval ──► capture & confirm ──► schedule cleaning ──► send confirmation
 (release)      (void)             (withdraw)         ▲ PIVOT             (retry until done)   (retry until done)
      ◄──────────── compensations run in reverse ─────┘
                    if anything before the pivot fails
```

A few rules shape the order:

- **Steps that are likely to fail, and cheap to undo, go first.** A declined card is common and voiding it costs nothing, so authorization comes early. Holding dates first also means a guest isn't charged for dates someone else just took.
- **Nothing that can't be undone goes before the pivot.** The confirmation email, a message to the host saying "you're booked," scheduling the cleaner: all after. That's the fix for the cleaning incident, by ordering rather than by code.
- **Choose the pivot deliberately.** It's the business decision point. Here it's capturing payment, because once money has moved, the fair thing is to complete the booking, and any later cancellation is a new process with its own rules (a cancellation policy, a refund), not a compensation.
- **Prefer cheaper compensations by choosing cheaper actions before the pivot.** Authorizing and later voiding costs nothing; capturing and later refunding costs fees, and that was the fourth incident. Many "rollbacks" become cheap once the expensive action is moved to the pivot.

### Compensations are business actions

A compensation must be:

- **Idempotent**, since it may run more than once (37.2a).
- **Able to run when there's nothing to undo.** A step can time out while still in flight, so the compensation may arrive before the step does, or instead of it. Releasing dates that were never held must succeed quietly.
- **Final against its step.** Once compensated, a late-arriving step for the same saga must be refused, not applied. Otherwise the dates get held after they were released, and nothing will ever release them again. The Implementation shows one table that handles all three.
- **Eventually successful.** A compensation that fails leaves the saga worse off than either end state. Retry it with backoff for as long as necessary (10.2a). If it truly can't succeed, such as a void rejected because the authorization already settled, escalate to a different compensation (a refund) or to a person, with an alert. Never let it fail silently.

Some actions have no compensation, only an apology. An email already sent can be followed by another email. Write these down; they're one reason to put steps after the pivot.

### No isolation, so design for what others see

While a saga is in progress, its completed steps are visible to everyone. Other processes can read and act on a booking that's halfway through. The standard countermeasures, from Lars Frank's work on relaxed isolation and popularized by Chris Richardson:

- **Semantic lock:** mark in-progress state explicitly, such as a hold with status `PENDING`, and make other processes respect it. The calendar shows pending dates as unavailable to new guests, but operations must not schedule cleaning for them, and reports must not count them as income. The lock is only as good as the number of consumers that know about it.
- **Reorder steps:** move actions others can see or act on to after the pivot, as with cleaning and emails. It's usually the cheapest countermeasure.
- **Commutative updates:** design updates so their order doesn't matter, such as a balance adjusted by +X and −X rather than set to a value.
- **Reread before acting:** a step checks that what it depends on hasn't changed since the saga started, such as the nightly price, and fails, triggering compensation, if it has.
- **Version or by-value checks:** record versions, and choose stricter handling (or a real transaction) for high-risk values.

Isolation anomalies aren't hypothetical in sagas; they're the default. The design question is which ones matter for this business, and which countermeasure each gets.

### Choreography

Each participant listens for the events it cares about and publishes its own:

```
BookingRequested ─► calendar ─► DatesHeld ─► payments ─► PaymentAuthorized ─► hosts ─► HostApproved ─► …
                                   ▲                          │ AuthorizationFailed
                                   └──── calendar releases ◄──┘
```

Its strengths are real: no central component, services that don't know about each other, and short flows that are easy to add. It suits two or three steps with a simple failure path.

Its costs grow with every step:

- **The flow exists nowhere.** Understanding it means reading every participant's subscriptions.
- **State is scattered.** "Where is this booking?" means querying five services, or maintaining a separate projection that reconstructs each saga from events (a **process monitor**). If you use choreography beyond a couple of steps, build that projection.
- **Timeouts belong to nobody.** "Host hasn't answered in 24 hours" isn't an event any service will publish.
- **Dependencies become cycles.** Calendar listens to payments' events, and payments to calendar's.
- **Changes spread.** Instant booking, which skips host approval, changed four services.

### Orchestration

An orchestrator owns the saga's state and the sequence:

```
             ┌───────────── booking saga (a row per booking) ─────────────┐
             │ state: RUNNING  step: authorize_card  deadline: …  attempt │
             └───────┬──────────────────────────────▲─────────────────────┘
       command via outbox                        reply
                     ▼                              │
       calendar · payments · hosts · operations · notifications
```

- **The saga's state is a row,** updated in the same transaction as the outgoing command is written to the outbox (37.2a). A crash at any point resumes from the row.
- **Participants reply** with success or failure, carrying the saga ID and step. The orchestrator applies each reply as a state transition, checked against the row's current state so duplicates and stale replies are ignored.
- **Timeouts are the orchestrator's job.** Each waiting step has a deadline in the row, and a scanner treats expired deadlines as failures.
- **Support can answer questions.** "Where is my booking?" is one row.
- **New steps change one place.** Instant booking is a condition in the orchestrator that skips host approval.

Its risk is that the orchestrator absorbs other services' logic. Keep it to sequencing and decisions about the flow. Whether a card can be authorized, or dates are free, stays with the participant that owns the rule.

A common arrangement uses both: orchestration inside one business process, and choreography between processes. The booking saga is orchestrated; when it completes, it publishes `BookingConfirmed` as an integration event (37.2c), and anyone may react.

### Timeouts, human steps and races

The host has 24 hours. The orchestrator stores the deadline, and a periodic scan picks up sagas past it and starts compensation. That creates a race: the host clicks "Accept" at the last moment, just as the timeout fires. Two rules resolve it:

- **Only one transition wins.** Both the approval reply and the timeout are conditional updates on the saga row, "if still waiting on host approval for attempt 3." The second finds the state already changed and does nothing.
- **The participant hears the verdict.** The compensation for host approval withdraws the request, so the host's app shows "This request expired" instead of confirming a booking that no longer exists. A late approval that reaches the hosts service after the withdrawal is refused there.

### Unknown outcomes

A step that times out may or may not have happened (36.3a). The orchestrator has two safe options: retry the same step with the same idempotency key, which the participant recognizes if it already happened, or compensate, which is safe because compensations handle "nothing to undo" and block the late step. What it must never do is treat "no reply" as "didn't happen" and carry on as if it hadn't.

## When It's Appropriate

- **One business outcome spans services that each own their data,** and a partial result is worse than either full outcome, such as held dates with no booking, or a charge with no reservation.
- **Steps take long,** minutes to days, especially with human decisions such as host approval, so no transaction could stay open that long.
- **Some steps call external systems** (payment providers, carriers) that can't take part in a transaction anyway.
- **Choose orchestration** once there are more than about three steps, timeouts, or anyone needs to ask "where is this one?" Choose choreography for two or three steps with a simple failure path.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **The data could live in one database.** A saga between two services owned by one team, which always change together, may mean the boundary is in the wrong place (2.1, 23.5). Merging them turns a saga back into a transaction.
- **Only one step can fail, and the rest can simply be retried.** Commit locally, then retry the remaining steps until they succeed, with the outbox and idempotent consumers (37.2a). That's forward recovery with no compensation, and it's enough.
- **Low volume, with a person in the loop anyway.** A dozen corporate onboarding cases a week can be a checklist and a review queue.
- **Reconciliation already covers it.** If a periodic job compares desired and actual state and repairs differences (10.2a), orphaned holds may be cheaper to sweep than to prevent.

## Trade-offs

- **Every failure path is code.** Each compensation needs writing, testing and maintaining, including tests where it runs before its step, twice, or after a partial failure.
- **No isolation.** Intermediate states are visible, and every consumer of those states has to understand semantic locks.
- **Compensations have real costs:** fees, emails, confused customers. Some effects can't be undone at all.
- **Eventual consistency is user-visible.** A booking is "pending" for seconds or a day, and the product has to explain that state.
- **The orchestrator is a dependency** on the critical path of the business process, and a place where logic tends to accumulate.
- **Hard to test end to end.** Races between replies, timeouts and compensations appear under load, not in single-user tests.
- **Operational tooling becomes necessary:** listing stuck sagas, retrying a step, forcing compensation, all by hand from an admin screen.

## Alternatives

- **One transaction.** Redraw the boundary so the data is in one database. Often right, rarely considered.
- **Two-phase commit.** Atomic and isolated, at the cost of blocking and of participants that support it. Most modern services and all external APIs don't.
- **Reservations (try, confirm, cancel).** Each participant offers a reservation that expires on its own if not confirmed. The dates hold and the card authorization are already this shape. When every step can be a reservation, a crashed coordinator leaves nothing permanent behind: the reservations simply expire. It's a saga where the compensation is built in as a timeout.
- **Forward recovery only,** described above, when there's no real failure point before the end.
- **A workflow engine** (37.4b). Durable state, timers, retries and compensation provided by the platform, with the flow written as ordinary code.
- **A human review queue** for rare, high-value, irregular cases.

## How to Achieve It

1. **Map the process.** List each step, what undoes it and what that costs, and classify each as compensatable, pivot or retriable. Reorder so nothing irreversible comes before the pivot. This alone fixed two of the four incidents.
2. **Make participants safe.** Idempotent steps keyed by saga ID and step, compensations that succeed when there's nothing to undo, and a guard that refuses a step after its compensation. _Next when:_ every participant has been tested with duplicated, reordered and early compensations.
3. **An orchestrator as a table.** A saga row per booking, transitions applied with conditional updates, commands through the outbox, replies through idempotent handlers, and deadlines scanned by a job. _Next when:_ it runs in production for the main flow.
4. **Countermeasures and visibility.** Semantic locks where intermediate states are visible, alerts on sagas stuck in any state beyond their expected time, and admin actions to retry or compensate.
5. **Consider a workflow engine** (37.4b) when there are many saga types, long-running flows with many timers, or the table-based orchestrator is growing a scheduler, a retry system and a history view of its own.

## Implementation

Go, with Postgres. The orchestrator is a state machine stored in a table, and Go's explicit transaction handles keep "state change and outgoing command in one commit" visible in every function, as in 37.2a.

### The saga, as data

```go
type Step struct {
	Name      string
	Do        string        // command sent to a participant
	Undo      string        // compensating command; empty after the pivot
	Timeout   time.Duration // how long to wait for a reply
	Pivot     bool
}

var booking = []Step{
	{Name: "hold_dates",        Do: "calendar.Hold",         Undo: "calendar.Release",      Timeout: 30 * time.Second},
	{Name: "authorize_card",    Do: "payments.Authorize",    Undo: "payments.Void",         Timeout: 30 * time.Second},
	{Name: "host_approval",     Do: "hosts.RequestApproval", Undo: "hosts.Withdraw",        Timeout: 24 * time.Hour},
	{Name: "capture",           Do: "payments.Capture",      Pivot: true,                   Timeout: time.Minute},
	{Name: "schedule_cleaning", Do: "operations.Schedule",   Timeout: time.Minute}, // retried until done
	{Name: "send_confirmation", Do: "notify.Confirmed",      Timeout: time.Minute},
}
```

Reading down the list is reading the business process, which is the thing choreography couldn't offer. Everything after `capture` has no `Undo`: by construction, nothing irreversible can be compensated, because nothing irreversible comes before the pivot.

### Applying a reply

```go
func (o *Orchestrator) OnReply(ctx context.Context, r Reply) error {
	return pgx.BeginFunc(ctx, o.db, func(tx pgx.Tx) error {
		s, err := lockSaga(ctx, tx, r.SagaID) // SELECT ... FOR UPDATE
		if err != nil {
			return err
		}
		if r.Step != s.Step || r.Attempt != s.Attempt || s.Done() {
			return nil // duplicate, stale, or lost a race with a timeout: ignore
		}

		switch {
		case s.State == Running && r.OK:
			return o.advance(ctx, tx, s) // next step, or Completed after the last

		case s.State == Running && s.Step <= pivotIndex(booking):
			// A step up to and including the pivot failed: undo completed steps, newest first.
			return o.beginCompensation(ctx, tx, s, r.Reason)

		case s.State == Running: // past the pivot: forward only
			return o.retryLater(ctx, tx, s, r.Reason)

		case s.State == Compensating && r.OK:
			return o.compensateNext(ctx, tx, s) // previous step's Undo, or Compensated when none left

		default: // a compensation failed: it must eventually succeed
			return o.retryLater(ctx, tx, s, r.Reason)
		}
	})
}
```

Each branch ends in the same kind of write: update the saga row (state, step, attempt, deadline) and insert the next command into the outbox, in one transaction. The command's idempotency key is `sagaID/step/attempt/Do` (or `/Undo`), so a participant that sees it twice acts once.

The `Attempt` check is what resolves races. A timeout, a retry and a late reply all refer to a specific attempt, and whichever updates the row first moves it on. The others arrive, find a different attempt, and are ignored.

`retryLater` for a failed compensation increments the attempt and sets the next deadline with backoff (10.2a). A compensation still failing after, say, an hour moves the saga to `NeedsAttention`, which pages someone, rather than retrying forever in silence.

### Deadlines

```go
// Runs every few seconds on each orchestrator instance.
func (o *Orchestrator) ExpireDeadlines(ctx context.Context) error {
	rows, err := o.db.Query(ctx, `
		SELECT saga_id, step, attempt FROM sagas
		WHERE state IN ('running', 'compensating') AND deadline < now()
		ORDER BY deadline
		LIMIT 100
		FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`)
	if err != nil {
		return err
	}
	for _, t := range collectTimeouts(rows) {
		// A timeout is a failure reply for this exact attempt, and loses to any real reply.
		_ = o.OnReply(ctx, Reply{SagaID: t.SagaID, Step: t.Step, Attempt: t.Attempt,
			OK: false, Reason: "timeout"})
	}
	return nil
}
```

`SKIP LOCKED` fits here, unlike in the ordered relay of 37.2a: sagas are independent, so several scanners can share the work. For host approval, the timeout starts compensation. For a 30-second step whose outcome is unknown, compensating is safe, because the participant's guard below makes a late step harmless.

### The participant's side: one table for idempotency and the late-step guard

```go
// calendar service
func (c *Calendar) Hold(ctx context.Context, tx pgx.Tx, cmd HoldCmd) (Reply, error) {
	tag, err := tx.Exec(ctx, `
		INSERT INTO holds (saga_id, listing_id, dates, status)
		VALUES ($1, $2, $3, 'held')
		ON CONFLICT (saga_id) DO NOTHING`, cmd.SagaID, cmd.ListingID, cmd.Dates)
	if err != nil {
		return failed(cmd, "dates_unavailable"), nil // the exclusion constraint below said no
	}
	if tag.RowsAffected() == 0 {
		return c.replyFromExisting(ctx, tx, cmd) // a repeat, or already released: report that
	}
	return ok(cmd), nil
}

func (c *Calendar) Release(ctx context.Context, tx pgx.Tx, cmd ReleaseCmd) (Reply, error) {
	// Works whether the hold happened, hasn't arrived yet, or was already released.
	_, err := tx.Exec(ctx, `
		INSERT INTO holds (saga_id, listing_id, dates, status)
		VALUES ($1, $2, $3, 'released')
		ON CONFLICT (saga_id) DO UPDATE SET status = 'released'`,
		cmd.SagaID, cmd.ListingID, cmd.Dates)
	return ok(cmd), err
}
```

The row keyed by saga ID carries all three compensation properties from How It Works:

- **Idempotent:** a repeated `Hold` or `Release` finds the row and reports what's there.
- **Safe when there's nothing to undo:** `Release` before `Hold` inserts a `released` row.
- **Final:** a `Hold` arriving after that finds the row and doesn't hold. The dates can't be held after they were released.

The semantic lock lives in the same table. A Postgres exclusion constraint on `(listing_id, daterange)` for rows whose status is `held` or `confirmed` makes overlapping holds impossible, however many sagas run at once. Other services read `held` as "pending, not a booking", which is exactly what operations failed to understand in the cleaning incident, and why cleaning now happens after the pivot anyway.

## Further Reading

- **Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem, "Sagas"** (SIGMOD 1987). The original: long-lived transactions as sequences of local transactions with compensations. Short and still clear.
- **Chris Richardson, _Microservices Patterns_** (Manning, 2018), chapter 4, and the Saga pages on microservices.io. Step classification (compensatable, pivot, retriable) and the isolation countermeasures.
- **Lars Frank and Torben U. Zahle, "Semantic ACID properties in multidatabases using remote procedure calls and update propagations"** (_Software: Practice and Experience_, 1998). The source of semantic locks and the other countermeasures.
- **Caitie McCaffrey, "Distributed Sagas: A Protocol for Coordinating Microservices"** (talk, 2017). Sagas in services, with a precise treatment of what compensations must guarantee.
- **Pat Helland, "Memories, Guesses, and Apologies"** (2007). Why real businesses run on compensations and apologies rather than atomicity.
- **Bernd Rücker, _Practical Process Automation_** (O'Reilly, 2021). Orchestration vs choreography in practice, and where each one fails.
- **Microsoft Azure Architecture Center:** the "Saga" and "Compensating Transaction" pattern pages.
- **In this collection:** 2.1 and 23.5 (whether the boundary belongs there at all), 10.2a (retries), 17.1 (idempotency keys), 24.4 (erasure as a saga), 36.3a (unknown outcomes), 37.2a (outbox and idempotent consumers), 37.2c (integration events vs commands), 37.4b (workflow engines).

A **Java** sibling with Axon Framework's saga support would be worth writing. Axon associates events with saga instances by property, persists saga state, and schedules deadlines for you, which is the table and the scanner above, managed by a framework. The instructive part is what it leaves to you: compensation ordering, the late-step guard in participants, and the question of whether a handler is sequencing the flow or quietly taking over another service's rules. Not yet written.
