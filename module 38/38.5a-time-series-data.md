# Time-Series Data: Chunks, Columns, Rollups, and the Arithmetic of Time

_Module 38.5a — Advanced Database Techniques_

## The Problem

Suppose you run an energy-monitoring platform for commercial buildings. There are 60,000 smart meters in 9,000 buildings. Each meter reports once a minute: current power in kW, and a cumulative energy counter in kWh. Customers get dashboards: today, the last 30 days, the last 12 months. They also get a monthly report that their finance team reconciles against the utility bill.

Version one is an ordinary Postgres table:

```sql
CREATE TABLE readings (
  meter_id  bigint      NOT NULL,
  ts        timestamptz NOT NULL,
  power_kw  real,
  kwh_total double precision,
  PRIMARY KEY (meter_id, ts)
);
```

That's 1,000 rows a second, 86 million a day and about 10 GB a day with the index. Over the first year and a half, five things go wrong.

**Month 9: inserts slow down.** Inserts that took about 1 ms now take 15 to 40 ms at peak. The primary-key index is 900 GB. Each new row belongs to a different meter, so consecutive inserts land on leaf pages all over the index. Once the index no longer fits in memory, most inserts wait on a disk read.

**Monday, 09:00: the 12-month chart takes the database down.** A building with 40 meters has 21 million raw rows for the year. The chart takes 11 seconds per building, and at 09:00 hundreds of facility managers open it at once.

**Month 11: the rollup table disagrees with the utility bill.** The team adds an hourly rollup table storing each meter's average power per hour. The monthly view averages those hourly averages, and "peak" is the highest hourly average. Customers' utilities charge for peak *demand*, measured as the highest 15-minute average. That's 480 kW for one building, but an hour smooths it to 310, so the platform's peak never matches the bill. The daily totals are also cut at UTC midnight, so a New York customer's "Tuesday" runs from 20:00 Monday to 20:00 Tuesday.

**Month 12: the rollups are permanently wrong.** Gateways buffer readings when a building's internet drops and upload them when it returns, sometimes six hours later. The hourly job only recalculates the last two hours. A Chicago building shows zero consumption from 02:00 to 09:00 on March 3, and will keep showing it. The invoice for that month is too low.

**Month 13: one customer gets a credit of 1.28 million kWh.** A meter is replaced, and its cumulative counter drops from 1,284,300 to 12. Daily consumption is computed as the last reading minus the first, which comes to −1,284,288 kWh. The billing export treats the negative consumption as a credit and issues it.

The same month, retention starts. The nightly `DELETE` of data older than 13 months removes 86 million rows, takes five hours, and leaves bloat behind it.

The first two incidents are about storage. The last three are about arithmetic. A database built for time-series data fixes the storage problems for you, but nothing fixes the arithmetic except understanding it.

## What It Is

**Time-series data** is a sequence of measurements, each tied to a moment and to a **series**: a meter, a sensor, a ticker symbol, a user's usage counter. It has several properties that ordinary business data lacks, and every technique in this article relies on one of them:

- **Append-mostly.** New readings are inserted, and old ones are rarely updated.
- **Arrives roughly in time order**, with some readings late.
- **Queried by time range**, usually within some set of series, and **aggregated** far more often than it's read row by row.
- **Loses value with age.** Last hour's readings are queried constantly. Last year's are read as monthly totals, if at all.
- **Large.** Volume grows with the number of series × frequency × time, and keeps growing.

A **time-series database** is a storage engine arranged around those properties. It can be an extension to Postgres (TimescaleDB), a dedicated engine (InfluxDB), or a columnar analytics database used for time series (ClickHouse). The techniques are the same across products: partition by time, store older data by column, precompute rollups, and delete by dropping whole partitions.

Two related topics are covered elsewhere:

- **Operational metrics** (request rates, latencies) are also time series, but they live in a monitoring system with tight limits on how many series you can have (Module 16, Module 19). This article covers time series that are *product data*: readings customers see, bill on and export.
- **Downsampling a chart to fit its pixels** (9.3b) is a display technique. The rollups here are storage. They decide which questions can still be answered once the raw data is deleted.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Insert latency grows as the table grows, even though the insert rate hasn't changed.
- One index is larger than the server's memory, and its key starts with a series ID.
- Retention runs as a `DELETE` that takes hours, and bloat follows it.
- Dashboards over long ranges scan millions of raw rows each time they load.
- A rollup table stores averages, and something averages those averages.
- Your numbers disagree with a customer's own source: a utility bill, an exchange, a bank statement.
- Daily totals are cut at UTC midnight for customers who live elsewhere.
- Readings arrive late, and nothing recalculates the aggregates that already covered their time range.
- A cumulative counter has ever gone backwards, or a consumption figure has been negative.
- "Current value per device" is a query over the history table.
- Gaps in the data show up as zeros on charts.

## How It Works

### Partition by time

Split the table into **chunks**, one per time interval (a day or a week, say), so each chunk has its own indexes:

```
readings
 ├── chunk 2026-09-22   (compressed, read-only)
 ├── chunk 2026-09-23   (compressed)
 ├── chunk 2026-09-24   (row store, recent)
 └── chunk 2026-09-25   ← every insert lands here; its indexes fit in memory
```

Three problems from the incidents go away:

- **Inserts touch only the newest chunk's indexes.** Those indexes are small and stay in memory, so month 9's random disk reads disappear.
- **Retention drops whole chunks.** Dropping a table takes the same time whether it holds one row or 86 million, and leaves no bloat. This is 23.2b's monthly-partition point, done automatically.
- **Queries skip chunks.** A query for "last 24 hours" never opens older chunks.

**Chunk size matters.** The usual guidance is that the chunk currently being written, plus its indexes, should fit in about a quarter of memory. Chunks that are too big bring back month 9's problem. Chunks that are too small leave the query planner with thousands of partitions to consider.

TimescaleDB calls a table partitioned this way a **hypertable** and manages chunks for you. Native Postgres partitioning plus `pg_partman` does the same with more setup. Either way, a **BRIN index** on the time column (a tiny index that records the minimum and maximum time for each block of the table) often replaces a large B-tree for range scans, because rows are stored in roughly time order.

Time partitioning concentrates writes on the newest chunk. On one server that's what you want: all the hot data is together. Across many servers, it's 38.2a's hot-spot problem, since every write goes to the node holding "now". That's why distributed time-series stores partition by series hash first and by time within each series.

### Store older data by column

Once a chunk stops receiving writes, rewrite it in **columnar** form. Each column is stored separately, grouped by series and sorted by time. Each column can then use an encoding suited to its data:

```
timestamps  every 60 s      → delta-of-delta: mostly zeros, a few bits per value
power_kw    slowly changing → XOR of consecutive floats (Gorilla): mostly leading zeros
meter_id    repeated        → one value per segment
```

Compression of 10 to 20 times is normal. Queries read only the columns they need, and an aggregation over compressed columns can be faster than one over rows. TimescaleDB calls this the **columnstore**. ClickHouse and InfluxDB 3 (which writes Parquet files to object storage) store everything by column.

The cost is that changing compressed data is expensive: an update, a late insert or a GDPR deletion in a compressed chunk means decompressing and recompressing part of it. So compress only chunks older than your **late-data window**, the longest delay you'll accept for a reading.

### Rollups: store what can be combined

A **rollup** precomputes aggregates per bucket (every 15 minutes per meter, say) so long-range queries read thousands of rows instead of millions. The rule the month 11 incident broke is simple: **store values that can be combined, not final answers.**

```
bucket A: 4 readings, avg 100      bucket B: 1 reading, avg 20
avg of avgs:      (100 + 20) / 2         = 60      ← wrong
from sum & count: (400 + 20) / (4 + 1)   = 84      ← right
```

| You want | Store per bucket | Combine buckets with |
|---|---|---|
| average | sum, count | Σsum / Σcount |
| peak or minimum | max, min | max of maxes, min of mins |
| totals of events | count or sum | sum |
| consumption from a counter | the increase within the bucket (see below) | sum |
| percentiles | a sketch (t-digest, UDDSketch) | merge the sketches |
| distinct count | a HyperLogLog sketch | merge the sketches |
| value at a moment | first and last value, with their timestamps | earliest first, latest last |

A percentile of percentiles, like an average of averages, has no meaning. Sketches are the compact, combinable form of a distribution. They give up a small, bounded amount of accuracy in exchange for being able to merge.

**Bucket size is a product decision.** A rollup can't answer questions about finer intervals than its buckets. The month 11 peak demand needed 15-minute buckets, because that's the interval the utility bills on. Choose the bucket from the finest question anyone will ask about data older than your raw retention.

**Time-weighted averages** matter when samples are irregular. A thermostat that reports only when the temperature changes might send 200 readings during one busy hour and a single reading for a quiet day. Averaging the samples overweights the busy hour. Weight each value by how long it was in effect instead.

### Counters: increase, not difference

A cumulative counter (energy in kWh, bytes sent, total requests) only goes up, until the device resets: a replacement, a firmware update, an overflow. **Consumption over a period is the sum of increases between consecutive readings**, where any decrease is a reset and counts as an increase from zero:

```
readings:  1,284,280   1,284,300   12   40
increase:          +20              +12  +28      = 60, not 40 − 1,284,280
```

Two consequences follow:

- **Increases between buckets can be lost.** A rollup that computes `last − first` inside each bucket misses the step from the last reading of one bucket to the first reading of the next. The robust fix is to compute each reading's increase over the previous reading when it's ingested, and store it as its own column. After that, consumption is a plain `sum`, which combines across buckets correctly. The Implementation does this.
- **A reset and a missed interval look alike.** If a meter's reading fell, it was reset. If readings are missing, the increase across the gap is spread over time you have no readings for. Record which case it was, and don't let one reading's arithmetic overrule another data source (like a meter-swap record).

Prometheus's `increase()` and `rate()` handle resets in the same way.

### Late data and the refresh window

Rollups are derived data (23.2b), and late data makes them stale without anyone noticing, as in the month 12 incident. There are two parts to handling it:

- **Track which buckets changed.** Postgres materialized views recompute everything on refresh. TimescaleDB's **continuous aggregates** record which time ranges received inserts, updates or deletes, and recompute only those. A plain rollup job has to do this bookkeeping itself.
- **Declare a late-data window, and decide what happens beyond it.** Recalculate buckets up to, say, three days back. Readings older than that either trigger an explicit rebuild of the affected range, or get recorded as a data-quality event that a person reviews. Silently dropping them, or silently accepting them without recalculating, just produces the month 12 incident on a different date.

Three limits must be consistent with each other:

```
late-data window  ≤  compress after  <  raw retention
      3 days            7 days           30 days
```

Recalculating a range means reading its raw data, so the window must be shorter than the time raw data is kept. It's best if it's also shorter than the time before chunks are compressed, so late inserts don't land in compressed chunks.

### Time zones and calendars

A "day" means the customer's day. That means their time zone, including daylight-saving days with 23 or 25 hours. Bucketing functions that accept a time zone (TimescaleDB's `time_bucket(interval, ts, 'America/New_York')`) handle this correctly.

Rollups stored in UTC can be combined into any customer's local days only if the rollup's buckets divide evenly into every UTC offset you serve. Hourly buckets work for New York and Lisbon. India is UTC+5:30 and Nepal is UTC+5:45, and those need 15-minute buckets. Choosing the bucket size once, from your list of supported time zones, avoids recomputing every rollup later.

### Gaps are not zeros

A meter that sent nothing for six hours didn't use zero energy. You don't know what it used. When filling gaps for display or export, pick a rule for each measurement and state it:

- **Leave gaps empty** (null). Honest, and the default.
- **Carry the last value forward**, for states that persist, such as a thermostat's set point.
- **Interpolate**, for smoothly changing quantities, clearly labeled as estimated.
- **Zero**, only for counts of events, where "no readings" really means "nothing happened".

### The current value is a separate table

"The latest reading for every meter" shouldn't search the history for each meter's newest row. Keep a small table with one row per series, updated on every insert. It serves live dashboards and alerts at negligible cost, and the Implementation uses it to compute counter increases at ingest. InfluxDB 3 has an in-memory **last value cache** for the same purpose.

### Retention in tiers

Each tier has its own retention period:

```
raw readings (1 min)         30 days     → investigations, exports, recalculating rollups
15-minute rollups            25 months   → dashboards, demand peaks, year-on-year comparisons
daily rollups                forever     → trends, reports
```

Each line is a promise about which questions can be answered, and each promise is permanent once the data is gone. After 30 days, nobody can ask about a single minute. After 25 months, nobody can ask about a 15-minute peak. The question for the product team isn't "how long do we keep data?" but "what will customers ask about last year's data?"

## When It's Appropriate

- **Sustained, high-volume inserts of measurements.** Hundreds or thousands of readings a second, from IoT and telemetry, energy and utility metering, market data, or vehicle and fleet tracking.
- **Long-range dashboards** over data that grows every day. The raw query gets slower every week, and a rollup makes it constant.
- **Usage-based billing** (15.1), where per-customer consumption is summed from events or counters and has to reconcile with an external source.
- **Retention requirements** that would otherwise mean deleting millions of rows a day.
- **Aggregations in the customer's calendar:** daily, weekly and monthly totals in their own time zone.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Modest event tables.** An audit log that grows by 50,000 rows a day is fine as a normal table with a time index for years. Add monthly partitions when retention becomes painful (23.2b), not before.
- **Operational metrics.** Request latencies and error rates belong in Prometheus or a managed monitoring service (Module 19). Building your own time-series store for them repeats work those tools already do.
- **Rollups before the raw query is slow.** A rollup is derived data that has to stay correct as definitions change and late data arrives. Add one when a measured query needs it.
- **A separate time-series database while Postgres is keeping up.** A second engine means two sets of operations, no joins with your relational data, and another pipeline. Postgres with time partitioning, and an extension if needed, handles a lot of volume.

## Trade-offs

- **Rollups can drift from the raw data.** Each one is correct only if late-data handling, refresh windows and definitions stay consistent. Changing a definition ("peak now means 15 minutes") means rebuilding from raw data, and only for periods where raw data still exists.
- **Retention can't be undone.** Every tier's deletion permanently removes the ability to answer some questions.
- **Compression makes changes expensive.** Backfills, corrections and erasure requests on compressed chunks cost far more than on row-stored data. Erasure deserves a tested procedure (34.1).
- **Time-zone and bucket choices get built into stored data.** Choosing a bucket that doesn't divide some offset means recomputing every rollup later.
- **Sketches are approximate.** A percentile from a t-digest is very close but not exact. It must never be the basis of an invoice.
- **Chunk sizing needs tuning.** Too large brings back insert slowdowns; too small adds planning overhead. Most platforms have sensible defaults, but they're defaults, not measurements of your workload.
- **Extensions depend on your hosting.** TimescaleDB's columnstore and continuous aggregates are under the Timescale License, so some managed Postgres providers offer only a reduced edition or none at all. Check before choosing.
- **A dedicated engine is a second system.** It has its own backups, upgrades and query language, relational joins require copying data between systems, and ingest has to write to both (37.2a).

## Alternatives

- **Plain Postgres partitioning** (native partitioning plus `pg_partman`, BRIN indexes, your own rollup tables and refresh jobs). There's nothing extra to license or host, but you write and maintain the late-data bookkeeping yourself.
- **TimescaleDB.** A Postgres extension that adds hypertables, the columnstore, continuous aggregates that track changes, retention policies, and time-zone-aware bucketing. It keeps your relational data and time series in one database with joins.
- **Columnar analytics databases** (ClickHouse, with its MergeTree tables, TTL-based retention and materialized views). They're very fast for large aggregations and ingestion. Updates and deletes are more awkward, and it's a separate system.
- **InfluxDB 3.** A time-series engine that writes Parquet to object storage and uses SQL (via DataFusion), with an in-memory last value cache and no secondary B-tree indexes. The open-source Core edition limits how many files a query may read. The default covers roughly 72 hours of data and is configurable. Compaction and longer-range query performance are in the commercial Enterprise edition.
- **Parquet on object storage with a query engine** (DuckDB, Athena, Trino) for cold data. It's cheap to keep for years and slow to query interactively. It suits the "forever" tier, or data kept only for audits.
- **Monitoring backends** (Prometheus, Mimir) for operational metrics, but not for product data customers see or pay for.

## How to Achieve It

**1. Measure before choosing.** Write down the ingest rate, row size, number of series, late-data distribution (how late is the 99th percentile?), and the three most important queries with the ranges they cover.

**2. Partition by time and retain by dropping.** Set a chunk interval sized to memory, then replace retention `DELETE`s with chunk drops.
_Move on when_ insert latency no longer depends on table size.

**3. Add a latest-value table** with one row per series, upserted on ingest. Move live dashboards and alerts to it.

**4. Handle counters at ingest.** Compute and store each reading's increase with reset detection.
_Move on when_ no consumption figure can be negative.

**5. Decide the late-data window**, then compress chunks older than it.

**6. Add rollups for the slow long-range queries.** Store combinable values, choose buckets by the finest question and your time zones, set the refresh window equal to the late-data window, and send readings later than that to an explicit rebuild process.
_Move on when_ rollup totals match raw totals in a scheduled comparison, and match the customer's own source.

**7. Set tiered retention with product sign-off.** List the questions each tier answers.

**8. Only when Postgres can't keep up, move to a dedicated engine.** Keep relational data where it is, and choose how data gets to the new engine (38.4a).

## Implementation

This implementation is SQL on Postgres with TimescaleDB. For time-series data, the decisions that matter are in the schema, the rollup definitions and the policies. Application code would only wrap these statements. Syntax is for recent TimescaleDB 2.x releases. Older releases create hypertables with `create_hypertable()` instead.

### Storage: chunks, columnstore, retention, and the latest value

```sql
CREATE TABLE readings (
  meter_id   bigint           NOT NULL,
  ts         timestamptz      NOT NULL,
  power_kw   real,
  kwh_total  double precision,          -- the meter's cumulative counter, as reported
  kwh_delta  double precision           -- the increase since the previous reading, computed at ingest
) WITH (
  tsdb.hypertable,
  tsdb.partition_column = 'ts',
  tsdb.chunk_interval   = '1 day',      -- today's chunk and its indexes fit well within memory
  tsdb.segmentby        = 'meter_id',   -- the columnstore groups by meter...
  tsdb.orderby          = 'ts DESC'     -- ...and sorts by time within each meter
);

CALL add_columnstore_policy('readings', after => INTERVAL '7 days');     -- beyond the 3-day late window
SELECT add_retention_policy('readings', drop_after => INTERVAL '30 days');

CREATE TABLE meter_latest (             -- one row per meter: live dashboards, alerts, and ingest
  meter_id  bigint PRIMARY KEY,
  ts        timestamptz NOT NULL,
  power_kw  real,
  kwh_total double precision
);
```

### Ingest: the counter handled where the previous value is known

```sql
-- One reading. In production, batch a gateway's upload with unnest($1::bigint[], …).
WITH prev AS (
  SELECT ts, kwh_total FROM meter_latest WHERE meter_id = $1 FOR UPDATE
), ins AS (
  INSERT INTO readings (meter_id, ts, power_kw, kwh_total, kwh_delta)
  SELECT $1, $2, $3, $4,
         CASE
           WHEN prev.ts IS NULL         THEN NULL        -- first reading: no increase to report
           WHEN $4 >= prev.kwh_total    THEN $4 - prev.kwh_total
           ELSE $4                                        -- counter went backwards: reset, count from zero
         END
  FROM (SELECT) AS one LEFT JOIN prev ON true
  WHERE prev.ts IS NULL OR $2 > prev.ts                   -- late readings take the slow path
  RETURNING ts, power_kw, kwh_total
)
INSERT INTO meter_latest (meter_id, ts, power_kw, kwh_total)
SELECT $1, ts, power_kw, kwh_total FROM ins
ON CONFLICT (meter_id) DO UPDATE
  SET ts = EXCLUDED.ts, power_kw = EXCLUDED.power_kw, kwh_total = EXCLUDED.kwh_total;
```

> **The late path is part of this design.** A reading older than the meter's latest one has no correct increase to compute here, because its predecessor and successor are both in the history table. It goes to a queue. A worker inserts it, recomputes `kwh_delta` for it and for the following reading, and lets the continuous aggregate's change tracking recalculate the affected bucket. If it's older than the 3-day window, the worker records a data-quality event and schedules a rebuild instead. This path is where the month 12 incident gets fixed, and it's rare enough to be slow.

> **A reset rule is a guess.** "The counter went down, so it was reset" is right for a meter swap and wrong for a meter reporting a corrected lower value. Where a separate record of meter replacements exists, the late path should check it, and a reset that coincides with no replacement should be flagged for a person to look at, not billed silently.

### Rollups: combinable values, refreshed within the late window

```sql
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW readings_15m WITH (timescaledb.continuous) AS
SELECT meter_id,
       time_bucket('15 minutes', ts) AS bucket,  -- divides every UTC offset we serve, including +5:30 and +5:45
       sum(power_kw)                 AS sum_kw,   -- an average is recomputed from sum and count, never stored
       count(power_kw)               AS n_kw,
       max(power_kw)                 AS max_kw,
       sum(kwh_delta)                AS kwh       -- consumption: a sum of increases, safe to combine
FROM readings
GROUP BY meter_id, bucket;

SELECT add_continuous_aggregate_policy('readings_15m',
  start_offset      => INTERVAL '3 days',       -- the late-data window: recalculated on every run
  end_offset        => INTERVAL '15 minutes',   -- don't materialize the bucket still being filled
  schedule_interval => INTERVAL '5 minutes');

SELECT add_retention_policy('readings_15m', drop_after => INTERVAL '25 months');
```

> **The refresh window must stay inside raw retention.** A refresh recomputes its window from raw data. If `start_offset` reached back further than the 30 days raw data is kept, a refresh would find no raw rows for the oldest days and replace their rollups with nothing. Keep the three limits in the order shown earlier: 3 days ≤ 7 days < 30 days.

### The customer's report, in the customer's days

```sql
SELECT time_bucket('1 day', bucket, 'America/New_York') AS day,     -- DST days get 92 or 100 buckets
       sum(kwh)                                          AS kwh,
       sum(sum_kw) / nullif(sum(n_kw), 0)                AS avg_kw,  -- from sum and count, weighted correctly
       max(sum_kw / nullif(n_kw, 0))                     AS peak_15m_kw, -- the utility's demand definition
       sum(n_kw)                                         AS readings     -- so gaps are visible, not zeros
FROM readings_15m
WHERE meter_id = $1 AND bucket >= $2 AND bucket < $3
GROUP BY day
ORDER BY day;
```

> **Peak demand is the maximum of 15-minute averages, not of instantaneous readings.** `max_kw` in the rollup is the highest single one-minute reading, which is a different figure, and still stored for alarms. The report computes peak demand from each bucket's average, which is what the utility bills. The month 11 incident was two definitions sharing one word. Naming the columns after the definitions (`peak_15m_kw`, not `peak`) keeps them from being mixed up again.

## Further Reading

- **Tiger Data (TimescaleDB) documentation:** hypertables and chunk sizing, hypercore and the columnstore, continuous aggregates (particularly refresh policies and their interaction with data retention), and `time_bucket` with time zones.
- **Pelkonen et al., "Gorilla: A Fast, Scalable, In-Memory Time Series Database"** (VLDB 2015). The source of delta-of-delta timestamps and XOR float compression, which most engines now use.
- **Ted Dunning and Otmar Ertl, "Computing Extremely Accurate Quantiles Using t-Digests"** (2019). How a small, combinable summary gives accurate percentiles.
- **Prometheus documentation:** metric types, and `rate()` and `increase()` with counter resets. The clearest description of counter semantics, even if you never run Prometheus.
- **PostgreSQL documentation:** table partitioning and BRIN indexes; **pg_partman** for managing partitions without an extension that requires a special license.
- **ClickHouse documentation:** MergeTree, TTL and materialized views, as a columnar alternative.
- **InfluxDB 3 documentation:** the data model, the last value cache, and query file limits in Core.
- **Martin Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, chapter 3,** on column-oriented storage and why it compresses and scans so well.
- **In this collection:** 23.2b (partitioning for retention, derived data), 38.2a (why time-partitioning is a hot spot across nodes), 38.4a (CDC into an analytics store), 9.3b (display downsampling and LTTB), Module 16 (metrics and cardinality), Module 19 (observability backends), 15.1 (usage-based billing), 34.1 (erasure in compressed data), 37.2a (writing to two stores).

A **Go** sibling would be worth writing for the ingest side: batching a gateway's upload into `COPY`, sending late readings to the slow path, and applying backpressure when the database falls behind. The difficulty there is throughput and ordering under load, which SQL alone can't show. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: for every stored aggregate, ask whether it can be combined, and what happens to it when a reading arrives an hour late. The storage problems usually show up first, but the arithmetic mistakes are the ones customers find in their invoices.
