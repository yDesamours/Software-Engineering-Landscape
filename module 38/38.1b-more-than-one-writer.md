# More Than One Writer: Multi-Leader, Leaderless, and Conflict Resolution

_Module 38.1b — Advanced Database Techniques_

## The Problem

Suppose you run the software for a group of 60 hotels. Each hotel has its own database server, because the front desk has to keep checking guests in when the internet connection drops, which happens weekly at some properties. The central system runs the online booking engine, the CRM and the loyalty program. Changes replicate in both directions, so every hotel and the center all accept writes.

It worked for a year. Then four tickets arrived in one quarter. None of them was accompanied by an error.

**Ticket 1: a correction that vanished.** At 14:03, the Lisbon front desk fixes the spelling of a guest's surname. At 14:00, the same guest had changed their phone number in the app, which wrote to the central system. After replication, both databases show the new phone number and the old, misspelled name. Two things went wrong. The replication compares whole rows, so edits to different columns still collided. And the Lisbon server's clock was seven minutes slow because the hotel's firewall blocked NTP, so its later edit was stamped 13:56 and lost.

```
real time    Lisbon (clock −7 min)                  Central
14:00                                               phone := +351…  stamped 14:00
14:03        surname := "Kowalczyk" stamped 13:56
   → replication: row stamped 14:00 is "newer"; the surname fix is discarded everywhere
```

**Ticket 2: points that disappeared.** A guest has 12,000 loyalty points. Checking out in Porto earns 500, and Porto writes a balance of 12,500. At the same moment, the center credits a 1,000-point promotion and writes 13,000. Each write replaces the balance, so one of them wins: 13,000. The 500 points are gone, and the guest notices.

**Ticket 3: one room sold twice.** Lisbon loses its internet connection for three hours on a Tuesday. The front desk sells room 412 for Friday to a walk-in guest. Meanwhile, the central booking engine sells room 412 for Friday online. Both systems checked that the room was free, and it was, in their own copy. When Lisbon reconnects, the two reservations replicate without any trouble, because they are different rows and nothing conflicts. Two guests arrive on Friday.

**Ticket 4: an erased guest comes back.** A guest asks for their data to be erased under GDPR, and the center deletes the profile. Porto was offline at the time, and before reconnecting it added a note to that guest's stay record. When the update arrives, the center's sync treats "row not found" as "create it." The erased person's profile exists again, partly filled in. This one becomes a compliance incident.

The replication did exactly what it was configured to do in all four cases. Each ticket reflects a decision about what happens when two writers disagree. Nobody made those decisions deliberately, so the defaults made them.

## What It Is

**Multi-leader replication** (also called multi-master or active-active) means more than one node accepts writes to the same data, and the nodes replicate to each other, usually asynchronously. **Leaderless replication** removes the leader role entirely: a client sends each write to several replicas and reads from several, and the replicas reconcile among themselves. Amazon's Dynamo, Cassandra and ScyllaDB work this way.

Both designs let every node accept writes locally, even when it's cut off, and both therefore allow **conflicts**: two writes to the same data where neither writer had seen the other. Handling conflicts involves two separate jobs:

- **Detection:** did these two writes overlap, or did one happen after the other?
- **Resolution:** given that they overlapped, what should the result be?

Most systems do both badly by default, and they fail at different moments. Detection fails quietly, when concurrent writes are mistaken for sequential ones. Resolution fails visibly, when the rule discards something someone needed.

Three related topics are covered elsewhere:

- **9.3c** applies the same theory (last-write-wins, CRDTs, choosing the unit of conflict) at keystroke scale, with a user watching the merge happen. This article is about records merged in the background.
- **24.2b** made the region-level decision. Its conclusion that partitioning writes by a home region avoids conflicts entirely is not repeated here, except where it's the fix.
- **Distributed SQL databases** (Spanner, CockroachDB, YugabyteDB) also accept writes on many nodes, but the nodes agree on an order before committing. There's nothing to resolve afterwards. That's the other answer to the same problem, and it appears under Alternatives.

## Recognizing the Problem

- More than one place accepts writes to the same table. That includes a "temporary" dual write during a migration, bidirectional replication, offline-capable edge servers and mobile sync.
- Edits disappear without an error, usually when two changes landed close together.
- A counter, balance or stock level no longer equals the sum of the events that should have produced it.
- After an outage, you find duplicates of things that should be unique, such as bookings, usernames or invoice numbers.
- Deleted records come back.
- Nobody can state the conflict-resolution rule. That means it's last-write-wins or arrival order, and nobody chose it.
- Some nodes' clock synchronization is unmonitored.
- Cassandra repairs don't reliably finish within `gc_grace_seconds`.
- You rely on a conditional write or a uniqueness check to protect data globally, and it's evaluated against the local copy.

## How It Works

### Where concurrent writes come from

**Multi-leader topologies.** Leaders connect to each other in one of three common shapes:

```
   star / hub                 ring                    all-to-all
      H1                   L1 ──▶ L2               L1 ◀──▶ L2
      │                    ▲       │                ▲ ╲   ╱ ▲
 H2 ─ C ─ H3               │       ▼                │   ╳   │
      │                    L4 ◀── L3               ▼ ╱   ╲ ▼
      H4                                           L4 ◀──▶ L3
```

Every change carries its **origin**, so a node doesn't send a change back to where it came from. Postgres does this with `origin = none` on subscriptions (16+), and MySQL does it with server IDs and GTIDs. In a ring or star, one broken node stops the flow for everyone downstream of it.

All-to-all topologies have a subtler failure. Changes travel different paths at different speeds, so a third node can receive an `UPDATE` before the `INSERT` it modifies:

```
L1: INSERT booking 77 ──────────────(slow link)─────────────▶ L3
L2:        (receives it fast) UPDATE booking 77 ──(fast)──▶ L3   ← row not there yet
```

Postgres logical replication records this as `update_missing` and **skips the update**. The row later arrives without the change, and the nodes disagree permanently. Ordering by origin isn't enough here. The system has to track what each change depended on.

**Leaderless quorums.** Each key lives on **N** replicas. A write succeeds once **W** of them acknowledge it. A read asks **R** of them and takes the newest answer.

```
N = 3, W = 2, R = 2          write v2 ──▶ A ✔  B ✔  C ✗ (down)
                             read     ──▶ A (v2)  C (v1)   → return v2, repair C
```

When **R + W > N**, every read overlaps with every successful write on at least one replica. That's the source of the claim that quorums are consistent, and it is weaker than it sounds:

- **Concurrent writes still conflict.** Two clients writing to the same key both reach W acknowledgments on different replicas. The quorum guarantees you'll *see* both versions. It doesn't choose between them.
- **Sloppy quorums break the overlap.** If the key's own replicas are unreachable, some systems accept the write on other nodes and hand it back later ("hinted handoff"). The write succeeded, but a read quorum can miss it until the handoff completes.
- **Failed writes aren't undone.** A write that reached only one replica is reported as failed, but it stays on that replica and can later show up in reads.
- **Overlap isn't linearizability.** A read that runs while a write is still in progress can return the new value, and the next read can return the old one.

Replicas that missed writes catch up in three ways:

- **Read repair:** a read that sees a stale replica fixes it on the way.
- **Hinted handoff:** writes held on stand-in nodes are delivered once the real replica returns.
- **Anti-entropy:** background comparison of Merkle trees (hash trees over key ranges) finds differences without comparing every row. In Cassandra, this is `nodetool repair`.

Repair isn't optional maintenance. The system's correctness depends on it, as the section on deletes shows.

### Detection: did these writes see each other?

**Timestamps can order writes, but they can't detect concurrency.** A timestamp puts every pair of writes in some order, so two writes that never saw each other still look sequential. Wall clocks also drift, as in Ticket 1: 7 minutes is extreme, but hundreds of milliseconds is ordinary on unmonitored servers. **Hybrid logical clocks** (HLC) combine wall-clock time with a counter. Whenever a node receives a timestamp ahead of its own clock, it moves its clock forward. So if a write saw an earlier write, its HLC is always larger, whatever the clocks say. CockroachDB and YugabyteDB use HLCs. They fix ordering between related writes, but they still pick one arbitrarily between concurrent ones.

**Version vectors do detect concurrency.** Each record carries one counter per node, counting that node's writes to the record. To compare two versions, compare the counters entry by entry:

```
v1 = {lisbon: 3, central: 5}
v2 = {lisbon: 3, central: 6}   → v2 ≥ v1 everywhere: v2 happened after v1. Take v2.
v3 = {lisbon: 4, central: 5}   → v2 vs v3: each is ahead somewhere. Concurrent. Resolve.
```

This is the question timestamps can't answer. Riak exposed it directly: concurrent versions came back to the client as **siblings** for the application to merge. The cost is metadata that grows with the number of nodes that have written to each record.

**Granularity decides what counts as a conflict.** With row-level detection, a phone number edit and a surname edit collide, as they did in Ticket 1. Cassandra stamps every cell (column value) separately, so writes to different columns of the same row never conflict. Reducing the unit of conflict is usually the cheapest improvement available, as 9.3c argued for documents.

**Invariants spanning rows are invisible to detection.** Ticket 3 had no conflict to detect. The two bookings were different rows, each valid on its own. The broken rule was "at most one booking per room per night," a rule about *combinations* of rows. No replication system detects that, because none of them knows the rule exists.

### Resolution strategies, from least to most semantic

**Arrival order.** Each node applies whatever arrives last. This is what Postgres bidirectional logical replication does with an `update_origin_differs` conflict: it applies the incoming change. Postgres 18 logs and counts these conflicts when `track_commit_timestamp` is on, but doesn't resolve them differently. Arrival order doesn't even converge. Lisbon applies Central's value after its own, Central applies Lisbon's after its own, and each node ends up holding the other's version.

**Last-write-wins (LWW).** Every node keeps the version with the highest timestamp and breaks ties the same way everywhere, for example by node ID. This converges, but the other write is lost, and clock drift decides which one survives. DynamoDB global tables (in their default eventual mode) and Cassandra use LWW, per item and per cell respectively. It's the right rule when "latest" really is what the data means: last-seen times, user preferences, cached values. For anything else, it silently discards data.

**Per-field LWW.** Apply LWW column by column, with a timestamp per column. Ticket 1 would have kept both edits (given a sane clock).

**Merge by data type (CRDTs).** Some data has a correct merge that doesn't depend on order:

- **Counters:** each node keeps its own increment total, and the value is the sum of all nodes' totals (a PN-counter tracks decrements separately).
- **Sets:** add-wins or remove-wins semantics, decided in advance.
- **Registers:** a single value, either LWW or keeping all concurrent values.

Redis Enterprise Active-Active and Riak's data types provide these as database features. The approach that fits relational schemas better is to **store operations instead of state**. A points ledger of `+500` and `+1,000` entries has only inserts, and inserts with distinct IDs never conflict. The balance is derived by summing the ledger. Ticket 2 disappears. This is the event-sourcing idea from 2.4, applied only to the data that needs it.

**Application merge.** Keep all concurrent versions and let code or a person decide. The resolver can live in a sync service (as in the Implementation), run when the record is read (Riak siblings), or feed a conflict queue for staff to handle. This is the only strategy that can apply business meaning to a conflict, and the only one that needs someone to maintain it.

### Deletes need memory

A delete leaves nothing behind to compare against. If a node that missed the delete later sends an update, the receiver sees an update for a missing row, and the system has to guess. Ticket 4's sync guessed "recreate it."

The fix is a **tombstone**: the delete is stored as a record with its own version, so it can win against an older update that arrives late. Tombstones impose two rules:

- **Choose delete-wins or update-wins deliberately, per table.** For an erasure request, delete-wins is a legal requirement. For a shopping cart, the original Dynamo paper describes the opposite choice (additions win over concurrent removals) and its known symptom: removed items reappear.
- **Tombstones must outlive the longest disconnection.** Cassandra removes tombstones after `gc_grace_seconds`, which defaults to 10 days. A replica that was down longer than that, or that repair didn't reach in time, still holds the old value. When it rejoins, it replicates that value back, and the deleted data returns (Cassandra calls this "zombie data"). The same limit applies to your hotels: a hotel offline longer than your tombstone retention must be rebuilt from scratch, not resynchronized.

Postgres 19 adds `retain_dead_tuples` to subscriptions, so the subscriber keeps recently deleted rows long enough to detect `update_deleted` conflicts. It's the same trade-off: correct detection in exchange for rows that stay on disk.

### Invariants: where resolution can't help

Uniqueness, capacity ("room 412 once per night"), and balances that must not go negative can't be enforced by independent writers that don't talk to each other. After the fact, the only options are to pick a loser or to apologize. There are four real options, and they can be combined:

- **Avoid the conflict through ownership.** Give each item exactly one writer. The hotel owns its room inventory. The central booking engine sells from an **allotment**, for example three rooms per night that the hotel has handed over, and must ask the hotel for more. This is escrow: each writer spends only what it holds, so two writers can't sell the same room.
- **Prevent the conflict at commit through certification.** MySQL Group Replication in multi-primary mode, and Galera, order every transaction across the group before it commits. A transaction that touched rows a concurrent transaction already changed is rolled back ("first committer wins"). This works within a region. Across continents, every commit pays the round trip (24.2b).
- **Prevent the conflict at commit through consensus.** Distributed SQL runs Raft or Paxos per data range. Any node can accept a write, but a majority agrees on its order before it commits.
- **Compensate afterwards.** Detect the overbooking and put things right: move the guest to a partner hotel, upgrade them, pay them. Hotels and airlines already work this way. Pat Helland describes the general approach as "memories, guesses, and apologies." It's a legitimate design, provided the apology is an actual workflow and not something discovered at the front desk on Friday.

One trap is worth naming. **A conditional write is evaluated against the local replica.** In DynamoDB global tables in eventual mode, "put this booking only if the room is free" is checked in the region handling the write. Another region can accept a conflicting booking at the same moment. DynamoDB's strong multi-region mode instead rejects such writes with a retryable conflict error, and every write pays for cross-region agreement. Any database that accepts writes in several places behaves like the eventual mode unless it coordinates, so check which your uniqueness checks rely on.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Disconnected operation is a requirement.** Hotels, shops, ships, clinics, field devices and offline-first mobile apps must accept writes while cut off, so they need a local leader. The design question becomes which data each node may change while disconnected.
- **Writes must be local and the data divides by owner.** Each tenant, store or user has a home node (24.2b). This is multi-leader in topology, but because no row has two writers, there are no conflicts.
- **The data merges naturally.** Examples: activity feeds, telemetry, last-seen timestamps, carts, counters stored as ledgers, collaborative data built on CRDTs.
- **Very high write availability for key-value data with LWW-appropriate semantics.** Examples: session stores, device state and caches, on Cassandra or DynamoDB, where losing an occasional overwritten value is acceptable and has been shown to be.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **For failover.** A single leader with a standby and automated promotion (24.2a) gives high availability without any of this article's problems.
- **For read latency.** Read replicas (23.2a) put data near readers with one writer.
- **"Active-active" because there are two regions.** If most writes for a record come from one place, route them there. Accepting writes in both places adds conflicts and nothing else.
- **Bidirectional replication as a migration shortcut.** It's sometimes used for a short cutover window. Left running, it becomes a multi-leader system that no one designed.
- **Leaderless storage for relational, invariant-heavy data.** Invoices, inventory and bookings belong in a system that can say no at commit time.

## Trade-offs

- **Every table needs a written merge policy.** "What happens when two writers disagree?" becomes a product question for each piece of data, and a default is an answer nobody wrote down.
- **Invariants move out of the database.** Uniqueness and capacity become application protocols (ownership, allotments, compensation), each of which has to be designed, built and tested.
- **Clocks become a correctness dependency** for any timestamp-based rule. NTP needs monitoring and alerts, like any other dependency.
- **Deletes cost storage and set a limit on disconnection.** Tombstones accumulate, and any node offline longer than their retention period must be rebuilt.
- **Leaderless systems need regular repair.** Missed repairs don't show up until deleted data reappears.
- **Divergence is hard to debug.** When two nodes disagree, you need both versions, their version metadata and the replication history. Build the tooling to diff them before you need it.
- **Users see anomalies.** Reappearing items, reverted edits and counts that change on refresh are normal behavior for these systems, and support teams need to be able to explain them.
- **IDs must be generated without coordination.** Use UUIDs, ULIDs or per-node ranges instead of one shared sequence (38.1a).
- **Schema changes need coordinating across all leaders**, with the ordering rules from 38.1a applied everywhere.
- **Testing requires simulating partitions.** Nothing short of disconnecting a node, writing on both sides and reconnecting tests the policies you wrote. Jepsen's analyses show how often databases themselves fail this test.

## Alternatives

- **Single leader with fast failover** (24.2a). One writer, no conflicts. You pay write latency for distant users and have an outage window during failover.
- **Ownership-partitioned writes.** Each record has one home leader. This keeps local writes where locality exists and avoids conflicts by design (24.2b).
- **Distributed SQL with consensus** (Spanner, CockroachDB, YugabyteDB, TiDB). You get serializable transactions across nodes, and each commit pays a round trip to a majority. Pinning rows to regions keeps that round trip local for most data.
- **Certification-based multi-primary within one region** (MySQL Group Replication, Galera). Several writers, with conflicts rejected at commit. It suits LAN latency and struggles across a WAN.
- **Store-and-forward commands.** A disconnected node queues *requests* ("sell room 412 for Friday") rather than applying state changes. The owner accepts or rejects each request when it arrives. The node does less on its own, but invariants stay with one owner.

## How to Achieve It

This path fits a system that already has multiple writers, which is the usual situation.

**1. Find every writer.** For each table, list every node, service and job that writes to it, including migration dual writes and admin scripts.
_Move on when_ each table has a known set of writers.

**2. Classify each table.** Use three classes:
- *owned*: it can have a single writer
- *mergeable*: LWW is acceptable, or the changes commute
- *invariant-bearing*: uniqueness, capacity or balance rules apply

Most tables turn out to be owned or mergeable.

**3. Make ownership explicit.** Route writes for owned data to their owner (the home-routing pattern from 24.2b).

**4. Fix the cheap failures.** Monitor clock skew and alert on it (above 100 ms is a reasonable threshold). Move to per-column versions. Replace mutable counters and balances with append-only ledgers.
_Move on when_ a partition test on staging shows no lost updates for mergeable data.

**5. Add detection before changing resolution.** Turn on conflict logging (Postgres 18's `pg_stat_subscription_stats` counters, Cassandra's repair metrics, or your own sync service's conflict log), and chart it. You can't choose a resolution policy for conflicts you can't see.

**6. Make deletes explicit.** Use tombstones with a documented maximum disconnection period. Choose delete-wins or update-wins per table, deleting for erasure. Nodes offline longer than the limit are refused resync and rebuilt.

**7. Give each invariant its own mechanism.** Use allotments for inventory, coordination for uniqueness, and a compensation workflow with an owner for anything left over.

**8. Test partitions continuously.** In CI or on staging, disconnect a node, write on both sides, reconnect, then assert that all nodes converge and that the invariants still hold.

## Implementation

The implementation is TypeScript: the merge step in the hotel group's sync service. When a hotel reconnects, each record it sends is compared with the center's copy and merged. The code shows how detection (version vectors) and resolution (per-field rules) fit together. Ticket 3 has no merge fix. It needs allotments, which is step 7 rather than code.

### Clocks and version vectors

```ts
// Hybrid logical clock: wall time plus a counter. If a write saw an earlier
// write, its stamp is always larger, whatever the wall clocks say.
type HLC = { wall: number; count: number; node: string };

let last = { wall: 0, count: 0 };

function stamp(node: string, physical = Date.now()): HLC {
  const wall = Math.max(last.wall, physical);
  const count = wall === last.wall ? last.count + 1 : 0;
  last = { wall, count };
  return { wall, count, node };
}

function observe(remote: HLC) {            // call on every received record
  if (remote.wall > last.wall || (remote.wall === last.wall && remote.count > last.count)) {
    last = { wall: remote.wall, count: remote.count };
  }
}

const later = (a: HLC, b: HLC) =>          // total order; node id breaks ties the same way everywhere
  a.wall !== b.wall ? a.wall > b.wall : a.count !== b.count ? a.count > b.count : a.node > b.node;

// Version vector: one counter per node. Answers the question timestamps can't.
type VV = Record<string, number>;
type Order = "before" | "after" | "equal" | "concurrent";

function compare(a: VV, b: VV): Order {
  let aAhead = false, bAhead = false;
  for (const n of new Set([...Object.keys(a), ...Object.keys(b)])) {
    if ((a[n] ?? 0) > (b[n] ?? 0)) aAhead = true;
    if ((b[n] ?? 0) > (a[n] ?? 0)) bAhead = true;
  }
  return aAhead && bAhead ? "concurrent" : aAhead ? "after" : bAhead ? "before" : "equal";
}
```

> **What `observe` fixes, and what it doesn't.** Once Lisbon has received any record stamped 14:00 from the center, its own later stamps come after that, even with its clock seven minutes slow. In Ticket 1, though, the two edits were made independently, so neither had seen the other. The HLC still orders them by wall clock, and the slow clock still loses. Only the version vector recognizes that they were concurrent, and only per-field resolution keeps both edits. Clock monitoring is still necessary.

### The merge: detection first, then a rule per field

```ts
type Field<T> = { value: T; at: HLC };
type GuestProfile = {
  id: string;
  vv: VV;
  deleted?: HLC;                  // tombstone; outlives the longest allowed disconnection
  surname: Field<string>;
  phone: Field<string>;
  marketingOptIn: Field<boolean>;
};

const lww = <T>(a: Field<T>, b: Field<T>) => (later(a.at, b.at) ? a : b);

function mergeGuest(local: GuestProfile, remote: GuestProfile, log: ConflictLog): GuestProfile {
  const vv = mergeVV(local.vv, remote.vv);   // entry-by-entry maximum

  // Erasure is delete-wins by policy: a tombstone beats any update, concurrent or later.
  if (local.deleted || remote.deleted) {
    return { ...(local.deleted ? local : remote), vv };
  }

  switch (compare(local.vv, remote.vv)) {
    case "after":
    case "equal":  return local;             // remote is older: nothing to merge
    case "before": return remote;            // remote saw everything we have
    case "concurrent":
      log.record(local.id, local, remote);   // detection is data: chart it
      return {
        ...local,
        vv,
        surname: lww(local.surname, remote.surname),   // per field, so Ticket 1 keeps both edits
        phone: lww(local.phone, remote.phone),
        // Consent: when two concurrent edits disagree, the withdrawal wins.
        marketingOptIn: {
          value: local.marketingOptIn.value && remote.marketingOptIn.value,
          at: lww(local.marketingOptIn, remote.marketingOptIn).at,
        },
      };
  }
}
```

> **Every field line is a business decision.** `marketingOptIn` isn't LWW, because a concurrent "no" should never be overwritten by a "yes" that raced it. That's a legal choice expressed as a boolean AND. Reviewing a merge function means reviewing product policy, so the people who own that policy should be reviewers, not just engineers.

> **The tombstone check comes first on purpose.** It runs before the comparison, so an update from a hotel that was offline for days can't revive a profile that was erased (Ticket 4). It only works while tombstones are kept. If a hotel reconnects after the retention period, the sync service must refuse to merge and require a rebuild. Otherwise the zombie-data problem returns through another route.

### The ledger that makes Ticket 2 impossible

```sql
-- Points are operations, not a balance. Inserts with distinct IDs never conflict,
-- and a replayed insert is a no-op, so retries after reconnection are safe too.
CREATE TABLE points_ledger (
  entry_id  uuid PRIMARY KEY,           -- generated where the points were earned, not centrally
  guest_id  uuid NOT NULL,
  delta     integer NOT NULL,
  reason    text NOT NULL,              -- 'stay:PORTO-88121', 'promo:AUTUMN26'
  origin    text NOT NULL,              -- which hotel or service wrote it
  at        timestamptz NOT NULL
);

-- The balance is derived, so it converges once every node has every entry.
SELECT sum(delta) FROM points_ledger WHERE guest_id = $1;
```

> **The ledger doesn't cover spending.** Earning points commutes: +500 and +1,000 give the same total in either order. Spending doesn't, because two hotels can each approve redeeming the same 10,000 points while disconnected. Redemption is an invariant, and it needs the invariant tools: only the center approves redemptions, or each hotel can approve up to a small local limit (an allotment again).

## Further Reading

- **Martin Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, chapter 5**: "Multi-Leader Replication" and "Leaderless Replication," including topologies, causality problems in all-to-all setups, quorum edge cases and version vectors. It's the best single treatment of this article's subject.
- **DeCandia et al., "Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store"** (SOSP 2007). The origin of the leaderless design: sloppy quorums, hinted handoff, Merkle-tree anti-entropy, vector clocks, and the shopping cart whose deleted items came back.
- **Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero and Zawirski, "Conflict-free Replicated Data Types"** (2011). The formal basis for counters, sets and registers that merge without coordination.
- **Kulkarni et al., "Logical Physical Clocks and Consistent Snapshots in Globally Distributed Databases"** (2014). The hybrid logical clock paper.
- **Pat Helland, "Memories, Guesses, and Apologies"** (2007) and **Helland and Campbell, "Building on Quicksand"** (CIDR 2009). The case for compensation over coordination, and for making apologies a designed part of the system.
- **Apache Cassandra documentation**: tombstones, `gc_grace_seconds` and repair. Read these before running any leaderless store in production.
- **PostgreSQL documentation, "Logical Replication: Conflicts"** (18 and 19). The conflict types, what Postgres does with each (mostly: applies or skips), and the `retain_dead_tuples` trade-off.
- **Amazon DynamoDB documentation, "Global tables: how they work."** The difference between the eventual (MREC) and strong (MRSC) modes, and that conditional writes are evaluated against the local region's copy.
- **MySQL documentation, "Group Replication: Multi-Primary Mode."** Certification, first committer wins, and the restrictions that follow.
- **Jepsen analyses** (jepsen.io). Partition testing of real databases, including many that claimed stronger guarantees than they provided.
- **In this collection:** 9.3c (merging at keystroke scale, and choosing the unit of conflict), 24.2a (single leader with failover), 24.2b (home regions and the latency floor), 38.1a (what the replication stream carries; ID generation), 2.4 (event sourcing, for the ledger idea), 10.2 and 17.1 (idempotent retries), 38.2 (sharding, which assigns every row an owner by construction).

A **Go** sibling would be worth writing from the storage side: a small leaderless key-value store with configurable N, W and R, read repair and hinted handoff. Simulating replica timeouts and concurrent writers in goroutines would show what this article can only describe: a "successful" quorum read returning an old value, and a failed write surfacing on a later read. Not yet written.
