# Revenue Recognition & Recurring Revenue Metrics

_Module 15.5 — Subscription & Billing Management_

## The Problem

A customer signs a $12,000 annual contract on 15 March and pays the same day. Five people ask what it is worth, and all five correct answers are different numbers.

```
$12,000 annual contract — signed, invoiced and paid 15 March

BOOKINGS   ██                                                    $12,000, all in March
CASH       ██                                                    $12,000, all in March
DEFERRED   ███████▉▊▋▌▍▎▏                                        $12,000 → $0 across the term
REVENUE    ▌ █ █ █ █ █ █ █ █ █ █ █ ▌                             ~$500 Mar, $1,000/mo, ~$500 in Mar
MRR        ────────────────────────                              +$1,000 from March onward
           Mar Apr May Jun Jul Aug Sep Oct Nov Dec Jan Feb Mar
```

Sales booked $12,000 in March. The bank received $12,000 in March. Accounting recognised about $500 of revenue in March and carries $11,500 as a liability. The board deck shows $1,000 of new MRR. An investor hears $12,000 of ARR. Nobody is wrong.

Here is how a system that has not thought about this fails.

**The revenue chart sums payments.** March shows a spike and the following eleven months show nothing from this customer. A quarter with four annual deals looks like a breakout; the next one looks like collapse. Every decision made from that chart is made from an artefact of billing timing.

**MRR is `SUM(price)` over active subscriptions.** That single query counts trials as revenue, counts past-due accounts at full value, counts a customer on a 40% coupon at list price, adds annual contracts at their annual amount, and ignores usage and credits entirely. Five errors, one line of SQL, and it is the number on the wall.

**Usage has no obvious MRR at all.** What is the monthly recurring revenue of a customer who spent $3,000 last month and $800 the month before? Every answer is a convention, and yours is currently undocumented and implemented by accident.

**Churn is reported as one number.** Someone says 3%, someone else says 8%, and both are defensible, because one is counting customers and the other is counting revenue, and neither said which. Nobody can say whether a downgrade is churn, or whether a customer whose card failed on the 2nd is churned this month and un-churned on the 9th.

**A March invoice is refunded in July.** Your dashboard recomputes March. The number in April's board deck no longer reproduces, and the version of history you presented to investors has quietly been replaced.

**Finance rebuilds everything in a spreadsheet**, because they cannot get numbers they trust out of the product. Now there are two sets of books that disagree by an amount nobody can decompose, and reconciling them is a monthly ritual performed by the two people who understand it.

**And then diligence.** An acquirer asks for a revenue waterfall: opening MRR, new, expansion, contraction, churn, closing, by month, for three years. Nothing in the system can produce it, because MRR was never recorded — it was always computed from current state, and current state has changed every day since.

The thread: **bookings, cash, recognised revenue, and MRR answer four different questions about the same money, and the billing system is asked for all four.** And underneath that, the requirement that makes any of them trustworthy: **a number you cannot recompute next year and get the same answer is not a metric.**

## What It Is

**Revenue recognition** is the accounting determination of *when* money counts as revenue, independent of when it was invoiced or collected. Under ASC 606 and IFRS 15, revenue is recognised as the performance obligation is satisfied — for subscription software, usually rateably across the service period.

**Deferred revenue** is the liability created when you have been paid (or have invoiced) for service not yet delivered. It is on the balance sheet as an obligation, not as income. It is not yet your money.

**MRR and ARR** are normalised recurring revenue per month or year. These are *not* accounting figures. They are management conventions describing a run rate.

**Churn** is the rate at which recurring revenue or customers leave, and it comes in several distinct flavours that are routinely confused.

The disambiguation that saves the most time: **recognised revenue and MRR are different quantities computed for different purposes.** Recognised revenue is backward-looking, governed by a standard, includes one-off fees as they are earned, and is auditable. MRR is forward-looking, governed by nothing, excludes one-off fees, and exists to describe momentum. They will never be equal and nobody should try to make them.

And the fact that makes this tractable rather than maddening: **there are no universal definitions of MRR, ARR, or churn.** Public companies, analytics vendors, and investors all use slightly different ones. The engineering requirement is not to find the correct definition. It is to choose one, write it down, implement it once, and make it reproducible.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The revenue chart is a sum of payments, and it spikes when annual deals land.
- MRR is computed from current subscription rows.
- You cannot reproduce last quarter's reported MRR today.
- Two dashboards show different MRR and both have defenders.
- Discounts, coupons, and credits are invisible in MRR.
- Usage revenue is excluded because nobody decided how to include it.
- Trials, past-due, and paused subscriptions are all counted as active.
- Churn is a single number and nobody can say whether it is logo or revenue, gross or net.
- Finance maintains a parallel model in a spreadsheet.
- There is no deferred revenue balance anywhere in the system.
- Refunds retroactively change closed periods in your reporting.
- No one can produce an MRR waterfall.
- Monthly and annual plans are summed without normalisation.

## How It Works

### Recognition, reduced to what engineering has to build

The standard's five steps — identify the contract, identify the performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it across obligations, recognise as each is satisfied — matter mostly because of what they imply for your schema.

**Every invoice line gets a revenue schedule**: an amount, a start, an end, and a method. Generated at invoice finalisation, which is already the snapshot moment (15.4a), and stored — never recomputed on read.

Methods differ by line type, and the differences are the part that surprises people:

- **Subscription lines** recognise rateably across the service period.
- **Usage lines** recognise in the period the usage occurred, which is generally the period being invoiced in arrears.
- **One-time setup or implementation fees** frequently do *not* recognise at a point in time. If the fee does not transfer a distinct good or service on its own, it is often spread across the expected customer life — which can be longer than the contract. This is the classic case where the engineering instinct and the accounting answer diverge completely.
- **Discounts** are allocated across the obligations in the contract rather than applied to whichever line is convenient.

**Allocation** is where bundles get real: sell a subscription plus onboarding at a package price, and the discount must be spread across both by their standalone selling prices. A single-SKU monthly product never meets this. A product with implementation fees, multi-year ramps, or mid-term amendments meets it constantly, and that is the point at which spreadsheets stop working.

**Period close** is the constraint that shapes the rest of the system. Once a month is closed, its numbers are frozen. A refund in July does not change March; it is a July adjustment referencing a March invoice. Any dashboard that recomputes history from current data is contradicting the books, and it will win the argument with anyone who does not know why it is wrong.

### MRR as stored events, not as a query

The design that makes every question in this article answerable: **each change to recurring revenue emits a movement** with a normalised monthly delta, a reason, and an effective date.

```
opening MRR
  + new             first paid subscription for a customer
  + expansion       upgrade, seat addition, price increase
  − contraction     downgrade, seat removal
  − churn           subscription actually ended
  + reactivation    a returning customer, not a new one
  ─────────────
= closing MRR
```

That identity is the reason to do it this way. It is a testable invariant: if the movements in a month do not bridge opening to closing MRR, a subscription changed without emitting an event, and you have found a bug that would otherwise have been invisible for a year.

The normalisation rules must be written down, because every one of them is a choice:

- **Annual divided by twelve, quarterly by three.** Not a design decision, but state it anyway.
- **Discounts are reflected.** MRR is what they actually pay, not list price.
- **One-time fees are excluded.** They are not recurring.
- **Trials are excluded** until they convert — or included, if you say so, consistently.
- **Past-due subscriptions stay in MRR until they actually end.** Churn is recognised at the end of dunning (15.2b), not at the first decline, or your MRR will oscillate with your decline rate.
- **Paused subscriptions** contract to zero and reactivate later, or are excluded. Pick one.
- **Usage** is the hard one, below.

### The usage problem, honestly

Three conventions exist and none is clean. **Exclude usage** — cleanest, reproducible, and understates a usage-heavy business badly. **Trailing average** — smooth, common, and invents a recurring number from something that is not recurring. **Committed only** — count the platform fee and the contractual minimum, treat overage as variable revenue. 

For products where usage is a meaningful share of revenue, the convention most companies converge on is to stop forcing it: report **recurring revenue and usage revenue as separate lines**, and let net revenue retention carry the growth story that MRR alone cannot. This is less satisfying than a single number and considerably more honest.

### Churn, precisely

- **Logo churn** — customers lost over customers at period start. Ignores size, so one enterprise account and one $9 account count the same.
- **Gross revenue churn** — (churn + contraction) over opening MRR. Never negative. This is your leak rate.
- **Net revenue churn** — (churn + contraction − expansion) over opening MRR. Can be negative, meaning existing customers grew more than others left.
- **Net revenue retention** — the same thing expressed as a ratio, usually measured over twelve months on a cohort, excluding customers acquired in the period. The exclusion is what makes it meaningful and the thing most home-grown implementations get wrong.

Two further distinctions carry real information: **voluntary versus involuntary** (15.2b), because they have entirely different fixes, and **cohort versus aggregate**, because aggregate churn hides that early-life churn is much higher than mature-cohort churn, and a growing company's aggregate number improves for reasons unrelated to the product.

And one timing rule: a customer churns when their subscription actually ends, not when they clicked cancel. That is 15.3b's billing clock, and using the request date instead moves revenue between months for no reason.

### Reproducibility as a design requirement

Four properties, and they are the whole argument for everything above:

- Amounts, dates, and reasons are stored as **immutable events**, not derived from current state.
- Period-end metrics are **snapshotted after close** and never recalculated.
- Corrections are **current-period adjustments** that reference the original, never edits to history.
- Definitions live in **one place** — a semantic layer, a metrics file, a documented model — not in eleven dashboard queries that drifted apart over two years.

### Where the boundary is

The billing system is the **source**, not the **book**. Past a certain size, the general ledger belongs in an accounting system and revenue schedules may belong in a dedicated tool; your application's job is to emit complete, immutable, well-described transaction data into them. Teams that miss this boundary end up building a small ERP by accident, badly, and discover it during their first audit.

## When It's Appropriate

- **An MRR movement log** — from the first paying customer. It is cheap to add and impossible to backfill, which is the same argument as location evidence in 15.4b.
- **Deferred revenue schedules** — as soon as you invoice for more than one period at a time, or as soon as anyone prepares accrual financials.
- **Formal allocation under ASC 606** — when you are audited, raising a priced round, or selling bundles with more than one performance obligation.
- **Cohort retention and NRR** — once you have a year of cohorts, so the numbers mean something.
- **A dedicated revenue recognition tool** — when contract complexity (multi-year terms, ramps, mid-term amendments, multi-element arrangements) has outgrown what a spreadsheet can hold correctly.
- **Separating usage from recurring in reporting** — the moment usage revenue becomes material.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Building a general ledger.** You are not an ERP, and the version you build will be audited by someone who has seen real ones.
- **Full ASC 606 allocation machinery for a single-SKU monthly product.** One obligation, rateable, done.
- **ARR for a purely monthly self-serve business.** MRR is the number; ARR is a multiplication performed on a slide.
- **A BI stack before the definitions exist.** Tooling does not resolve definitional disputes; it industrialises them.
- **Daily MRR.** It invites reaction to noise at a cadence faster than any decision you can actually make.
- **Retroactively restating history every time a definition improves.** Restate deliberately, once, with a documented note, or you will have several incompatible versions of the past in circulation.

## Trade-offs

- **The movement log has to be maintained.** Every new way a subscription can change needs a corresponding event, and a missed one breaks the waterfall identity — which is why the identity needs to be a test rather than a documented expectation.
- **Frozen periods mean your dashboard permanently disagrees with a naive query**, and someone will eventually "fix" the dashboard to match the query.
- **Revenue schedules multiply rows.** One annual line becomes twelve entries; across thousands of customers that is a table with real size and real query cost.
- **Writing definitions down starts arguments you were previously avoiding.** That is the point, and it still costs two weeks.
- **Parity with finance is ongoing work**, not a project that finishes.
- **Real-time and auditable pull in opposite directions.** The number that is instant is the one computed from current state, and that is exactly the one that cannot be reproduced.

## Alternatives

**Provider and analytics dashboards.** Stripe's own reporting, ChartMogul, Baremetrics, ProfitWell. Excellent early, effectively free, and their definitions are theirs — which is fine until your board asks a question their model does not express. They handle simple subscription businesses very well and usage-based or heavily amended contracts poorly.

**Warehouse plus a semantic layer.** Billing events land in the warehouse; metrics are defined once as models and consumed everywhere. This is the scalable answer for the metrics half, and it depends entirely on the events existing in the first place.

**Accounting system as system of record.** NetSuite, Sage Intacct, Xero, with the application as an upstream feed. The right end state for the revenue half of this article, and the sooner the boundary is drawn the less there is to unpick.

**Dedicated revenue recognition platforms** — Maxio, Zuora RevPro, Leapfin and similar — when contract complexity justifies them.

**A spreadsheet maintained by finance.** What most companies actually do for their first years, and honestly the right answer at that size. Its failure mode is silent divergence from the product's numbers, so the useful mitigation is agreeing on one shared source of transaction data even while the modelling lives in the sheet.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — revenue is the sum of payments.** Fine until someone buys an annual plan.

**L1 — define MRR and implement it once.** Normalisation, discounts, trials, past-due, and usage handled explicitly and documented. One query, one place, one owner. *Signal:* your first annual contract, or your second dashboard.

**L2 — MRR movements as events**, with reasons and effective dates, and the waterfall identity as an automated test. *Signal:* anyone asking why MRR moved.

**L3 — revenue schedules generated at invoice finalisation**, stored per line, with methods that differ by line type. *Signal:* the first accrual-basis financial statements, or the first multi-period invoice.

**L4 — period close.** Snapshot metrics at month end, freeze them, route corrections to the current period with references. *Signal:* the first time a historical number changed under someone.

**L5 — cohorts, NRR, and separated usage reporting.** *Signal:* expansion becoming a real motion, or usage becoming material.

**L6 — hand the ledger over.** Accounting system or rev-rec platform owns the books; the application becomes a clean, complete feed. *Signal:* an audit, a diligence process, or complexity that no longer fits a spreadsheet.

## Implementation

Go, because the most valuable artefact in this article is a test rather than a function, and a language where tests are ordinary code makes that easy to show.

```go
type MovementReason string

const (
	New          MovementReason = "new"
	Expansion    MovementReason = "expansion"
	Contraction  MovementReason = "contraction"
	Churn        MovementReason = "churn"
	Reactivation MovementReason = "reactivation"
)

type MRRMovement struct {
	SubscriptionID string
	CustomerID     string
	DeltaMinor     int64          // normalised to ONE MONTH: annual/12, quarterly/3.
	                              // Contraction and churn carry negative values, so every
	                              // caller adds and nobody has to remember a sign rule.
	Reason         MovementReason
	EffectiveAt    time.Time      // when it takes effect, not when it was requested:
	                              // a cancellation on the 3rd churns at period end (15.3b)
	SourceRef      string         // the change, invoice, or payment event that caused it
}
```

Classification is the part that quietly goes wrong:

```go
// Classify by the sign of the normalised delta, never by the name of the action
// that produced it. A "plan change" that lowers the rate while adding seats is
// expansion or contraction depending on arithmetic, and a system that trusts the
// UI's verb will produce a waterfall that does not balance — while looking right.
func classify(before, after int64, everPaid bool) MovementReason {
	switch {
	case before == 0 && after > 0 && !everPaid:
		return New
	case before == 0 && after > 0:
		return Reactivation
	case after == 0:
		return Churn
	case after > before:
		return Expansion
	default:
		return Contraction
	}
}
```

And the invariant, which is the actual deliverable:

```go
// closing = opening + new + expansion + reactivation + contraction + churn
// (the last two being negative). If this fails, something mutated a subscription
// without emitting a movement — the only failure mode of this design, and
// otherwise completely silent until diligence.
func TestWaterfallBalances(t *testing.T) {
	for _, m := range monthsSince(launch) {
		opening := mrrAsOf(m.Start())
		closing := mrrAsOf(m.End())

		sum := opening
		for _, mv := range movementsIn(m) {
			sum += mv.DeltaMinor
		}

		if sum != closing {
			t.Errorf("%s: movements bridge to %d, closing MRR is %d (drift %d)",
				m, sum, closing, closing-sum)
		}
	}
}
```

Running it across *every* month rather than only the most recent is deliberate: it catches the case where a late correction silently restated a period that was already reported.

Revenue schedules are the other stored artefact, and the shape matters more than the code:

```go
type RevenueSchedule struct {
	InvoiceLineID string
	Method        Method    // Rateable | PointInTime | AsIncurred
	Start, End    time.Time
	AmountMinor   int64
	Entries       []PeriodEntry // one per accounting period, generated at finalisation
}
```

`Entries` is generated up front and stored, not computed on read. That is the same rule as the invoice snapshot in 15.4a and for the same reason: a schedule derived from current data will silently restate itself when a price, a term, or a rounding rule changes.

## Further Reading

- **A plain-language summary of ASC 606 / IFRS 15** — the five-step model, from FASB, IFRS, or any of the large accounting firms' guides. Read a summary, not the standard.
- **The AICPA's revenue recognition guide for software and SaaS.** Specific about the cases that actually arise: setup fees, term licences, hosting, and implementation services.
- **ChartMogul's and Baremetrics' published metric definitions, read side by side.** Both are public, both are careful, and they disagree in places. That disagreement is the most efficient possible demonstration that these are conventions rather than facts.
- **a16z's writing on startup metrics**, particularly the pieces on how the same business produces very different numbers under different definitions.
- **dbt's documentation on the semantic layer and metric definitions.** The tooling expression of "define it once, consume it everywhere," which is the durable fix for dashboard drift.
- **Documentation from a revenue recognition platform** — Maxio, Zuora RevPro, or similar — on schedules and contract modifications. Vendor material, and the clearest available description of what a modification does to an existing schedule.
- **15.4a in this collection**, for why finalisation is the moment schedules are generated.

The companion worth writing here is not a translation into another language — there is little in the Go above that Java or TypeScript would change. It is the **warehouse version**: the same movement log and the same identity expressed as incremental models over an immutable event table, which is where most companies actually compute these numbers. That version has genuinely different failure modes — late-arriving events landing in a period already reported, incremental models silently rebuilding and restating closed months, and a semantic layer whose definitions diverge from the application's — and none of them are visible from an application-side implementation. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying past billing: **if you cannot recompute a number a year from now and get the same answer, you do not have a metric — you have a screenshot.** Anything that informs a decision should be derivable from immutable records plus a written definition, and anything computed from mutable current state is a reconstruction that changes underneath you without telling anyone. The same test applies far outside finance: an SLO measured from a retention-limited metrics store, an experiment result recomputed after the feature flag changed, a capacity forecast built on a table that gets updated in place, a security posture score assembled from whatever the scanner sees today. In every case the question is the same and it is worth asking out loud before the number goes on a slide: *what would I need to keep, to be able to produce this exact figure again?*
