# Failed Payments & Dunning

_Module 15.2b — Subscription & Billing Management_

## The Problem

Two thousand subscribers at $49 a month. On the first of the month the renewal job runs and roughly seven percent of the card charges come back declined. That is 140 customers and about $6,900 of monthly recurring revenue, and almost none of those people decided to leave.

That number is the whole subject. Across the industry, somewhere between a fifth and a third of all SaaS churn is **involuntary** — cards expiring, banks reissuing after a breach, balances briefly short, issuers refusing for reasons they decline to explain. It is churn from customers who still want the product, which makes it the cheapest revenue in the building to recover and the easiest to lose by accident.

Here is how it gets lost by accident.

**Version one: cancel on failure.** The charge declines at 3am, the subscription is cancelled, and access is revoked. The customer's bank had reissued their card two weeks earlier after a breach at a hotel chain. They discover the cancellation live, in front of their own client, and the support conversation that follows is not about a card.

**Version two: retry every day until it works.** Now the retries have their own failure modes. A `stolen_card` decline gets seven attempts, which is a card network rules violation with a fee attached, not merely poor manners. Visa caps retries of a declined recurring transaction and some response codes mean *do not try again, ever*. Meanwhile, an `insufficient_funds` decline is retried at the same hour every morning, which is the one variable that could have helped and was held constant.

**The email.** It goes out from `no-reply@`, subject line "Payment failed," body linking to a login page, and it is indistinguishable from the phishing simulation the customer's security team ran last month. It also goes to the address that signed up nineteen months ago, belonging to someone who left the company in March. The person who could fix the card has never heard about any of it.

**The end of the road.** After four attempts the code calls `cancel()`. What `cancel()` does was decided by whoever wrote it, on a Tuesday, without a meeting — and in this system it happens to trigger the tenant teardown path. A customer whose card expired now has no data.

**The double charge.** On day six the customer notices the banner, updates their card, and pays through the hosted invoice link. On day seven the scheduled retry fires anyway, because the retry schedule lives in your job table and knows nothing about a payment it did not initiate. They are charged twice, and the apology email arrives the same day as the fourth dunning reminder, which was also still queued.

**The question nobody can answer.** "How much revenue did we recover last month?" Nothing in the system distinguishes *paid on time* from *paid on the third attempt after two emails*, so there is no numerator, no denominator, and no way to tell whether any of this is working.

The thread: **a failed payment is not an error, it is a state with a duration.** It has a clock, a bounded sequence of attempts, a communication track, an access policy, and a terminal condition. All five are product decisions. Left as defaults, each one defaults to something expensive.

## What It Is

**Dunning** is the recovery process for a failed payment: a bounded sequence of retry attempts and customer communications, running on a deadline, ending in either recovery or a defined terminal state.

The vocabulary it needs:

- **Involuntary churn** — the subscription ends because a payment failed, not because anyone chose to end it.
- **Past due / delinquent** — the subscription state while recovery is in progress.
- **Grace period** — the window during which the customer keeps some or all access while unpaid.
- **Recovery rate** — the share of failed payments eventually collected, which is the number this entire apparatus exists to move.

One neighbour worth separating. **Dunning is not collections.** Collections is chasing an invoice on a push method — a bank transfer that was never sent, an enterprise account on net-30 — and its levers are statements, account managers, escalation, and eventually legal. Dunning is automated recovery of a *pull* attempt that a bank refused. They look similar on a dashboard and share almost no machinery. B2B products with both self-serve and enterprise customers need both, running separately.

And the idea that organises everything below: **failures divide by whether time alone can fix them.** A retry recovers cards that will work later. A message recovers cards that will never work again. Each is useless against the other's population, and a system that has only one of them is leaving most of the money on the table regardless of which one it has.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A single decline cancels the subscription.
- Or the opposite: a fixed cadence — daily, four times — applied to every decline code identically.
- Your database records that a charge failed but not *why*; the decline code was never stored.
- One dunning email, sent once, from a no-reply address, linking to a generic login page.
- The billing contact is whoever created the account, with no way to change it.
- Voluntary and involuntary churn are the same number in every report.
- A customer who updates their card still receives the next scheduled retry, or the next scheduled email, or both.
- Nobody can state what happens on the last day of dunning without reading the code.
- Support routinely "un-cancels" people by hand.
- Cards expire and you find out when the charge fails, despite having stored the expiry date yourself.
- Failed-payment emails have never had their deliverability checked, though they are the most consequential mail you send.
- Your chargeback rate rises after you make dunning more aggressive, and nobody connects the two.

## How It Works

### Classify the failure before doing anything else

Decline codes are not interchangeable, and the first branch in any dunning system should be on the class of failure rather than on the attempt number.

| class | typical codes | what fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-healing** | `insufficient_funds`, `processing_error`, `issuer_unavailable`, velocity limits | time, possibly a different time of day |
| **Needs the customer** | `expired_card`, `invalid_account`, `authentication_required` off-session | a human updating something |
| **Must not be retried** | `lost_card`, `stolen_card`, `pickup_card`, `fraudulent` | nothing; retrying breaks network rules |

The complication is that the largest bucket is none of these. Issuers routinely return generic responses — `do_not_honor`, `card_declined` — precisely so as not to leak information about the account. That bucket has to be treated as probably-retryable with a smaller attempt budget, and it is the reason recovery rates are empirical rather than derivable.

The attempt budget is not just etiquette. Card networks limit how many times a declined recurring transaction may be retried — Visa's limit is 15 attempts within 30 days for the same transaction, with certain response codes prohibited from retry entirely, and non-compliance carries fees. Any schedule you design is designed inside that ceiling.

### Timing

Two things reliably matter and one thing reliably does not.

**Spacing matters.** Attempts on days 3, 5, and 7 recover more than three attempts in three days, because the thing you are waiting for — a deposit, a resolved hold, a reissued card arriving in the post — happens on human timescales.

**Time of day matters**, for the simple reason that balances change when salaries land. Retrying at exactly the moment of the original failure, every time, is holding constant the one variable most likely to change the answer.

**Sophistication mostly does not matter until you have volume.** Providers offer machine-learned retry timing trained on billions of transactions, and it genuinely beats fixed schedules. Building your own on three hundred failures a month is not an optimisation, it is a hobby.

### Prevention beats recovery

The cheapest failed payment is the one that never happens, and most teams build recovery first because failure is what triggers the conversation.

**Card expiry is known in advance.** You are storing the expiry month and year. A message three weeks before removes that entire failure class for the customers who act on it, at the cost of one scheduled query.

**Account updater and network tokens.** Visa Account Updater and Mastercard's equivalent let issuers push replacement credentials into your provider's vault, so a reissued card keeps working with no customer involvement at all. Network tokens achieve the same thing structurally, by storing a token the issuer can re-point rather than a card number that goes stale. Where reissued cards dominate your decline mix — and for consumer products they often do — this single feature outperforms every retry schedule you could write.

**A backup payment method**, collected at signup or after the first failure, turns a decline into a fallback rather than a dunning cycle.

**A billing contact that is a role, not a person.** The founder's address on an account that now has a finance department is a guaranteed missed message.

### The communication track

The message does the work that retries structurally cannot, so it deserves the same design attention as the schedule.

**Channel.** Email plus an in-app banner, always both. The in-app banner is the one that cannot be filtered, cannot go to a departed employee, and appears in front of the person actually using the product. This is 14.1's point arriving with money attached: the strongest move is usually to stop relying on the channel graded by someone else.

**Content.** What failed, the amount, what to do, one link, and a sender identity nobody has to squint at. Link to your own domain and never ask for card details in the message itself — you are competing for attention with real phishing that looks exactly like a lazy dunning email, and losing that comparison costs you the recovery.

**Friction.** The link should land on a page where updating the card is possible without a password reset detour. A signed, short-lived, single-purpose link to a hosted update page is the standard shape.

**Cadence.** Tied to the attempt schedule, not running independently of it. Early messages should assume a technical glitch, because that is usually what it is. Later ones state the consequence and the date.

**Segmentation.** Above some account size, a human outreach beats every email ever written. If one account is 40× another, they should not receive the same sequence.

### Access during dunning

This is where the reversible states from 5.3a earn their existence. Three broad policies:

- **Full access throughout.** Best recovery, and you are extending unsecured credit for the length of the grace period. For a product with real per-customer cost — inference, storage, egress — a delinquent account can consume more than the invoice you are trying to collect.
- **Progressive degradation.** Full access with a banner, then read-only, then suspended. Read-only is the state most systems lack and the one that does the most work: it makes the consequence real without destroying the reason to come back.
- **Immediate cutoff.** Defensible only when usage is expensive and the relationship is transactional.

The rule underneath: **the lever must be reversible and proportional.** Suspension is reversible. Deletion is not, and it must never be an action on the billing clock. The terminal state of dunning is *suspended with data retained*; whatever happens after that belongs to the tenant lifecycle, with its own notice period and its own audit trail.

### The state machine, and who drives it

```
  active ──────charge fails──────► past_due ──────payment────► active
                                     │  ├ attempts 1..n   (bounded, class-aware)
                                     │  └ messages 1..m   (cadence-aligned)
                                     ▼ deadline
                                  unpaid / SUSPENDED ──payment──► active
                                     │
                                     ▼ retention window (5.3a, not billing)
                                  canceled
```

One property matters more than the diagram: **the machine is driven by payment events, never by the retry job's beliefs.** Recovery can arrive from routes you did not initiate — the customer pays through the hosted invoice link, support charges them manually, the provider's own smart retry succeeds. Every one of those must cancel the pending attempts *and* the pending messages. The double charge from the Problem section is what happens when the schedule is the source of truth; the threatening email received the day after paying is the same bug in the other channel, and customers remember it longer.

### Measure it, and know what constrains it

Recovery rate by decline class and by attempt number tells you where the schedule is earning its keep. Involuntary churn split out from voluntary tells you whether this deserves more investment. Revenue recovered and revenue at risk are the numbers to put in front of anyone asking why you spent a sprint on billing.

The guardrail is the dispute rate. Dunning hard against people who wanted to leave anyway converts churn into chargebacks, and chargebacks cost a fee, count against the network monitoring thresholds from 15.2a, and are strictly worse than the cancellation you were avoiding. An easy-to-find cancel button and a pause option are not concessions to churn; they are how you keep the dispute rate low enough to be allowed to keep processing cards.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any card-billed recurring product past a few dozen subscribers.** The arithmetic justifies a three-attempt schedule and two messages almost immediately; the implementation is an afternoon and recovers a percentage of MRR every month thereafter.
- **Full machinery — segmentation, escalation, tuned schedules —** once involuntary churn is a visible fraction of total churn and the absolute number is worth a person's attention, typically in the hundreds-to-thousands of subscribers.
- **Generous grace periods** when the product is embedded in the customer's daily workflow, where lockout damages a relationship worth far more than the unpaid invoice, and where marginal cost per customer is near zero.
- **Human escalation** for accounts above an ARR threshold where a CSM's hour is obviously cheaper than the loss.
- **Account updater and network tokens** as soon as you hold a meaningful number of stored cards. This is the highest return per unit of effort in the entire article.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Prepaid credits, or invoicing on bank transfer.** There is no decline to recover. What you need is collections and accounts-receivable ageing, which is a different system with different people operating it.
- **Trials without a card.** Nothing to dun; that is a conversion problem.
- **Building your own retry-timing model.** Below tens of thousands of failures there is nothing to learn from, and your provider has already learned it.
- **Long grace periods on usage-heavy products.** If a delinquent account can run up more cost in two weeks than the invoice is worth, the grace period is a subsidy for the worst customers.
- **Eight-step sequences on a $9/month subscription.** The email volume damages the deliverability of every other message you send — including the receipts — for a recovery that rounds to nothing.
- **Any retry at all on the do-not-retry codes**, at any volume, for any reason.

## Trade-offs

- **Aggressiveness trades recovery against disputes.** The setting that maximises recovered revenue is not the setting that minimises total cost, and finding the difference requires watching a metric that lags by weeks.
- **Every day of grace is unsecured credit plus cost of goods.** It is a lending decision made by whoever set a constant.
- **The state machine leaks into everything downstream.** "Is this customer active?" now has a longer answer, and entitlements, analytics, support tooling, reporting, and every internal dashboard has to hold the same nuance.
- **Recovered revenue is late revenue**, which makes MRR reporting messier: a payment collected in March for February's cycle belongs to one of them and will be reported as the other by at least one system.
- **You are adding volume to your most deliverability-sensitive email**, and a dunning sequence in the spam folder is worse than no sequence, because you will believe the money was unrecoverable.
- **Attribution stays weak.** Whether the retry or the email recovered a payment is usually unknowable without an experiment you may not have the volume to run.
- **False-positive lockouts will happen** — a payment recorded at the provider and not in your database, and a customer suspended who paid. You need a support override with an audit trail, and you need it before the first occurrence.
- **Provider-managed retries mean the schedule is not yours.** Better outcomes, less visibility, and a behaviour change you will find out about from a graph.

## Alternatives

**Let the provider do it.** Stripe Billing, Chargebee, and Recurly all ship retry scheduling, customer emails, and a subscription state machine. For most products this is the correct answer, and the cost is that the copy, the cadence, and the state transitions are theirs — which becomes a real constraint when dunning needs to interact with your own access policy in a way their model does not express.

**Recovery specialists** — Churn Buster, Gravy, Butter, and similar — priced as a share of recovered revenue, offering better segmentation and sometimes human outreach. The arithmetic is unusually easy to evaluate: you are paying a percentage of money you were not otherwise getting.

**Prevention only.** Account updater, pre-expiry notices, backup methods, and a hard cancel on failure. Simpler than a dunning system, leaves money on the table, and is an honest choice for a small team that would otherwise build the aggressive version badly.

**Move the cohort off cards.** Annual invoicing or bank debit for high-value accounts removes card declines for the customers where a decline costs the most. Bank debits fail differently and more slowly, which is its own problem, but the failure modes are less frequent and less abrupt.

**Offer a pause or a downgrade at the moment of failure.** Some of the past-due population is the voluntary-churn population that simply has not told you yet. A pause button converts them into a retained relationship and, critically, into someone who does not call their bank. This is usually the cheapest single addition to a dunning flow and it is almost never the first thing built.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — cancel on decline.** Where most systems start, and where the money goes.

**L1 — store the decline code, and schedule class-aware retries.** Three attempts over a week or two, skipped entirely for the do-not-retry class, a smaller budget for generic codes. One afternoon, and the largest single jump in this list. *Signal:* you have more than a handful of failures a month.

**L2 — a real communication track.** Three messages aligned to the attempts, an in-app banner, and a one-time signed link to a hosted card-update page. *Signal:* measuring your decline mix and finding that a third of it can never be fixed by a retry.

**L3 — define the terminal state and the access policy.** Explicit states, read-only before suspension, suspension that retains data, deletion moved onto the tenant-lifecycle clock. *Signal:* the first wrongly-locked-out customer — or do this first, ahead of everything above, if today's terminal action is destructive.

**L4 — prevention.** Account updater or network tokens, pre-expiry notices, backup methods. *Signal:* expired and reissued cards showing up as a large share of failures, which for consumer products is usually immediately.

**L5 — segmentation and escalation.** Different schedules by cohort, human outreach above an ARR line, provider smart retries where available. *Signal:* enough volume that differences between cohorts are measurable rather than anecdotal.

**L6 — the measurement loop.** Recovery rate by class and attempt, involuntary churn as its own metric, dispute rate as the guardrail on all of it. *Signal:* somebody asks whether any of this worked and you cannot answer.

## Implementation

TypeScript, chosen for one specific property: exhaustiveness. The dangerous failure here is a decline code falling into a `default` branch that retries it, and a type system that refuses to compile an unhandled class is a better defence than a code review.

```ts
type DeclineClass = "self_healing" | "needs_customer" | "never_retry";

function classify(code: string): DeclineClass {
  switch (code) {
    case "insufficient_funds":
    case "processing_error":
    case "issuer_unavailable":
      return "self_healing";

    case "expired_card":
    case "invalid_account":
    case "authentication_required": // off-session; no server-side recovery exists (15.2a)
      return "needs_customer";

    case "lost_card":
    case "stolen_card":
    case "pickup_card":
    case "fraudulent":
      return "never_retry"; // retrying is a network-rules violation with a fee, not bad manners

    default:
      // do_not_honor, card_declined, and friends. The largest bucket by far —
      // issuers deliberately don't say why. Retryable, on a shorter leash.
      return "self_healing";
  }
}
```

The schedule belongs in data, not in control flow, so that changing it is a config review rather than a deploy of new branching:

```ts
type DunningPolicy = {
  retryAfterDays: number[];   // spacing, not count — the gaps are what recover money
  messageOnDays: number[];
  deadlineDays: number;
};

const POLICY = {
  self_healing:   { retryAfterDays: [3, 5, 7], messageOnDays: [0, 5, 12], deadlineDays: 21 },
  needs_customer: { retryAfterDays: [7],       messageOnDays: [0, 3, 10], deadlineDays: 21 },
  never_retry:    { retryAfterDays: [],        messageOnDays: [0, 2],     deadlineDays: 14 },
} satisfies Record<DeclineClass, DunningPolicy>;
```

That `satisfies` is doing real work. Add a fourth `DeclineClass` next quarter and the build fails here, instead of the new class silently inheriting whatever the last `default` did.

The part that fixes the double charge:

```ts
// Called from the payment-succeeded webhook, whatever caused it: our retry,
// the provider's smart retry, the hosted invoice link, or support charging by hand.
// The state machine follows payment events. It never follows the retry job's plan.
async function onPaymentSucceeded(invoiceId: string, subscriptionId: string) {
  await scheduler.cancelAll(`dunning:${invoiceId}`);   // else tomorrow's retry charges again
  await comms.cancelPending(`dunning:${invoiceId}`);   // else the threat arrives after they paid
  await subscriptions.setState(subscriptionId, "active");
  await tenants.setAccess(subscriptionId, "FULL");
  await recovery.record(invoiceId, { attempt: await attempts.countFor(invoiceId) });
}
```

Two asides. Cancelling the messages is not a nicety — receiving "your account will be suspended" the morning after paying is the dunning bug customers actually complain about publicly, and it is caused by exactly one missing line. And `recovery.record` exists only so the question "how much did we recover, and on which attempt" has an answer later; nothing else in the flow needs it, which is why it is always the piece that gets dropped.

The deadline:

```ts
async function onDeadline(sub: Subscription) {
  // Reversible and proportional. Suspension retains everything; deletion is
  // not a billing action and runs on the tenant-lifecycle clock (5.3a).
  await subscriptions.setState(sub.id, "unpaid");
  await tenants.setAccess(sub.tenantId, "SUSPENDED");
  await comms.send("dunning_final", sub.billingContact);
}
```

## Further Reading

- **Stripe's documentation on failed payments, smart retries, and automatic customer emails**, plus their published recovery data. The clearest statement of what a tuned schedule is worth.
- **Visa's and Mastercard's rules on retrying declined recurring transactions** — the attempt limits, the prohibited response codes, and the fees. The rulebooks are hard going; a reputable vendor summary is a reasonable substitute if you check the numbers against two of them.
- **Visa Account Updater and Mastercard Automatic Billing Updater documentation**, and any good overview of network tokenization. Read this before building a retry schedule, not after.
- **Recurly's and Chargebee's involuntary-churn benchmark reports.** Vendor data with an obvious incentive, still the best public numbers on recovery rates by decline reason.
- **14.1 in this collection**, for the deliverability half. Dunning mail is the highest-stakes email you send and sits in the same reputation pool as your marketing.
- **5.3a in this collection**, for the states this article assumes — particularly why `READ_ONLY` exists and why deletion is never a billing action.

A **Java sibling built on a durable workflow engine** would reveal what the TypeScript above deliberately hides. "Schedule an attempt in three days and cancel it if anything else happens" is one line here and is genuinely the hard part: a timer that survives deploys, fires exactly once, and can be cancelled by an event arriving from a completely different process. Expressed as a durable workflow — Temporal, or a Quartz-and-state-table equivalent — dunning stops being rows plus cron and becomes a single long-running function with sleeps and signals in it, which is a different way to think about the whole class of multi-week business processes. That framing is worth an article of its own. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying past billing: **when an external system says no, the first question is whether time alone can change the answer.** If it can, you owe it a bounded, spaced retry budget. If it cannot, retrying is pure cost and the only move is to involve whoever can change the underlying condition. That single distinction is soft versus hard bounces in email, 429 versus 403 in an API client, redelivery versus dead-letter in a queue, and self-healing versus needs-customer here. Systems that retry everything and systems that retry nothing are making the same mistake, which is refusing to look at the reason.
