# Payment Processing

_Module 15.2a — Subscription & Billing Management_

## The Problem

The invoice is computed. Now the money has to move, and the first version of that code is one line:

```ts
const ok = await gateway.charge(customer.cardToken, invoice.amountDue);
if (ok) await invoices.markPaid(invoice.id);
```

Every failure below comes from that line being a reasonable-looking lie. `charge` does not return a boolean, it does not return promptly, and what it returns is not final.

**The request times out.** Thirty seconds, no response. Did the charge happen? You do not know, and the two obvious moves are both wrong: retry and you may have charged twice; give up and mark the invoice failed, and you may have taken money from someone whose account now says they owe you. The customer finds out before you do.

**`ok === true`, and the money arrives in three days.** The customer paid by bank debit. The charge was accepted, not completed — ACH and SEPA settle over days and can be returned weeks later. `markPaid` is now a claim your system cannot support, and there is no state between "attempted" and "paid" to put it in.

**The charge comes back `requires_action`.** The customer's bank wants to challenge them — a 3-D Secure prompt, in a browser, with the human present. Your billing job is a cron worker at 3am. There is no browser, there is no human, and there is nothing the worker can do except give up and generate an email.

**A decline that is four different things.** `insufficient_funds` is worth retrying on payday. `expired_card` is worth retrying only after the customer does something. `do_not_honor` is a shrug from the issuer. `stolen_card` must never be retried, and retrying declines indiscriminately is how a merchant account ends up in a network monitoring program. Your code branches on `ok`.

**Four months later, a chargeback.** The customer disputed the payment with their own bank, not with you. The money is removed from your balance along with a fee, you are invited to submit evidence, and you may lose anyway. The invoice in your database still says `paid`, because nothing in your model allows a payment to become unpaid after the fact.

**The auditor asks for your PCI scope.** Because the card number was posted to your server — even in transit, even unstored — you are in scope for the long version: SAQ D, several hundred controls, quarterly scans, an annual assessment. Had the number gone from the customer's browser straight to the provider and never touched your infrastructure, the same business would have filled in SAQ A, which is a fraction of the work, every year, forever.

Two threads run through this.

**A payment is not a function call. It is a long-running, externally-owned, reversible workflow that you observe.** Accepted is not settled, settled is not final, and final takes months.

**And the card data question is not a security question, it is a scope question.** The cheapest control available to you is never possessing the data in the first place, and that decision is made by where you put a form field.

## What It Is

**Payment processing** is moving money from a customer's funding instrument into your account through a chain of intermediaries, each of which can refuse, and several of which can reverse an approval long after it was given.

The chain, because the vocabulary is genuinely needed and rarely taught:

```
  customer          you                                   the money rails
 ┌────────┐   ┌──────────────┐   ┌──────────┐   ┌─────────┐   ┌────────────┐
 │ browser│──►│ your backend │──►│ gateway  │──►│ acquirer│──►│  network   │
 │  + SDK │   │  (no PAN)    │   │  / PSP   │   │(your bk)│   │ Visa/MC/.. │
 └────────┘   └──────────────┘   └──────────┘   └─────────┘   └─────┬──────┘
      ▲                                                             │
      │                       approve / decline                     ▼
      │                                                      ┌────────────┐
      └──────────── 3-D Secure challenge ───────────────────►│   issuer   │
                                                             │ (their bk) │
                                                             └────────────┘
```

The **issuer** is the customer's bank and the only party that actually decides. The **acquirer** is your bank. The **network** routes and sets the rules both banks must follow. The **gateway/PSP** — Stripe, Adyen, Braintree — is the API you talk to, and in modern practice bundles gateway, processor, tokenization vault, and often acquiring into one account.

Two disambiguations worth making now.

**Authorization, capture, settlement, and payout are four different moments**, and "payment" is used for all of them. Authorization asks the issuer to reserve funds. Capture says take them. Settlement is the batch process where money actually moves, typically a day or two later. Payout is when your PSP sends you a net amount — net of fees, refunds, chargebacks, and reserves, which is why the number in your bank account never equals the sum of your invoices.

**A merchant of record is not a payment processor.** Stripe processes your payments; you are still the seller, and you owe sales tax and VAT wherever you have obligations. Paddle or Lemon Squeezy become the legal seller, and they own tax registration, remittance, and chargebacks. That is a business-structure decision with large engineering consequences, and it appears again under Alternatives.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A card number, encrypted or not, has ever been written to your database, your logs, or your support tooling.
- Your charge call has no idempotency key.
- Retries are disabled on the payment job, because enabling them once caused double charges.
- `invoice.paid` is a boolean.
- There is no state for "payment initiated, outcome unknown," so timeouts are recorded as failures.
- You find out about disputes from your provider's email, not from your own system.
- Reconciliation means someone opening the provider's dashboard once a month.
- Your payout amounts do not match your revenue and nobody can explain the difference line by line.
- All decline codes take the same branch.
- Nothing in your code distinguishes on-session from off-session payments, and nothing handles `requires_action`.
- You take payments from the EEA or UK with no 3-D Secure path set up at signup.
- Nobody knows which PCI self-assessment questionnaire applies to you.
- Adding a payment method is treated as a configuration change rather than a new lifecycle.
- A refund is implemented by charging a negative amount, or by hand in the dashboard.

## How It Works

### The four moments, and the three reversals

```
 auth ──────► capture ──────► settlement ──────► payout
  │             │                 │                 │
  │ void        │ refund          │ refund          │ chargeback (up to ~120 days,
  │ (free,      │ (fee usually    │                 │  sometimes longer)
  │  no trace)  │  not returned)  │                 ▼
  ▼                                            funds clawed back + fee,
 expires in ~7 days if never captured          evidence submitted, may still lose
```

For software subscriptions, auth and capture almost always happen together and the distinction rarely matters. It starts to matter when the final amount is not known up front, when you fulfil something physical, or when you are a marketplace splitting funds. It matters immediately for one common case: a **card verification** at trial signup is a zero-amount or small authorization that is voided straight away, existing only to confirm the card is real and the customer's bank will speak to you later.

Chargebacks are the reversal that shapes the data model. The customer disputes with their issuer; money leaves your balance before anyone asks your opinion; you submit evidence and wait weeks. Beyond roughly a 0.9–1% dispute rate the networks put you in a monitoring program with escalating fines, which is why fraud, refund policy, and billing-descriptor clarity are engineering concerns and not just business ones. The modelling consequence: **paid is not a terminal state**, and everything downstream that consumed "paid" — entitlements, commissions, revenue reports, affiliate payouts — needs a compensating path.

### Never touching the card

The mechanism is simple and the consequences are not. The card details go from the customer's browser or mobile app **directly to the PSP's domain** — an iframe hosting the input fields, or a client SDK — and your server receives back a token: an opaque reference to a payment method held in the provider's vault. Your server charges the token. An attacker who exfiltrates your entire database gets a list of strings that are useless anywhere except your own PSP account.

What that buys is **scope**. PCI DSS applies to the systems that store, process, or transmit cardholder data, and the self-assessment you owe depends on which of those you do:

| | what your systems touch | rough control count |
|---|---|---|
| **SAQ A** | nothing; fields are hosted by the PSP | tens |
| **SAQ A-EP** | your page controls the form, data still goes direct to the PSP | ~150 |
| **SAQ D** | the card number reaches your servers | 250+, plus scans and often a QSA |

The gap between the top and bottom rows is person-months of work every year, in perpetuity, for the privilege of rendering your own `<input>`. That is the actual trade being made when someone says the hosted form does not match the brand guidelines.

The common way teams silently re-enter scope is not the checkout page. It is logging. A request body logged wholesale, a crash reporter capturing form state, a support tool that lets an agent type a card number into a text field "just this once" — each of those drags a system back into scope, and none of them look like a payments change in review. Redact at the edge, and treat the ability to type a PAN anywhere in your product as a defect.

### On-session, off-session, and why renewals need a browser six months earlier

Subscription billing charges a customer who is not there. In network terms that is a **merchant-initiated transaction** against a **credential on file**, and it only works if the credential was set up correctly while the customer *was* there.

Under PSD2 strong customer authentication rules in the EEA and UK, an off-session charge needs an exemption, and the exemption depends on the initial setup having been authenticated and flagged as establishing an agreement for future payments. The practical shape is:

```
signup (customer present)          renewal (nobody present)
  ├ collect method in PSP iframe     ├ charge stored method, off_session = true
  ├ 3-D Secure challenge if asked    └ issuer honours the agreement → approved
  └ store payment_method id               ...or asks for authentication → dead end
```

The dead end is worth dwelling on, because it is the failure teams discover only after shipping to Europe. An off-session charge that returns `authentication_required` cannot be rescued by your worker. There is no retry that fixes it. The only recovery is to get the human back into a browser — which means an email, a link, and a flow that re-authenticates and retries. That is dunning machinery, and it exists because of a decision made at signup.

Bank debits have the same shape under a different name: a **mandate**, collected once, referenced by every later collection, with rules about how it was obtained and what notice the customer is owed before each charge.

### Idempotency and the third outcome

Every payment call has three outcomes, not two: approved, declined, and **unknown**. Unknown is the one the design has to be built around, because it is the only one where your state and the provider's state can disagree.

An **idempotency key** sent with the request lets the provider recognise a repeat and replay the original result rather than creating a second payment. The key must come from the cause, not from a random generator at call time — otherwise the retry is, by definition, a different request.

There is a subtlety that catches people. The key cannot be just the invoice id, because you will genuinely want to attempt the same invoice again next Tuesday after a decline. It has to include the attempt: `invoice_8812:attempt_3`. Use the invoice id alone and the provider dutifully replays last week's decline for the next twenty-four hours, and your retry logic silently does nothing.

### Webhooks are the truth; the API response is a hint

This is the same shape as email's accepted-versus-delivered, with money attached. The response to your charge call tells you the state at that instant. What actually happened — settled, returned, refunded, disputed — arrives later as a webhook, if you have built somewhere for it to land.

Three properties of webhook delivery drive the design. They are **retried**, so handlers must be idempotent. They arrive **out of order**, so a `succeeded` and a `refunded` for the same payment may land in either sequence. And they carry a **snapshot**, which may be older than the object's current state by the time you read it.

The rule that falls out: **never apply a delta, apply a state.** A handler that says "on `refunded`, subtract the amount" is broken by a duplicate delivery. A handler that fetches the payment and writes its current status, conditional on not moving backwards, is correct under duplication and reordering both.

### Payment methods are not interchangeable

The taxonomy matters because each row is a different lifecycle you support forever.

- **Cards.** Near-instant, universally supported, expensive (percentage-based), expire, and reversible by chargeback for months. Renewal failure rates in the mid-single digits to low teens are normal, which is the entire reason 15.2b exists.
- **Bank debits — ACH, SEPA Direct Debit, Bacs.** Pull-based, cheap and usually flat-fee, asynchronous over days, and returnable long after: SEPA allows eight weeks unconditionally and thirteen months for unauthorised collections. Excellent for large recurring invoices, terrible for anything where you must know quickly.
- **Bank transfers and invoicing (push).** The customer's finance team initiates. Nothing can decline; payments simply fail to arrive, and matching an incoming transfer to an invoice by reference is its own reconciliation problem. The default above a certain contract size whether you like it or not.
- **Wallets — Apple Pay, Google Pay, PayPal.** Mostly tokenized cards underneath, with materially better conversion and, for PayPal, its own dispute regime running parallel to the networks'.
- **Local methods — iDEAL, Bancontact, Pix, UPI, Boleto, konbini.** Often push-based, often irreversible, frequently requiring a redirect, and many of them **cannot be charged off-session at all**, which quietly makes them unusable for subscriptions without a separate flow.

Two properties are all your subscription logic actually cares about: **can this method be charged without the customer present**, and **how long can this payment be reversed**. Model those two explicitly per method, and the rest is provider detail.

### Your ledger is not your provider's balance

The provider's dashboard shows their view of money they hold on your behalf, net of fees, refunds, disputes, reserves, and conversion. Your system needs its own record of what was owed, attempted, received, refunded, and reversed — because those are different questions, asked by different people, and reconciling them is the only way to notice that a payment succeeded at the provider and never landed in your database. That thread continues in 15.4 and 15.5.

## When It's Appropriate

Every product that takes money processes payments, so the useful question is which option fits which situation.

- **Hosted checkout page (fully redirected)** — self-serve products, small teams, and anyone who would rather not own a checkout UI. Minimum PCI scope, maximum provider-side improvements you get for free, least control over the flow.
- **Embedded provider elements (iframed fields on your page)** — you control layout and copy, the card data still never touches you, and you stay near SAQ A. This is the right default for most SaaS.
- **Direct API with your own form** — appropriate when you are a payments company, or have a flow nobody supports. You are accepting SAQ D. Choose this on purpose or not at all.
- **Separate authorization and capture** — physical fulfilment, marketplace splits, or a final amount that differs from the estimate. Also the mechanism behind card verification at trial signup.
- **Bank debit alongside cards** — once a typical invoice is large enough that percentage fees exceed a flat charge by a wide margin, commonly somewhere in the few-hundred-dollar range, and a multi-day settlement is acceptable.
- **A merchant of record** — selling to consumers globally, where the blocker is not payments but registering for and remitting VAT, GST, and sales tax across dozens of jurisdictions.
- **More than one PSP** — at volumes where a one-point difference in authorization rate is a meaningful amount of money, with measurements showing your current provider underperforms in specific markets.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Building your own card form because the hosted one is off-brand.** The design cost is a week. The compliance cost is annual and permanent.
- **Multi-PSP failover before you have measured your authorization rate.** You are doubling your integration surface, your reconciliation surface, and your webhook surface to insure against an outage you have not had, while ignoring the far larger revenue line hiding in declines.
- **Your own tokenization vault.** There are real reasons — network tokens, provider portability at scale — and none of them apply before you are large enough to have a payments team.
- **Supporting a dozen local payment methods before you have customers in those countries.** Each one is a distinct lifecycle with distinct reversibility and off-session rules, not a checkbox.
- **Modelling auth and capture separately when you always capture immediately.** You are carrying a state machine for a case you do not have.
- **A generic payments abstraction over three providers when you integrate one.** The interface you invent will encode your first provider's assumptions anyway; you will discover which ones only when you migrate.

## Trade-offs

- **Your provider holds your money and can decide to keep holding it.** Reserves, rolling holds, and account reviews are normal responses to growth, chargebacks, or an unusual month. This is concentration risk that no amount of code removes.
- **Lock-in is deeper than the API.** The stored payment methods are the moat. Providers will do a vault-to-vault migration and it is a compliance-supervised project, not a config change — and every migrated credential's off-session agreement has to survive the move.
- **The entire system becomes webhook-shaped.** Payment state is eventually consistent, and any UI that reports it must be able to say "pending" honestly.
- **Fees become a permanent revenue line that engineering moves.** Authorization rates, retry behaviour, network tokens, and method mix are all worth real percentages, and nobody outside the team will look for them there.
- **Testing is structurally weak.** Sandboxes simulate issuers rather than being them, you cannot reproduce a real decline pattern, and dispute flows are nearly impossible to exercise end to end. Expect production to teach you things.
- **Compliance is a recurring obligation, not a project.** An annual questionnaire, scans where applicable, and a re-scoping conversation every time someone adds a field or a log line.
- **Revenue is reversible for months**, so any consumer of "paid" needs an undo path — including the reports your board has already seen.
- **Every payment method added is supported forever.** Removal is a customer-facing migration.

## Alternatives

**A merchant of record.** Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, FastSpring. They are the seller; they own tax registration and remittance worldwide, chargebacks, and invoicing. You get a materially simpler system and a materially higher fee, and you give up parts of the customer relationship — their name appears on the statement, and some enterprise buyers will object. For a global self-serve product, this trade is frequently correct and frequently dismissed too quickly.

**App store billing.** Not an alternative so much as a constraint: if you distribute through iOS or Android and sell digital goods consumed in the app, platform billing may be mandatory, with its own receipt-validation model, its own subscription state machine, and a 15–30% rate. Products with both web and app purchase paths end up maintaining two billing integrations and reconciling entitlements between them.

**Invoice and bank transfer only.** No PSP at all. Entirely viable for enterprise B2B, and it does not remove work — it moves it to accounts receivable: matching incoming transfers to invoices, chasing late payment, and handling partial payments, which cards never do.

**Payment orchestration** — Spreedly, Primer, or similar in front of multiple PSPs. Routing, redundancy, and negotiating leverage, paid for with a lowest-common-denominator feature set and one more party in the chain to debug.

**Crypto and stablecoin settlement.** Honestly: irreversible, which removes chargebacks and removes your ability to make a support problem go away with a refund. Real for some cross-border and high-fee corridors, still a niche with its own compliance surface, and not a replacement for card acceptance in any consumer market.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — hosted checkout, cards, auto-capture, one success webhook.** Correct to start here; it is also where a surprising number of products correctly stop for a year.

**L1 — idempotency keys and a local attempts table.** Every payment attempt is a row in your database before the call, keyed by invoice and attempt number, updated by the result. *Signal:* the first duplicate charge, or the first timeout nobody could resolve.

**L2 — webhook-driven state, including the unhappy events.** Payment state comes from webhooks; `dispute.created`, `charge.refunded`, and `payment.failed` are wired to something rather than dropped. *Signal:* you learned about a chargeback from an email.

**L3 — set up off-session properly.** Authentication at signup, stored agreement, `off_session` on renewals, and an explicit handler for `authentication_required` that hands off to customer contact. *Signal:* your first EEA or UK customer — ideally before, since retrofitting means re-authenticating existing customers.

**L4 — method diversity where the economics justify it.** Bank debit for large invoices, wallets for conversion. Model reversibility and off-session capability per method rather than branching on provider strings. *Signal:* fees on large accounts becoming visibly worth engineering time.

**L5 — reconciliation against payouts.** A daily job that ties your attempts, the provider's payments, and the actual payout together, and alerts on the difference. *Signal:* the first month-end where finance's number and yours disagreed and it took a day to find out why.

**L6 — routing, network tokens, and authorization-rate work.** *Signal:* declines measured in real money, with evidence they are addressable.

## Implementation

Go, because the central point of this article is a distinction that Go's error handling refuses to let you blur: a timeout is not a decline, and a language that returns `(result, error)` makes you say which one you got.

```go
// The key includes the attempt. Deriving it from the invoice alone means
// a deliberate retry next week silently replays this week's decline.
func chargeKey(invoiceID string, attempt int) string {
	return fmt.Sprintf("inv_%s_try_%d", invoiceID, attempt)
}

func (b *Biller) Attempt(ctx context.Context, inv Invoice, attempt int) (Outcome, error) {
	key := chargeKey(inv.ID, attempt)

	// Recorded BEFORE the call. If the process dies mid-request, the row is
	// what tells a later run that an attempt with this key may exist upstream.
	if err := b.store.RecordAttempt(ctx, inv.ID, attempt, key); err != nil {
		return Outcome{}, err
	}

	res, err := b.psp.CreatePayment(ctx, psp.Request{
		Amount:         inv.AmountDue,
		Currency:       inv.Currency,
		PaymentMethod:  inv.Customer.DefaultMethodID,
		OffSession:     true, // nobody is in a browser at 03:00
		Confirm:        true,
		IdempotencyKey: key,
	})

	switch {
	case err == nil:
		return b.apply(ctx, inv, res)

	case errors.Is(err, context.DeadlineExceeded), psp.IsTransport(err):
		// The most important branch in the file. This is not a decline.
		// The payment may have succeeded. Do not create a new attempt, and
		// do not mark the invoice failed — go and find out what happened.
		return b.resolve(ctx, inv, key)

	default:
		return Outcome{}, err
	}
}

// resolve replays the same idempotency key. The provider returns the original
// result rather than creating a second payment; if it never saw the request,
// this is the first one.
func (b *Biller) resolve(ctx context.Context, inv Invoice, key string) (Outcome, error) {
	res, err := b.psp.CreatePaymentIdempotent(ctx, key)
	if err != nil {
		return Outcome{}, err // still unknown: leave the attempt open, alert, retry later
	}
	return b.apply(ctx, inv, res)
}
```

Leaving the attempt open rather than resolving it optimistically is the whole point. An unresolved attempt is a visible, alertable state; a guess is an invisible one.

The outcomes themselves are not a boolean, and naming them is most of the design:

```go
type Outcome struct {
	State   State  // Succeeded | Processing | RequiresAction | Declined
	Code    string // issuer decline code, when State == Declined
	Method  Method // carries CanChargeOffSession and ReversalWindow
}
```

`Processing` is the bank-debit case: accepted, not money. `RequiresAction` off-session is the dead end — there is no server-side recovery, and the only correct response is to stop and hand the invoice to the customer-contact path in 15.2b. Treating it as a decline and retrying it produces a loop that can never succeed.

The return path, where ordering is not guaranteed:

```go
func (h *Hooks) apply(ctx context.Context, e Event) error {
	// Webhooks are retried and arrive out of order, and the payload may
	// already be stale. Re-read the authoritative object instead of trusting
	// the snapshot, and never apply a delta — apply a state.
	p, err := h.psp.GetPayment(ctx, e.PaymentID)
	if err != nil {
		return err // ack was already sent; this retries out of band
	}
	// Conditional on version: a late-arriving older state must not win.
	return h.store.SetPaymentState(ctx, p.ID, p.Status, p.Version)
}
```

Re-reading costs one API call per event, which is nothing at billing volumes and would be unacceptable at streaming volumes — where you would instead trust the payload and rely solely on the monotonic version. Worth knowing which situation you are in before copying either.

## Further Reading

- **PCI SSC's SAQ A, SAQ A-EP, and SAQ D documents**, read side by side. Not enjoyable, and the clearest possible statement of what "the card touched our server" costs annually.
- **PCI DSS v4.0.1**, particularly the scoping guidance. The definition of a connected system is the part that surprises people.
- **Stripe's documentation on payment intents, idempotent requests, and webhook best practices.** The payment intent state machine is the converged vocabulary for the auth/action/capture lifecycle, worth knowing even on another provider.
- **Stripe's SCA and 3-D Secure guides**, plus the **EBA regulatory technical standards on strong customer authentication**. The exemptions are the reason the signup flow and the renewal flow are coupled.
- **Adyen's authorisation-rate and payment-method documentation.** The most substantial public material on why identical charges get different answers in different markets.
- **Nacha operating rules (ACH)** and the **SEPA Direct Debit rulebook** — return reason codes, mandate obligations, and the return windows that make "paid" provisional for weeks.
- **Visa's and Mastercard's dispute monitoring program thresholds**, for the numbers at which chargebacks stop being a cost and become an existential problem.
- **Brandur Leach, "Implementing Stripe-like idempotency keys in Postgres."** The best available walkthrough of doing this yourself, including the parts that are harder than they look.

A **TypeScript sibling** would be worth writing and would cover what this article structurally cannot. The Go above is the server-initiated half — the renewal at 3am with nobody present. The on-session half lives almost entirely in the browser: mounting provider-hosted fields, confirming a payment client-side, handling a 3-D Secure challenge that suspends the flow and resumes on redirect, and doing all of it without your own code ever seeing a card number. The correctness concerns there are different ones — return-URL handling, resuming an interrupted confirmation, and not double-submitting when a user taps twice — and none of them are visible from the server. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry past payments: **any call that changes the real world has three outcomes, and the third one is the design.** Approved and declined are both answers; a timeout is the absence of one, and the difference between a system that handles it and a system that guesses is an idempotency key and a row written before the request. Charges are the loudest case because the guess costs money in public, but the shape is identical for sending an email, provisioning a server, publishing a message, or calling any API that does something rather than returning something.
