# Invoicing & the Billing Run

_Module 15.4a — Subscription & Billing Management_

## The Problem

On the first of the month a cron job wakes up, iterates every active subscription, and charges it. Here is the order in which that arrangement falls apart.

**The loop does not survive its own growth.** At 300 subscriptions the run takes four minutes. At 30,000 it takes seven hours, and at hour three a routine deploy restarts the process. Which subscriptions were charged? The only record of the run is that it was running. Re-running it charges the first twelve thousand customers twice; not re-running it leaves eighteen thousand unbilled, and nobody notices the second outcome for weeks because customers do not write in about not being charged.

**The invoice is a template, not a document.** The PDF is rendered on demand from current data. A customer asks for last March's invoice; you render it again, and it now shows your new company address, the current price, and the current tax rate. It is a different document with the same filename, and it does not match the one they paid — which, to their accountant and to an auditor, is the entire problem.

**Numbering by `COUNT(*) + 1`.** Two concurrent workers produce two invoice 4471. A failed run leaves a gap at 4480. In a fair number of jurisdictions, a gap in a sequential invoice series is precisely what a tax inspector asks you to explain, and "our batch job crashed" is an answer you have to give in person.

**There is no invoice object, only charges.** Which means your model cannot express: an invoice issued on 30-day terms and not yet paid, an invoice settled in two payments, an invoice paid entirely from a credit balance with no money moving at all, or an invoice issued in error and voided. Each of those arrives as a feature request that turns out to be a schema change.

**Receipts sent to people who asked for invoices.** They are different documents. A receipt evidences that money was paid. An invoice states an obligation and carries fields a tax authority requires — your legal entity and tax number, theirs, the tax breakdown, the sequential number, the date of supply. Sending the first when someone asked for the second means their finance team cannot book it.

**Delivery to whoever signed up.** The invoice email goes to the founder's address at a 400-person customer. Accounts payable has never seen it. The account goes past due for reasons that have nothing to do with willingness to pay, and dunning (15.2b) starts chasing a person who cannot help.

**Support "fixes" an invoice** by editing the row. The customer downloaded the PDF yesterday. Two versions of one document now exist and only one of them is yours.

Two threads, and they are the two halves of this article.

**An invoice is a document, not a record of a payment.** It is issued at a moment, numbered in a sequence, frozen after issue, and legally distinct from both the payment and the receipt.

**The thing that produces invoices is a large idempotent batch job**, and that is a different engineering problem from everything else in this module — one where the characteristic failure is silent, and silent in the direction that loses money.

## What It Is

An **invoice** is a document stating what a customer owes, for a defined set of items or period, issued on a date, identified by a unique number, carrying whatever fields the jurisdiction of supply requires.

Its lifecycle is short and the transitions are where all the meaning lives:

```
 draft ────finalize────► open ────payment────► paid
   │                      │
   │  mutable:            ├────void────────► void          issued in error; never deleted
   │  lines accumulate    └────write off───► uncollectible  validly owed, not collectible
   │  all period
```

**Finalization is the irreversible act.** It assigns the number, freezes the lines, snapshots the parties and the rates, and produces the document. Before it, the invoice is a working draft that lines are added to throughout the period. After it, nothing changes — corrections are credit notes (15.3b).

Void and uncollectible are not synonyms and accounting treats them differently: void means the document should never have existed, uncollectible means the obligation was real and you have given up on it.

A **billing run** is the scheduled process that, for every subscription due, assembles a draft, finalizes it, and hands it to collection.

Two disambiguations.

**Invoice, receipt, credit note.** Obligation, proof of payment, correction. Three documents, three moments, three sets of required fields.

**Invoice and charge are not the same object.** One invoice may be settled by two payments, by a credit balance with no money moving, or by nothing at all. Systems that model only charges cannot represent the gap between owing and paying, which is the gap all business-to-business billing lives in.

There are also two billing modes, and most products need both eventually. **Charge automatically** — a card on file, collected at finalization, where the invoice is effectively a receipt with extra fields. **Send invoice** — terms, a due date, and a customer who pays by bank transfer when their AP cycle gets to it. The second one is where invoicing becomes a real subsystem rather than a rendering concern.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Invoice PDFs are rendered on demand from current data.
- Invoice numbers come from a count, duplicate under concurrency, or have gaps nobody can account for.
- Invoices are corrected by updating the row.
- There is no invoice object — only charges — so an unpaid or partially paid invoice cannot be represented.
- The billing run is one loop with no per-subscription record of its outcome.
- Re-running the billing run is considered dangerous, and everyone knows it.
- Nobody can answer "did every subscription get billed this month" without writing a query by hand.
- Everything bills at midnight on the 1st, and your provider rate-limits you every month.
- A subscription anchored on the 31st behaves unpredictably in February.
- Invoices go to the signup email; there is no billing-contact concept.
- Nobody knows whether an invoice was actually delivered.
- Invoices are not archived as files; the PDF is a view over mutable rows.
- There is no way to void an invoice issued in error.

## How It Works

### Snapshot at finalization

The finalized invoice must carry **copies** of everything it asserts: your legal entity, address, and tax identifiers; the customer's name, address, and tax identifier; each line's description, unit price, and quantity; the tax rates applied — the rates themselves, not a reference to a rate that can be edited; the currency and any exchange rate used; the dates of supply; and the payment terms.

This inverts 15.1's rule, and the inversion is worth stating plainly because it looks like a contradiction. The *agreement* references the catalogue, because an agreement is a living thing that must track a versioned price. The *document* copies everything, because a document is a frozen assertion about a moment that has passed. Reference for state, copy for evidence.

### Numbering

Uniqueness is the minimum. Beyond it, many jurisdictions require the sequence to be **gapless**, per legal entity and often per year, and some require a separate series per document type. If you sell into those markets, gaps are a compliance question rather than an aesthetic one.

Gapless numbering is a serialization point, and the obvious implementation is wrong in a specific way worth knowing: **database sequences are non-transactional**. In PostgreSQL, `nextval` is not rolled back when the surrounding transaction aborts, so a failed finalization consumes a number and leaves exactly the gap you were trying to avoid. The correct shape is a counter row read with a lock inside the same transaction as the finalization, so that a rollback takes the number back with it. That costs you a contended row during the billing run, which is a real throughput consideration and a genuine reason to want unique-but-not-gapless numbering if your jurisdictions allow it.

Prefix by entity, document type, and year — `INV-2026-000412`, `CN-2026-000019` — and resist encoding anything else. Customer identifiers inside invoice numbers become a privacy question the first time an invoice is forwarded.

### The billing run as a batch job

Five properties separate a run that survives from a loop that does not.

**Idempotent by natural key.** The unit of work is (subscription, period), and that pair is also the idempotency key. A unique index on it means a re-run cannot double-invoice, no matter what the job's own bookkeeping believes. Correctness comes from the database, not from the runner's memory.

**Resumable.** Each unit's outcome is recorded — generated, already done, failed, skipped — so a restart processes what remains. A run with no per-unit record is a run that can only be executed exactly once, safely, ever.

**Bounded parallelism.** Fan out with a worker pool sized to your provider's rate limits, not to your CPU count. The bottleneck is someone else's API.

**Load spread across time.** Anniversary billing distributes naturally because customers signed up on different days. Calendar-month billing piles everything onto one hour, which your payment provider will notice before you do. If customers require aligned invoices, shard the run across the day by a hash of the subscription.

**A defined answer for the month-end problem.** A subscription anchored on the 31st has no anniversary in February. The standard answer is to clamp to the last day of the month and — the part that gets missed — not to let the clamp become permanent, so a March invoice still lands on the 31st.

It is also worth separating **generation from collection** into two passes. Computing what is owed and taking the money are different operations with different failure modes, and splitting them means you can generate, inspect, hold a suspicious invoice, and re-run the first pass without any risk of charging anyone. For usage-based billing where a single invoice can be large and wrong, a review window between the two passes is cheap insurance.

Finally, **instrument for absence**. Expected units versus produced invoices, per run, alerting on the difference. An over-billing bug is reported by customers within hours. An under-billing bug is discovered at quarter-end, by finance, in a meeting.

### Delivery, and receipts

The invoice email is second only to the dunning email in consequence, and it lives in the same deliverability pool as everything else you send (14.1). Send it on the transactional stream, from the transactional subdomain, and make sure a marketing suppression cannot swallow it.

Send it to a **billing contact** — a role on the account, editable, distinct from the person who signed up. Attach the PDF and provide a hosted link: some AP systems want a file to forward, some want a URL to pull. Track whether it was delivered; "we sent it" and "they have it" are different claims, and only one of them justifies starting dunning.

A **receipt** is a separate document issued on payment. For card-on-file SaaS, most companies send the invoice marked paid and that is generally accepted — but be aware that a finance team asking for a receipt may mean something specific, and in some jurisdictions receipts have their own requirements.

And a landscape item most developers meet by surprise: **structured e-invoicing mandates**. A growing number of countries require B2B invoices to be submitted electronically, in a defined format, to or through a government platform — Italy's SdI, India's IRN system, and Brazil's NF-e are long-standing examples, and the EU's VAT-in-the-Digital-Age direction points the same way. Where these apply, a PDF attached to an email is not an invoice. If you sell B2B into those markets, this is a compliance integration, not a rendering feature, and it belongs in the plan early.

### Storage

Finalized invoices belong in immutable storage with a retention measured in years — commonly seven to ten, driven by tax law rather than by you. That retention **outlives the customer**: when a tenant is deleted, invoices are the canonical example of 5.3a's `RETAIN` class, kept deliberately and for a stated reason rather than by oversight.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A real invoice object rather than charges** — as soon as you have business customers, tax obligations, or any payment that is not an instant card capture. Which is to say, almost immediately for B2B.
- **Send-invoice mode with terms** — enterprise deals, purchase orders, bank transfer. It is not an optional mode above a certain deal size; it is how those customers pay.
- **Your own invoice rendering** — when jurisdictional fields, multi-entity selling, or contractual layout requirements exceed what your provider's template supports. Not for branding.
- **Two-phase runs with a review hold** — when a single invoice can be large enough that an error is an incident rather than a ticket. Usage-based enterprise billing is the obvious case.
- **Anniversary billing** — the default, because it spreads load for free.
- **Calendar-month billing** — when customers need invoices aligned to their own accounting periods, which many finance departments genuinely do.
- **Gapless numbering** — when you sell into jurisdictions that require it, and then not optionally.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Building a PDF renderer when your provider's output is adequate.** Invoice layout is heavily constrained by law and by convention, and it is not a place where design differentiates you.
- **Gapless numbering with no obligation to it.** You are adding a global lock to the most parallelizable job you own, for nothing.
- **A homegrown billing scheduler** when your provider runs cycles and emits webhooks. Most products should consume invoices rather than produce them, and should keep doing so until their pricing stops fitting.
- **E-invoicing integrations before selling into those markets.** Each one is a country-specific project with its own certification; build the one you need when you need it.
- **Parallelizing a run of 500 subscriptions.** A sequential run with per-unit outcome records is correct, debuggable, and finishes before you get coffee.
- **Generating an invoice per event** in a usage-based product. Accrue into a draft; invoice on a schedule or a threshold.

## Trade-offs

- **Immutability means corrections multiply documents.** One mistake produces an invoice, a credit note, and a replacement, and every report thereafter has to net them correctly.
- **Gapless numbering caps your throughput.** One contended row, held inside the transaction that finalizes an invoice, during the busiest job of the month.
- **Snapshots duplicate data and create legitimate disagreements.** The invoiced price and the current price differ on purpose, and everyone who queries billing data will be confused by that exactly once.
- **The billing run is the highest-consequence scheduled job you own**, and its worst failures are silent. Errors are easy; absence is not.
- **Two-phase runs add latency and a human step** that someone has to actually perform, every cycle, including in December.
- **A legally significant document now depends on email deliverability**, which is not a dependency anyone chose deliberately.
- **Self-rendered invoices mean you own jurisdictional field requirements forever**, and those requirements change without asking you.
- **Retention obligations conflict with deletion requests.** You will keep invoices belonging to customers who asked to be forgotten, and you need to be able to say why.

## Alternatives

**Provider-generated invoices.** Stripe Invoicing, Chargebee, Recurly, and the rest generate, number, render, deliver, and archive. This is the correct default, and the reason to leave it is almost never aesthetics — it is a pricing shape, a multi-entity structure, or a jurisdiction their template does not cover.

**Accounting system as the invoice authority.** Once finance has NetSuite, Xero, or QuickBooks as the system of record, invoices are frequently generated there from data your application pushes. Your app stops being the source of truth for invoices and becomes an upstream feed, which is a significant architectural change and often the right one.

**Order-to-cash platforms** for enterprise AR — dunning by humans, collections, aging reports, and payment application against open invoices. Relevant when net-terms invoicing becomes a meaningful share of revenue.

**No invoices at all.** Pure consumer card businesses in permissive jurisdictions can issue receipts and stop. Fine until the first business customer, which is usually sooner than expected.

**Threshold-based invoicing.** Bill when accrued charges exceed an amount, or at period end, whichever comes first. This is how cloud providers manage credit exposure on usage-based pricing, and it is a genuinely different model from a calendar schedule: it bounds how much a customer can consume before you find out they cannot pay.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — charges only, no invoice object.** Fine for consumer card billing and nothing else.

**L1 — an invoice object with a lifecycle**, and a unique index on (subscription, period). Provider-rendered documents. *Signal:* the first customer who asks for an invoice rather than a receipt.

**L2 — the run becomes units of work.** Per-unit outcome rows, a run summary, and an alert on expected-versus-produced. *Signal:* the first run that failed halfway, or the first time someone asked whether everyone got billed.

**L3 — finalization as a real transition.** Snapshot the parties, lines, and rates; assign the number from a transactional counter; write the document to immutable storage. *Signal:* the first invoice that displayed differently than when it was issued.

**L4 — two billing modes and a billing contact.** Send-invoice with terms and a due date, a billing-contact role, delivery tracking. *Signal:* the first enterprise customer, or the first past-due account that turned out never to have received anything.

**L5 — two-phase runs and load spreading.** Generation separated from collection, a hold path for anomalous invoices, sharding across the day. *Signal:* an invoice that was wrong and collected before anyone looked at it.

**L6 — jurisdictional work.** Gapless series per entity, e-invoicing integrations, archival and retention policy. *Signal:* a market entry, not a hunch.

## Implementation

Java, and specifically Java with a batch framework in view, because Spring Batch's job repository is the clearest existing expression of "record what completed" — and looking at it makes obvious why you still need the database constraint underneath.

Finalization, where the two hard details live:

```java
@Transactional
public Invoice finalize(UUID draftId) {
    Invoice inv = invoices.lockDraft(draftId);           // SELECT ... FOR UPDATE

    // Deliberately NOT a database sequence. In PostgreSQL nextval() is not
    // rolled back with the transaction, so a failed finalization consumes the
    // number anyway — producing exactly the gap that gapless numbering exists
    // to prevent. A counter row read inside this transaction rolls back too.
    String number = numbering.nextWithinTransaction(
        inv.sellerEntityId(), inv.issueDate().getYear());

    // Snapshot, not reference. This inverts the catalogue rule from 15.1 on
    // purpose: an agreement tracks a versioned price, a document freezes an
    // assertion about a moment that has already happened.
    inv.freeze(new InvoiceSnapshot(
        sellers.legalDetails(inv.sellerEntityId()),   // name, address, tax ids
        customers.billingDetails(inv.customerId()),   // name, address, tax id
        inv.lines(),                                  // descriptions, unit prices, quantities
        taxes.ratesApplied(inv),                      // the rates themselves, not rate ids
        inv.currency(), inv.fxRate()));

    inv.setNumber(number);
    inv.setStatus(OPEN);
    return invoices.save(inv);
}
```

The unit of work, where idempotency comes from the schema rather than from the runner:

```java
@Entity
@Table(name = "invoices", uniqueConstraints =
       @UniqueConstraint(columnNames = {"subscription_id", "period_start"}))
public class Invoice { /* ... */ }

void runFor(Subscription sub, BillingPeriod period) {
    try {
        Invoice draft = invoices.createDraft(sub.id(), period);   // violates the constraint on a re-run
        draft.addAll(proration.linesFor(sub, period));            // 15.3a
        draft.addAll(usage.linesFor(sub, period));                // 15.1's aggregate — the same
        draft.addAll(recurring.linesFor(sub, period));            //   function the dashboard reads
        finalize(draft.id());
        outcomes.record(sub.id(), period, GENERATED);
    } catch (DuplicateKeyException e) {
        outcomes.record(sub.id(), period, ALREADY_DONE);          // a re-run, not a failure
    }
}
```

Two things about that `catch`. Treating a duplicate key as success rather than as an error is what makes the run safely re-runnable by anyone, at any time, including a half-awake engineer at 6am — which is when it will actually be re-run. And the usage lines come from the same aggregate function the customer's dashboard calls, because a second query that computes "usage" differently is the bug 15.1 spent a section on.

Collection is a separate pass over `status = OPEN`, calling into 15.2a. Keeping it separate is what makes a bad generation re-runnable without anyone being charged.

Spring Batch would give you chunking, restartability, and a job repository that records item-level progress, and all of that is genuinely useful at scale. It does not remove the unique index. When a framework's record of what completed and the database's record disagree — after a hard kill, a network partition, a restored snapshot — the database is the one holding the customer's invoice, and it has to be the one that wins.

## Further Reading

- **Stripe's invoicing documentation** — the invoice lifecycle, invoice items accumulating into a draft, finalization, and `auto_advance`. The state machine is the same one described here, and their docs are explicit about what finalization freezes.
- **EN 16931**, the European standard for the semantic data model of an electronic invoice, and **Peppol BIS Billing 3.0** built on it. Read at least the field list; it is the clearest available specification of what an invoice legally *is*, stripped of layout.
- **Country e-invoicing documentation** for any market you sell into — Italy's SdI, India's e-invoice/IRN scheme, Brazil's NF-e — and the EU's VAT in the Digital Age proposals for where this is heading.
- **PostgreSQL documentation on sequences**, specifically the note that sequence values are not rolled back. Two sentences that decide your numbering implementation.
- **Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, chapter 10.** Batch processing, deterministic re-runs, and why idempotence beats exactly-once. The billing run is that chapter's subject with a legal deadline attached.
- **Spring Batch reference documentation**, particularly the job repository and restart semantics — worth reading even if you never use it, as a catalogue of the concerns any batch runner has to address somehow.

A **Go sibling** would reveal the half this article gets for free from a framework. Written without a batch engine, the run becomes an explicit worker pool with `errgroup` limits, a rate limiter shaped to the payment provider's quota, a checkpoint the operator can see, and graceful shutdown that stops accepting new units while letting in-flight ones finish — all of which Spring Batch supplies and therefore hides. Building it by hand makes the actual requirements visible, and makes clear which of them the framework was solving and which it was merely surviving. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying past billing: **for every scheduled job, ask which failure is silent.** Doing too much is loud — duplicate invoices, double emails, repeated webhooks all generate complaints within hours. Doing too little is quiet, and the quiet direction is usually the expensive one: invoices not generated, notifications not sent, records not synced, exports not produced. Error rates and exception dashboards only catch the loud failures, because absence throws nothing. The instrument that catches the other half is always the same shape — count what you expected to produce, count what you produced, and alert on the difference.
