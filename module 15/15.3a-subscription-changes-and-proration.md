# Subscription Changes & Proration

_Module 15.3a — Subscription & Billing Management_

## The Problem

A customer on the $49 plan upgrades to the $99 plan on day 12 of a 30-day cycle. What do you charge them?

That question has about a dozen defensible answers, and a team will pick one in a standup without noticing that they have just set policy. Here is the sequence in which the consequences arrive.

**The first version is a column update.** `subscription.priceId = "business"; save()`. The customer gets Business features immediately and pays nothing more until the first of the month. That is eighteen days of the expensive plan for free. It is also, for anyone who notices, a permanent discount: upgrade on the 20th, use the feature, downgrade on the 29th, repeat forever.

**The second version charges the difference.** $50 × 18/30 = $30. Reasonable, and it immediately raises the mirror-image question: a customer downgrades on day 20, so do you owe them $16.67? To their card — a real refund, whose processing fee you do not get back and which opens its own dispute window? Or as a credit toward the next invoice, which requires a credit balance you do not have?

**Seats make it frequent.** Adding three seats on day 12 is the same arithmetic, but a 200-person customer does it forty times a month. The invoice now has forty proration lines, and their finance team has written to ask what line 27 is for.

**Then the timing question, which nobody asked out loud.** When does the change take effect — now, or at period end? And must upgrades and downgrades answer that the same way? Almost every mature billing system answers *no*: upgrades apply immediately, downgrades apply at renewal. That asymmetry looks like laziness and is in fact a deliberate policy that eliminates the entire refund problem.

**Annual makes the numbers big.** A customer three months into a $588 annual plan upgrades to $1,188. The unused nine months are worth $441. Does the new annual term start today, or does their renewal date stay in March? Both are defensible, they produce different invoices, and whichever you implement first becomes the one you are stuck explaining.

**And metered items break the model entirely.** You cannot prorate usage. Those API calls already happened, at a price that was in effect when they happened. If the plan change also changed the included allowance, what allowance applies for this period? Nobody will guess your answer, including you, in six months.

The thread: **a subscription change is not an update to a row. It is a dated event that splits a billing period into two priced segments.** Everything difficult here follows from the fact that you charged in advance for time you are now re-selling.

```
|------------------- billing period, 30 days -------------------|
|------ 12 days @ $49/mo ------|-------- 18 days @ $99/mo -------|
                                ▲ change effective here

already collected           $49.00   (charged in advance on day 0)
credit, unused time         -$29.40   $49 × 18/30
charge, remaining time      +$59.40   $99 × 18/30
                            ───────
due now                      $30.00
```

The "charge the difference" shortcut gives the same $30 here only because both prices cover the same period length. The moment the cycles differ — monthly to annual, or a quantity change on a plan with a different anchor — the shortcut is simply wrong, which is why real systems always emit two lines instead of one.

## What It Is

**Proration** is dividing a periodic charge across a partial period, so that a mid-cycle change is priced by elapsed time rather than by the whole period.

The mental shift that makes it tractable: a subscription item is not "a price." It is **a price applied over an interval**. A change closes one interval and opens another. Proration is the arithmetic that settles the closed interval — a **credit for unused time** — and prices the new one — a **charge for remaining time**.

The disambiguation that matters most is not against a neighbouring concept but against two other decisions that get silently bundled with it. A subscription change is a point in a three-dimensional space, and the dimensions are independent:

1. **When does the change take effect?** Immediately, at period end, or on a specified date.
2. **How is the affected period priced?** Prorate, charge the full new price, or charge nothing extra.
3. **When does the money move?** Invoice and collect now, add the lines to the next invoice, or post them to a credit balance.

Almost every billing bug in this area is two parts of a system assuming different coordinates. The entitlement service thinks the change took effect now; the invoice thinks it takes effect at renewal. The UI says "you'll be charged $30"; the collection policy says next invoice. Naming the three separately is most of the fix.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A plan change is an `UPDATE` on the subscription row, with no record that a change occurred.
- Upgrades take effect immediately and bill at the next renewal with no proration, so there is a free window and someone has found it.
- Downgrades issue refunds to the card.
- Invoices carry dozens of proration lines that support cannot explain to a customer.
- Changing a plan silently moves the billing anniversary, or silently fails to.
- Your system and your provider both compute a prorated amount, and they differ by cents.
- Proration is computed in whole days, and "a day" means the server's timezone.
- Removing a seat immediately reduces the bill, and a customer has worked out that seats can be added and removed around the renewal date.
- "Downgrade at renewal" is implemented as a `pending_plan` column, or a calendar reminder for a human.
- You cannot reconstruct why a four-month-old invoice contained the amounts it did.
- Entitlements change at the moment of billing rather than at the effective date of the change.
- A plan change alters a metered allowance and nobody can state what this period's allowance is.

## How It Works

### The three decisions, and the combinations worth supporting

You do not need the full cube. Five named change types cover nearly everything:

| change | effective | pricing | collection |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Upgrade** | immediately | prorated | now, or next invoice |
| **Downgrade** | at period end | no proration needed | next invoice |
| **Seat increase** | immediately | prorated | next invoice |
| **Seat decrease** | immediately for access, period end for billing | none | — |
| **Cycle change** (monthly → annual) | immediately | prorated, cycle resets | now |

Two of those rows are doing quiet work. **Downgrade at period end** removes refunds, credit balances, and downgrade arbitrage in one stroke, which is why it is the near-universal default in self-serve SaaS. **Seat changes invoiced next cycle** avoids forty payment attempts a month — and every payment attempt is another chance to decline, which drags 15.2b's machinery into what should have been a click.

### The arithmetic

Credit the unused portion of the old item, charge the used-from-now portion of the new one, both against the same interval. Three details determine whether it holds up.

**Prorate by elapsed time, not by calendar days.** Days feel natural and quietly encode a timezone: a customer upgrading at 23:58 their time upgraded the following day in yours, and February prices differently from March if the denominator is the month. Compute on seconds, store the effective timestamp in UTC, and present it as days if days read better on the invoice. Presentation and arithmetic are allowed to differ; they just cannot be the same variable.

**Round once, at the line.** The ratio needs more precision than money does. Carry it unrounded, apply it, round each resulting line to the currency's minor unit, and let the invoice total be the sum of rounded lines. Rounding the ratio first, or rounding only the total, both produce invoices a customer can arrive at a different answer for — and on a prorated invoice, they will check.

**Decide whether the cycle resets.** Keeping the anniversary means the change lives inside the existing period and the renewal date does not move. Resetting means the old period is settled now and a fresh one starts today. Monthly-to-monthly changes almost always want to keep the anniversary; monthly-to-annual almost always wants a reset, because an annual term that expires eleven days from now is not what anyone bought.

### What cannot be prorated

**Metered items.** Usage is priced when it occurs, at whatever rate was in effect then. There is no partial period to divide, and a system that tries produces double-counting or silent discounts. Exclude metered items from the proration path explicitly rather than by convention.

Which leaves the harder question the plan change actually raised: **what happens to the included allowance?** A customer moves from a plan including 100,000 calls to one including 500,000, on day 12. Three coherent answers exist — prorate both allowances by time, grant the new allowance in full, or take the larger. None is obviously right, all are defensible, and the only wrong move is not choosing, because then the answer is whatever your aggregation query happens to do. Write it on the pricing page.

The same applies to rate tiers: if usage is billed on graduated tiers and the tier table changed mid-period, the meter has to be split at the change timestamp and each segment priced against its own table. That is real work, and it is the reason many systems restrict usage-price changes to period boundaries and leave it at that. That restriction is a legitimate answer, not a cop-out — but it needs to be a stated rule rather than an undocumented limitation.

**One-time fees and setup charges** are also outside proration by definition; they attach to an event, not to an interval.

### Scheduled changes

Once a change is a dated event, a *future* change is the same object with a later timestamp. That single representation covers downgrade-at-renewal, a price increase with 30 days' notice, a trial converting, and an enterprise contract renewing at a new rate — four features that otherwise become four columns.

It needs one rule to be safe: **a new change supersedes pending ones**. A downgrade scheduled for the 1st, followed by an upgrade on the 20th, must not quietly undo the upgrade eleven days later. Whether supersession is total or per-field is your call; having no rule is not.

### Entitlements follow the effective date, not the invoice

By design these are different moments. A downgrade effective at period end means the customer keeps the higher tier for eighteen more days while the invoice was written today. The entitlement resolver must therefore answer *what is in effect as of now*, derived from the subscription's timeline, rather than reading the most recent invoice or a cached plan name. This is the same requirement Module 5 states from the access side; the billing side is where it gets violated, because the invoice feels authoritative and is not.

### The change log

Every change is a durable row: what changed, effective when, requested by whom, through which interface, and which invoice lines it produced. Without it, a dispute about a four-month-old invoice is unanswerable, and the proration arithmetic is unreproducible because the inputs no longer exist anywhere. It is the same requirement as the usage aggregate in 15.1 for the same reason: money computed from state that has since moved on is only defensible if you kept the state.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Prorate when the amounts are large enough to be noticed** — annual plans, per-seat pricing with substantial counts, anything above roughly the price of lunch. Business customers reconcile invoices; consumers mostly do not.
- **Upgrade immediately, prorated, collected immediately** when you want the revenue now and the customer is upgrading because they need the feature now. The immediate charge also confirms the card still works, which is not nothing.
- **Upgrade immediately, prorated, collected on the next invoice** when changes are frequent or the amounts are small. Fewer payment attempts, fewer declines, one invoice to explain instead of a stream of receipts.
- **Downgrade at period end, no proration** as the default for self-serve. It is fair — they paid for the period and they get the period — and it deletes the refund problem entirely.
- **Reset the billing cycle** when the cycle length itself changes, or when a customer is signing a new term rather than adjusting an existing one.
- **Let the provider compute it** in essentially every case where your pricing fits their model.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Second-precision proration on a $10 monthly plan.** Whole-day proration, documented and applied consistently, is fine, and it is easier to explain than it is to defend a 43-cent discrepancy.
- **Building proration yourself when your provider does it.** This is the single most expensive unnecessary build in this module. The arithmetic is a half-day; the hundred edge cases around it — cycle anchors, trials, cancellations mid-change, currency rounding, scheduled changes colliding — are a quarter, and they have already been paid for by someone else.
- **Supporting every point in the three-dimensional space.** Five named change types and a rule against anything else will serve for years.
- **Refunding downgrades to the card.** You lose the processing fee, you open a dispute window, and you have created an arbitrage. A credit balance is better in nearly every case.
- **Prorating a trial.** There is nothing to divide.
- **Mid-period price changes on metered items** before you have a customer who needs it and a rate-splitting story you have written down.

## Trade-offs

- **Prorated invoices are harder to read**, and a percentage of them generate a support conversation. That cost is real, recurring, and proportional to how clever the arithmetic is.
- **Invoice amounts become unpredictable.** Customers with purchase orders and budget approvals genuinely dislike this, and it complicates your own revenue forecasting more than the sums involved would suggest.
- **Credits are revenue-negative in the current period**, which interacts with revenue recognition in ways finance will want to discuss (15.5).
- **Every immediate collection is a new decline opportunity.** A plan change that fails to charge leaves the subscription in a state nobody designed: upgraded, unpaid, mid-period.
- **"What plan are they on right now" acquires multiple candidate answers** — the subscription row, the pending change, the last invoice, the entitlement cache — and keeping them agreeing is ongoing work.
- **Time arithmetic is a permanent bug surface.** Daylight saving, month lengths, leap days, and client-supplied timestamps will all produce a wrong invoice eventually.
- **The asymmetry between upgrades and downgrades is a policy customers will discover.** It is defensible, it is standard, and it must be stated at the moment of the change rather than found in the terms of service afterwards.

## Alternatives

**No proration at all; changes take effect at renewal.** The simplest coherent policy, expressible in one sentence on the pricing page. The cost is that you cannot take upgrade money today, and an upgrade the customer cannot have until the 1st is an upgrade they may not make.

**Immediate upgrade, no charge until renewal.** Generous and trivially simple. Exploitable in exactly one way — upgrade, use, downgrade before the cycle ends — which is a real problem at consumer price points and mostly theoretical at B2B ones.

**Everything through a credit balance.** Changes post credits and debits to a balance that the next invoice settles. No refunds, no mid-cycle charges, no extra payment attempts, and one number to explain to the customer. It costs you a balance system with its own rules about expiry, refundability, and what happens on cancellation — which is 15.3b.

**Quantity-only pricing.** One price, N seats. There are no plan changes to prorate, only quantities, and quantity proration is the easy half. A surprising number of products could adopt this and have not considered it.

**Contract amendments.** In enterprise billing, a change is a signed amendment with its own effective date and a manually issued invoice. Exact, auditable, and completely unscalable — which is fine, because it applies to the twelve customers where being exact matters most.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — plan column update, change at renewal, no proration.** Coherent, and correct for longer than most teams believe.

**L1 — name the change types.** Write down the five rows of that table: for each supported change, its effective timing, its pricing, and its collection. No new arithmetic; it turns an implicit policy into a stated one, and it usually reveals that two parts of the product already disagree. *Signal:* the first argument about what should have been charged.

**L2 — changes become dated events with a change log.** The subscription's current state is derived rather than overwritten. *Signal:* the first invoice dispute you could not reconstruct.

**L3 — proration on upgrades, computed by the provider.** Two lines on the invoice, credit and charge, with your system storing the effective timestamp and the resulting lines. *Signal:* upgrade revenue you are visibly not collecting, or a free-window exploit.

**L4 — scheduled changes.** Downgrade-at-period-end becomes a future-dated change rather than a flag, and supersession has a rule. *Signal:* the second pending-state column.

**L5 — a credit balance, and batching of frequent changes.** Seat changes accumulate to the next invoice; downgrades and cancellations produce credits rather than refunds. *Signal:* enough mid-cycle payment attempts that declines on them are a measurable line.

**L6 — metered interactions.** Allowance policy on change, meters scoped to price versions, usage-price changes at boundaries only unless you have a real need. *Signal:* a customer asking what their included allowance is this month and nobody being sure.

## Implementation

Java, for one reason: `java.time` refuses to let you avoid the question this article is about. `Period` is calendar arithmetic, where "one month" has no fixed length; `Duration` is exact elapsed time. Proration is a question about elapsed time, and a language that makes you name the type makes you make the decision.

```java
public record SubscriptionChange(
    UUID subscriptionId,
    Instant effectiveAt,        // future values are how "at renewal" is expressed —
                                // one mechanism instead of a pending_plan column
    ChangeTarget target,        // new price, new quantity, or both
    ProrationPolicy proration,  // PRORATE | NONE | FULL_PERIOD
    CollectionPolicy collection,// IMMEDIATELY | NEXT_INVOICE | CREDIT_BALANCE
    String requestedBy,
    Instant recordedAt
) {}
```

Three separate fields because they are three separate decisions. Collapsing them into a `ChangeKind` enum works right up until someone needs an upgrade collected on the next invoice, and then the enum grows a suffix.

```java
List<InvoiceLine> prorate(SubscriptionItem before, SubscriptionItem after,
                          BillingPeriod period, Instant effectiveAt) {

    if (before.isMetered() || after.isMetered()) {
        // Usage was priced when it occurred. There is no partial period to
        // divide, and pretending otherwise double-counts or silently discounts.
        throw new IllegalArgumentException("metered items are never prorated");
    }

    // Duration, not Period. Period would make February price differently from
    // March for the same elapsed time, and would drag the server's calendar
    // (and therefore its timezone) into a money calculation.
    Duration whole     = Duration.between(period.start(), period.end());
    Duration remaining = Duration.between(effectiveAt, period.end());

    BigDecimal ratio = BigDecimal.valueOf(remaining.toSeconds())
        .divide(BigDecimal.valueOf(whole.toSeconds()), 12, RoundingMode.HALF_UP);

    // Two lines, never one net figure. The customer can check each half against
    // a price they recognise; a single number is unverifiable — by them now, and
    // by you in four months when they ask.
    Money credit = before.periodAmount().multipliedBy(ratio, RoundingMode.HALF_UP);
    Money charge = after.periodAmount().multipliedBy(ratio, RoundingMode.HALF_UP);

    return List.of(
        InvoiceLine.credit("Unused time on " + before.priceId(), credit, effectiveAt, period.end()),
        InvoiceLine.charge("Remaining time on " + after.priceId(), charge, effectiveAt, period.end())
    );
}
```

The ratio carries twelve digits and the money carries two. That is the 15.1 rounding rule applied here: the intermediate value keeps its precision, and rounding happens exactly once, at the line that a human will read.

Scheduling, with the rule that makes it safe:

```java
void schedule(SubscriptionChange change) {
    // A later request supersedes pending ones. Without this, a downgrade
    // scheduled for the 1st silently reverses an upgrade made on the 20th,
    // weeks after anyone remembers scheduling it — and the customer is billed
    // for a plan they thought they had left behind.
    changes.cancelPendingFrom(change.subscriptionId(), change.effectiveAt());
    changes.insert(change);
    audit.record(change);
}
```

`cancelPendingFrom` rather than `cancelAllPending` is deliberate: a change scheduled for next March should not be wiped out by one taking effect tomorrow. Which of those two you want is a policy question, and the method name is where you answer it.

## Further Reading

- **Stripe's documentation on proration, `proration_behavior`, billing cycle anchors, and subscription schedules.** The clearest public specification of the three-dimensional decision space, including the cases where their defaults will surprise you.
- **Stripe's and Chargebee's documentation on credit notes versus refunds**, which is the bridge into 15.3b.
- **The `java.time` design rationale (JSR-310 / ThreeTen).** Stephen Colebourne's writing on why `Period` and `Duration` are different types is the best short argument that calendar time and elapsed time are not the same quantity.
- **"Falsehoods programmers believe about time"**, and the IANA time zone database release notes for a year of your choosing. Both are funnier and more useful than they sound when the output is an invoice.
- **Martin Fowler's temporal patterns** — Effectivity, Temporal Property, Snapshot, and Audit Log. Written long before SaaS billing and still the most precise treatment of "this value was different last Tuesday and I need both answers."
- **An ASC 606 overview on contract modifications.** The accounting distinguishes a modification that creates a new contract from one that continues the existing one, which maps directly onto the reset-the-cycle question and explains why finance cares about it.

A **TypeScript sibling** would be worth writing, and would reveal the inverse of what Java shows here. JavaScript has no calendar type at all — `Date` is a millisecond instant with a timezone-dependent formatter bolted on — so the `Period`-versus-`Duration` decision that Java forces is one you must remember to make, and most codebases make it accidentally by reaching for `date-fns`. The new `Temporal` API reintroduces the distinction explicitly, with separate `Duration` and calendar-aware `ZonedDateTime` arithmetic, and a sibling written against it would show a language mid-migration between the two worlds — which is a genuinely different lesson from Java's "the type system already decided for you." Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying past billing: **any value that is true now and was different yesterday wants to be an event with an effective date, not a column.** The column can exist — as a cache, as a projection, as the thing the hot path reads — but it must be derivable, because sooner or later someone will need the value *as of* a timestamp rather than the value now. Subscriptions are the loudest instance because the wrong answer is a wrong invoice, and the same shape is underneath tax rates, permissions, org charts, contract terms, and every configuration that has ever had a "wait, when did that change?" attached to it.
