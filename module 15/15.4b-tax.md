# Tax

_Module 15.4b — Subscription & Billing Management_

## The Problem

You sell a $49/month product. For the first year every customer is in your own country, tax is one rate, and the line on the invoice never changes. Then the customers stop being in your country.

**The first EU customer.** You charge $49. The place-of-supply rules for digital services say the supply happened where *they* are, which means you owed German VAT on that sale — and for a non-resident seller of digital services there is usually no threshold to cross first. The obligation started at sale number one. The customer has long since been charged, so the VAT comes out of the $49 you already booked as revenue, along with interest.

**The B2B customer who emails about it.** They have a VAT number; the supply should have been reverse-charged, meaning you charge nothing and their invoice says so in those words. Fixing it means a credit note, a corrected invoice, and a VAT number you should have validated at signup. Had you accepted an invalid number, the liability would have been yours — the customer's assertion is not a defence.

**US sales tax, where "SaaS isn't taxable" is half true.** It depends on the state, and in several states it depends on whether your product is classified as software, a service, or data processing. The rate depends on the customer's street address, because state, county, city, and special-district rates stack, and there are more than ten thousand taxing jurisdictions. Meanwhile you crossed economic nexus thresholds in three states eight months ago, because nobody was counting sales per state.

**A rate change effective the first of next month.** Your annual subscriptions were billed in advance. Which rate applies? The one in force at the *time of supply* — a defined concept, defined differently per jurisdiction, that nobody on the team has heard of.

**Rounding.** You compute tax on the invoice total; the customer's ERP computes it per line; the two differ by four cents and their system rejects the invoice.

**A refund.** You return $49. Does the tax come back? Yes — and you have already remitted it, so this is a credit note and an adjustment on a future return, not a negative charge.

**And then filing.** Suppose every calculation above was right. You now owe periodic returns in every jurisdiction where you are registered, each with its own format, frequency, and deadline, forever, including the quarters where you sold nothing.

The thread: **tax is not a multiplication.** It is four questions — where did the supply happen, do we have an obligation there, is this thing taxable to this customer, and at what rate as of what date — followed by a reporting duty that outlives most of the code in your repository.

And the conclusion is unusual for this collection, so it goes first: **you should almost certainly not build this.** The rest of the article is about what buying it does not cover, because the inputs are yours and no vendor can supply them.

## What It Is

**Indirect tax** is tax on a sale, collected by the seller from the buyer and remitted to a government. The money is never yours; you are a collection agent holding it briefly.

Two families behave differently enough to be worth separating:

**VAT / GST** — the EU, UK, and most of the world. Applied at every stage of a supply chain, with businesses reclaiming the tax they paid on inputs, so the economic burden lands on the final consumer. For cross-border digital services to consumers, the place of supply is the customer's location, which is why a German consumer means German VAT regardless of where you are.

**US sales tax** — imposed once, at retail, by states and thousands of local jurisdictions, with no reclaim mechanism. A business buying your software generally pays it as a real cost unless it holds an exemption. There is no federal sales tax, which is why "the US rate" does not exist.

The vocabulary that makes the rest legible:

- **Place of supply** — whose rules apply to this sale.
- **Nexus / registration threshold** — whether you are obliged to register in that jurisdiction at all.
- **Taxability** — whether your particular product is taxed there. SaaS is not uniformly taxable across US states.
- **Reverse charge** — for cross-border B2B in VAT systems, the buyer self-accounts for the tax; you charge zero and say so on the invoice.
- **Tax point / time of supply** — the date that fixes which rate applies.
- **Exemption certificate** — documentary proof that a specific customer is not taxed, which you must hold and be able to produce.
- **Remittance and filing** — the periodic returns.

Two disambiguations.

**Tax calculation is not tax compliance.** The calculation is a small fraction of the work. Registration, evidence, filing, remittance, and audit response are the rest, and no API call performs them.

**Withholding tax is a different animal.** In some countries a business customer deducts income tax from your invoice and remits it on your behalf, so you receive less than the invoice says. It is not indirect tax, it has nothing to do with this article's machinery, and it will arrive one day as a mysterious short payment. Recognise it and hand it to finance.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Tax is a constant in the code, or a column someone sets by hand.
- You have customers abroad and charge everyone the same thing, or nothing.
- There is no tax ID field at checkout, or there is one and it has never been validated.
- Your only evidence of a customer's location is a country they picked from a dropdown.
- Nobody is counting sales per US state.
- Invoices show a total with no tax breakdown, omit one party's tax number, or fail to state reverse charge when it applies.
- Refunds do not reverse tax.
- Tax on a reprinted invoice is recomputed from today's rates.
- Your pricing page is ambiguous about whether tax is included.
- No one can list the jurisdictions you are registered in.
- Filing is a quarterly spreadsheet built from a CSV export.
- Changing a rate requires a deploy.

## How It Works

### The four questions, in order

**1. Where did the supply happen?** For digital services to consumers, generally the customer's location. For cross-border B2B in VAT systems, generally the customer's location with the reverse charge moving who accounts for it. In the US, generally the destination address.

**2. Do we have an obligation there?** Registration follows from physical presence, from economic nexus thresholds measured in sales or transactions (the US model, and thresholds vary by state and change), or — for non-resident sellers of digital services into many VAT/GST regimes — from the first sale, with no threshold at all.

**3. Is it taxable, and is this customer special?** Product taxability varies by jurisdiction, which is why every line needs a **tax code** classifying what it is. Customers vary too: resellers, nonprofits, and government bodies may be exempt, and an exemption requires a certificate you hold and can produce.

**4. What rate, as of when?** The tax point, which is why 15.4a insists the invoice snapshots the rate applied rather than a reference to a rate table that will move.

### Evidence of location

VAT regimes for digital services generally require non-contradictory evidence of where the customer is — typically two independent items from billing address, IP geolocation, payment instrument country, and phone country code. A self-declared country is not evidence.

The engineering consequence is the important one: **capture the evidence at the time of sale and store it on the transaction.** You cannot reconstruct an IP address four years later when an auditor asks why you charged Irish VAT, and "the customer told us" is not an answer that survives.

### Tax IDs, and validating them

Collect the customer's VAT or GST number, validate it against the official service — VIES for EU numbers, the equivalent elsewhere — and store the result with a timestamp. That stored result is your defence if the number later turns out to be bad.

Validation services are unreliable often enough that the outage policy is a real design decision: block the sale, or accept optimistically and re-validate on a schedule. Most systems accept and re-check, recording the attempt and its outcome, because blocking checkout on a government service's availability is its own kind of failure. Either way, re-validate periodically — numbers get deregistered.

### Inclusive or exclusive pricing

A $49 price either includes tax or excludes it, and the choice is a revenue decision, not a display decision:

```
tax-EXCLUSIVE  $49.00 → customer in Germany pays $58.31, you keep $49.00
               $49.00 → customer in Texas   pays $53.04, you keep $49.00

tax-INCLUSIVE  $49.00 → customer in Germany pays $49.00, you keep $41.18
               $49.00 → customer in Hungary pays $49.00, you keep $38.58
```

Exclusive pricing holds your revenue constant and makes the customer's total vary. Inclusive pricing holds the customer's total constant and makes your revenue vary by geography — which quietly means your MRR per customer is now a function of where they live, something every downstream report needs to know. Consumer markets generally expect (and consumer protection law frequently requires) inclusive display; B2B and US markets expect exclusive.

### Where tax attaches, and rounding

Tax is computed **per line**, because lines can carry different tax codes and therefore different rates, and then summed. Jurisdictions differ on whether rounding happens per line or per invoice; computing and rounding per line, then summing, is defensible nearly everywhere and matches what most customer ERP systems do — which is what stops the four-cent rejection.

Store tax per line on the finalized invoice along with the rate and the jurisdiction. A total is not enough: a partial credit note has to reverse tax line by line, and an auditor asks which rule produced which number.

### Rates change, so the rate table is effective-dated

This is 15.3a's habit applied to someone else's data. Rates change, jurisdictions are added, and reclassifications happen. A historical invoice must be computable at the rate in force at its tax point, which means the rate table is append-only and versioned by effective date — and it means recomputing an old invoice from the current table is not a fix, it is corruption.

### The part that is not code

Registration is a form and a relationship, per jurisdiction. The EU's One Stop Shop lets a seller register once and remit across member states for cross-border B2C supplies rather than registering in each, and equivalents exist elsewhere; they reduce the number of registrations, not the obligation.

Filing is periodic, per registration, forever. Penalties attach to lateness independently of whether you owed anything.

And the ongoing task your system genuinely must perform even when a vendor does the rest: **threshold monitoring.** Count sales and transactions per jurisdiction continuously, and alert as you approach a registration threshold rather than after crossing it. Most tax vendors offer this; the point is that it has to be somebody's job, because the obligation attaches silently.

### What stays yours no matter what you buy

- Customer location, with stored evidence.
- Customer type — B2C or B2B — and a validated tax ID.
- A tax code per product or SKU, chosen deliberately.
- Immutable per-line tax on the finalized invoice.
- Reversal of tax on refunds and credit notes.
- A complete, queryable transaction record that can support a return or an audit.

A tax engine consumes all six. It cannot produce any of them.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Engage a tax engine** as soon as you sell across a border, or into the US at any meaningful scale. The break-even is very low, because the alternative's failure mode is retroactive liability.
- **Register before the first sale** in digital-services regimes that have no threshold for non-residents; register in US states as thresholds approach, not after.
- **Tax-inclusive pricing** for consumer markets, exclusive for B2B — and state which on the pricing page, in the checkout, and on the invoice.
- **Reverse-charge handling** the moment you have one EU business customer, because the invoice wording and the zero rate are both required, not optional.
- **A merchant of record** (15.2a) when you are a small team selling to consumers globally. This is the scenario where an MoR's higher rate is straightforwardly worth it: you are buying an entire compliance function.
- **Building any of this yourself** — realistically only when you operate in a single jurisdiction with a single rate and no cross-border sales.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Maintaining your own rate table.** Something changes somewhere most weeks. A table you maintain is not an asset, it is a liability with a version number.
- **Integrating a tax engine** when you sell in one country, to consumers, at one rate, with no foreign customers. That day ends, but it need not end early.
- **Tax logic in application code.** It belongs behind a call whose result you snapshot. `if (country === "DE")` in a service is a bug with a deployment schedule.
- **Registering preemptively "to be safe."** Every registration is a permanent filing obligation, including for periods with zero sales, and unwinding one is harder than creating it.
- **Exemption-certificate management** before you have exempt customers.
- **Automated filing** before you have more than a couple of registrations; an accountant is cheaper and better at it.

## Trade-offs

- **The tax engine sits on your checkout path.** Latency, availability, and a documented fallback policy are now yours, for a dependency whose wrong answer is a legal problem rather than a UX one.
- **Per-transaction cost**, on every invoice, forever.
- **Tax-inclusive pricing makes net revenue vary by geography**, which complicates MRR, cohort analysis, and every per-customer margin calculation.
- **Registration creates permanent obligations** with fixed costs per jurisdiction per period, regardless of sales.
- **Historical correctness constrains your data model.** Effective-dated rates, snapshotted tax, immutable invoices — you cannot fix the past by fixing the table, which is the instinct everyone has first.
- **Errors are retroactive and one-directional.** Uncollected tax comes out of your revenue, with interest, and you generally cannot go back and bill the customer for it. Over-collected tax has to be refunded, and you may still owe it.
- **You are now storing addresses and IP evidence for years**, which is retention you must justify against deletion requests (5.3a) and protect like any other personal data.
- **Every new market is a project**, not a configuration change.

## Alternatives

**A merchant of record.** Paddle, Lemon Squeezy, FastSpring, and similar become the legal seller, which moves registration, collection, filing, and audit exposure to them entirely. The cleanest complete answer available, priced accordingly, and it costs you some of the customer relationship (15.2a).

**A tax engine.** Stripe Tax, Avalara, Vertex, TaxJar, Anrok, Quaderno, Fonoa. They calculate, monitor thresholds, and several will file. You remain the seller and the registrant; they remain a supplier. Read their "your responsibilities" documentation before their feature list — it is an accurate description of the work that stays with you.

**An accountant and a spreadsheet.** Genuinely viable with one or two registrations and low volume, and it is what most companies actually do for their first two years. It fails at precisely the point where growth makes failure expensive, which is a bad property, so watch the threshold rather than the workload.

**Sell in fewer places.** Under-considered and entirely legitimate. Declining to sell into a jurisdiction is cheaper than registering in it, and some companies geoblock deliberately until a market is worth the compliance.

**Sell through a marketplace or app store.** In many regimes the platform is the deemed supplier and handles tax on the sale, which is one of the less-discussed things the platform fee buys.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — one jurisdiction, one rate.** Correct while true.

**L1 — capture the inputs, even unused.** Full address, IP at signup, B2B/B2C flag, tax ID field, and a tax code per product. Evidence cannot be backfilled, which makes this the one step with a deadline. *Signal:* your first customer with a foreign billing address.

**L2 — a tax engine on the invoice path.** Per-line tax, snapshotted at finalization (15.4a). *Signal:* selling across any border, or into the US.

**L3 — tax ID validation and reverse charge.** Validation with stored results, zero-rating, and the required wording on the invoice. *Signal:* the first B2B customer who asks why you charged them VAT.

**L4 — threshold monitoring and a registration process.** Counts per jurisdiction, alerts before thresholds, and a named owner for registrations. *Signal:* any US sales at all, or approaching a foreign market seriously.

**L5 — exemptions and classification.** Certificate storage with expiry, deliberate tax codes per SKU rather than one inherited default. *Signal:* the first customer claiming exemption, or a product line that is taxed differently.

**L6 — filing automation, or the decision to move to an MoR.** *Signal:* the number of registrations passing what a person can comfortably track.

## Implementation

This is the shortest Implementation section in the module, and deliberately so. If you find yourself writing tax *logic*, something has gone wrong. What you write is the request, the snapshot, and the failure policy.

TypeScript, for the request object — because the request is the actual deliverable, and every field in it is something only you can know:

```ts
type TaxContext = {
  sellerEntity: string;              // which of your legal entities is selling
  customerType: "b2c" | "b2b";
  customerTaxId?: {
    value: string;
    validatedAt: Date;
    result: "valid" | "invalid" | "unavailable";  // "unavailable" is a real outcome,
  };                                              // and storing it is your defence

  address: PostalAddress;            // street level: US rates stack by district
  evidence: LocationEvidence[];      // billing address, IP country, card BIN, phone —
                                     // captured now, because it cannot be reconstructed

  lines: Array<{
    sku: string;
    taxCode: string;                 // the classification decision. A correct engine
    amountMinor: number;             // returns a wrong answer from a wrong tax code,
  }>;                                // and this is the most common way that happens.

  taxPoint: Date;                    // the date that fixes the rate. NEVER `new Date()`
};                                   // on a reprint of a two-year-old invoice.
```

The call, with the part people skip:

```ts
async function taxFor(ctx: TaxContext): Promise<TaxResult> {
  try {
    return await engine.calculate(ctx);
  } catch (err) {
    // The engine is on the checkout path, so "it was down" needs a policy rather
    // than a stack trace. Estimating is defensible where a single national rate
    // applies to everything, and indefensible under US destination sourcing where
    // the rate depends on the street.
    const single = cachedRates.singleNationalRate(ctx.address.country, ctx.taxPoint);
    if (!single) throw err;                    // fail the sale rather than invent a number
    return { ...single.applyTo(ctx), provisional: true };  // flagged, reconciled before filing
  }
}
```

`provisional: true` is the whole point of the fallback. An estimated tax figure that is indistinguishable from a computed one will be filed, and then it is not an estimate any more.

Then persist per line — rate, jurisdiction, taxable amount, tax amount, the engine's own transaction identifier — onto the finalized invoice, never as a total, and never as a reference to a table that will move.

## Further Reading

- **OECD International VAT/GST Guidelines.** The conceptual source most national digital-services rules descend from, and the best single explanation of *why* place of supply is the customer's location. Read this before any national guidance.
- **The European Commission's explanatory notes on the place of supply of telecommunications, broadcasting and electronic services**, and its One Stop Shop guidance. Specific, official, and clearer than most vendor summaries.
- **The VIES VAT number validation service** documentation, including what a non-response means and does not mean.
- **South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc. (2018).** Short, readable, and the origin of economic nexus. Worth reading the actual opinion — it explains the logic that every state threshold since has been built on.
- **The Streamlined Sales Tax Governing Board's taxability matrices.** The most accessible public evidence of how much US taxability varies by state for software and digital goods.
- **Your tax engine's "responsibilities" documentation** — Stripe Tax, Avalara, and Anrok all publish one. It is the most accurate available list of the work that remains yours after buying.
- **Your own jurisdiction's guidance on time of supply for continuous supplies**, which is the rule that decides what happens to your annual subscriptions when a rate changes.

No second-language sibling is proposed for this one, and the reason is worth stating rather than leaving as an omission: there is almost no language-specific content here. The interesting variation is jurisdictional, not syntactic, and a Java or Go version of the same request object would teach nothing the TypeScript does not. The companion that *would* add value is a different article rather than a translation — on reconciliation and filing, where transaction records are aggregated into a return, adjustments from credit notes are applied, and the numbers have to tie out against both the ledger and the payment provider. That belongs alongside 15.5.

---

The habit worth carrying past tax: **some obligations attach to facts, not to decisions.** You do not opt into a registration requirement by building a feature; you incur it by making a sale in a place, and the duty exists from that moment whether or not anyone noticed. The same shape shows up in data-residency rules that bind once a record lands in a region, in licence terms that change once a user count crosses a line, in accessibility requirements that attach at a headcount, in breach-notification clocks that start at discovery. In each case a counter nobody was watching crossed a line nobody had encoded. The engineering response is always the same: find the counters that create obligations and instrument them as carefully as the ones that create revenue.
