# Cancellation, Refunds & Credits

_Module 15.3b — Subscription & Billing Management_

## The Problem

A customer presses Cancel. What happens?

**The first implementation answers everything at once.** The subscription is cancelled at the provider, the tenant is torn down, access ends. The customer pressed the button on day 3 of a month they had already paid for. They have lost twenty-seven days they own and all of their data, and the first anyone hears about it is a support ticket that now requires a refund nobody has built a path for.

**The second implementation cancels at period end**, which is correct and introduces a subtler failure. The subscription sits in `active` with a `cancel_at_period_end` flag, and every downstream system reads `active`. So the customer who cancelled on Monday receives an upsell email on Wednesday, a renewal reminder on Friday, and a call from an account executive about expansion the following week. Each of those is a small insult, and collectively they are the reason the customer tells other people about the experience.

**Then someone asks for a refund on an annual plan.** Two months into $588. There is no written policy, so the answer is decided by whoever picks up the ticket, and it is different from the answer the last person gave. Prorated works out to $490 — returned to a card charged sixty days ago, minus a processing fee you do not get back, against revenue that was already reported in a quarter that already closed.

**The credit balance nobody designed.** 15.3a's downgrades produce credits, so a `credit_cents` column appears on the customer. Within a year the column is asked six questions it cannot answer: does it expire, can it be withdrawn as cash, what happens to it on cancellation, does a promotional credit differ from a proration credit, which credit is consumed first, and — the one support asks weekly — *why is this number $37?*

**Refunds issued in the provider's dashboard.** Support has access, which is correct and necessary, and the refund happens entirely outside your database. Your invoice still says paid. Your MRR is unchanged. The quarterly revenue number is wrong by the sum of everything support did that quarter.

**And nothing reverses.** The original payment triggered a partner commission, an affiliate payout, a grant of bonus credits, an outbound webhook to the customer's own system, and a line in a revenue report. The refund triggers none of their inverses, because each of those consumers subscribed to "paid" and nobody wrote the other half.

**Four months later the customer comes back.** New account, new customer record, no history, and the metrics count them as a new logo. The data they asked about at cancellation is gone, because the retention window was never defined and the teardown ran on the billing clock.

The thread: **cancellation looks like one event and is at least four.** Money, access, data, and the relationship all end — on four different schedules, with four different owners.

```
             cancel requested
                    │
 billing clock ─────┤  no further renewals; the paid period stands
 access clock  ─────┤  ends at period end, usually plus an export window
 data clock    ─────┤  retention window, then deletion — the tenant lifecycle's clock
 relationship  ─────┘  does not end: receipts, exports, win-back, reactivation
```

Collapsing those four into one boolean is the single decision that produces every failure above.

## What It Is

**Cancellation** is the termination of the recurring agreement. It is not deletion, not deactivation, and not the end of the customer record — 5.3a separates those three verbs, and everything it says there applies here.

The vocabulary for money going backwards is worth getting right, because three different things are routinely called "a refund":

- **A refund** returns money to the original payment instrument. It reverses a payment.
- **A credit note** (credit memo) is a document that reduces what was owed on an already-issued invoice. Money may or may not move. It exists because in most tax jurisdictions an issued invoice is immutable — sequentially numbered, legally fixed — and the only lawful way to change one is to issue a second document that references it.
- **A chargeback** is a reversal you did not authorise, initiated by the customer's bank (15.2a).

All three reduce revenue. One is a decision, one is a document, and one is something that happens to you.

And **a credit balance** is a customer-level amount applied to future invoices — a liability, not a payment, and the thing 15.3a kept deferring to.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The cancel button revokes access to a period the customer has already paid for.
- Or there is no cancel button, and cancelling requires contacting support.
- `cancel_at_period_end` is set, and upsell email, renewal reminders, and sales outreach keep running.
- Refunds are issued in the provider dashboard and your database never finds out.
- Your refund policy is whatever the last person decided.
- Credits are a column. Nobody can explain how a given balance was reached.
- Cancellation triggers data deletion, on the billing clock, with no notice period.
- A returning customer is a new record and counts as new-logo revenue.
- Nothing reverses on refund: commissions, granted credits, outbound webhooks, revenue entries.
- Invoices are edited after issue.
- One churn number covers voluntary cancellations, failed payments, expired trials, and downgrades to free.
- You cannot tell which cancellations were preceded by a declined charge.

## How It Works

### Four clocks, set separately

**Billing.** The default is cancel at period end: no further renewals, the current period stands. Immediate cancellation is a distinct and rarer operation, and it is distinct precisely because it forces a refund-or-credit decision that period-end cancellation does not.

**Access.** Usually ends with billing. A short read-only window afterwards is cheap, generates goodwill out of proportion to its cost, and covers data-portability obligations you may have anyway. The `READ_ONLY` state from 5.3a is the mechanism; if it exists for non-payment it already exists for this.

**Data.** A retention window, then deletion, running on the tenant lifecycle with its own notice. Two things override it in opposite directions: an erasure request can shorten it, and tax law lengthens it for specific records — invoices in particular must usually survive the customer by years. That is 5.3a's retention classes; the billing system's only job is to not be the thing that decides.

**Relationship.** Does not end. They need their invoices after they leave, often as a legal matter. They may come back. The customer record outlives the subscription, and a return is a reactivation of an existing relationship rather than an acquisition.

### Cancellation is a state, and it is reversible

Model `cancel_at` (scheduled) separately from `canceled_at` (happened). Between those two timestamps the subscription is in a pending-cancellation state that downstream systems must be able to see — and the reliable way to make sure they do is to stop exposing raw status. If every consumer computes `isEnding` from a single helper, the upsell-email bug is fixed once instead of in eleven places.

Until the effective date, cancellation is reversible, and a one-click undo recovers a genuine fraction of people who cancelled on a bad afternoon. It costs almost nothing to build.

### Refunds: policy first, mechanics second

The policy matters more than its generosity. An unwritten policy means the loudest customer gets the best outcome, your support team absorbs the stress of deciding, and you cannot predict the cost of any of it. Common shapes that work: no refunds on monthly plans because a month is short and they had it; a stated money-back window on annual plans; prorated refunds for annual cancellations at your discretion, with the discretion documented.

Then the mechanics, which have sharper edges than people expect.

**Refund the original charge where you can.** Providers generally allow refunds against a charge for a bounded window — commonly a few months — after which returning money requires a bank transfer and a manual process.

**Processing fees are usually not returned.** A refunded $588 costs you the fee on the way in and often again if you re-acquire the customer, which is part of why credit is frequently the better offer.

**Offer credit, honour refunds.** Credit keeps the money, avoids the fee loss, and keeps the relationship; a customer who wanted cash and got a store credit tells people about it. Offer first, concede immediately.

**Partial refunds carry tax with them.** Refunding a line refunds its tax, and in jurisdictions with immutable invoices that requires a credit note rather than an edit. This is why billing providers have a credit-note object and why "update the invoice row" is not a legal option in most of Europe and Latin America.

**Build the reversal cascade.** Whatever consumed the payment event needs the inverse. The clean framing is that `payment.succeeded` and `payment.reversed` are a pair, every consumer subscribes to both, and a consumer that cannot implement the second one is a consumer that should not have implemented the first.

### The credit balance is a ledger, not a column

A balance must be explainable, and a single number cannot explain itself. Append-only entries can:

```
  +  $29.40  PRORATION   inv_881 downgrade credit      refundable    no expiry
  +  $50.00  GOODWILL    ticket #4412 outage           refundable    no expiry
  +  $25.00  PROMOTION   referral bonus                NON-refundable expires 2026-12-31
  -  $67.40  APPLIED     → inv_903
  ─────────
     $37.00  balance
```

The properties that force this shape: credits differ in **refundability** (a promotional credit must never become cash), in **expiry**, in **currency** (a EUR credit is not USD money), and sometimes in **scope** (usable only against certain products). Application order is therefore policy, not convenience — soonest-to-expire first, non-refundable before refundable, so the customer keeps the cashable credit longest and the credit you would have written off gets used.

And one rule that gets written down too late: **what happens to a balance on cancellation.** Typically non-refundable credits are forfeited and cash-origin credits are refundable, but the only genuinely wrong answer is not having one, because the customer will ask on their way out and the answer will be improvised.

### Retention at the moment of cancel, honestly

The cancel flow is a product surface and deserves design, within one hard constraint: **cancelling must be as easy as subscribing.** That is a consumer-protection requirement in a growing number of jurisdictions, and it is also self-interested — the alternative to an easy cancel button is a chargeback, which costs a fee, counts against the network monitoring thresholds from 15.2a, and cannot be won.

Inside that constraint, the flow does real work:

- **Ask why, with structured options.** This is the highest-quality churn data you will ever collect, and it is free.
- **Match the offer to the reason.** "Too expensive" and "missing a feature" and "project ended" want three different responses, and a blanket discount answers none of them well.
- **Offer pause.** It is the underrated option: the data stays, the relationship stays, the integrations stay, and a cancellation becomes a deferred decision. For anything seasonal or project-shaped, pause outperforms every discount you could offer.
- **Target save offers using a signal.** A discount accepted by someone who was not going to leave is pure margin given away, and customers talk to each other about the discount they got by threatening to cancel.

### Measuring it without lying to yourself

The billing system's contribution to churn metrics is one thing: keep the categories separate. Voluntary cancellation, involuntary churn (15.2b), trial non-conversion, and downgrade-to-free are four different problems with four different fixes, and a single churn number hides which one is moving. Record the reason, record whether a failed payment preceded the cancellation, and record reactivations as distinct from new logos. What to do with those numbers belongs to Module 16.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Cancel at period end** — the correct default for monthly self-serve. The customer paid for the period and gets the period; no refund decision is needed.
- **Immediate cancellation with a prorated credit** — when your marginal cost per customer is significant, when the customer is consolidating onto another account, or when continued access is a liability rather than a courtesy.
- **A stated money-back window** — when acquisition friction is your bottleneck. A visible guarantee converts more than it costs in nearly every product where the trial cannot demonstrate the value.
- **Credit-first responses** — once a credit ledger exists and the relationship is worth more than the cash.
- **Pause** — seasonal customers, agencies between clients, products with natural dormancy. It is a real feature with real state, and where it fits it is the highest-yield retention mechanism in this article.
- **A full ledger** — as soon as credits arrive from more than one source, which happens the moment proration credits meet goodwill credits.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A credit ledger when goodwill is your only credit source.** Your provider's customer balance handles it, and moving to your own ledger later is a migration you can afford.
- **A multi-offer retention flow at forty customers.** Call them. You will learn more in three conversations than the flow would tell you in a year.
- **Approval workflows for refunds** before the volume makes ad-hoc decisions expensive.
- **Building pause because it sounds good.** Pause reaches further than expected — seats, integrations, scheduled jobs, webhooks, data retention, and what the invoice looks like on resume — and it is worth building only when someone has asked for it.
- **Immediate cancellation support** when everyone is monthly and period-end is fine.
- **Win-back campaigns before fixing the reason for leaving.** They perform exactly as well as the product change you did not make.

## Trade-offs

- **Easy cancellation raises measured churn and lowers chargebacks, support cost, and regulatory exposure.** The trade is almost always worth making and the graph still looks worse for a quarter.
- **Credits are a liability, not revenue.** Issuing them defers income and puts an obligation on the balance sheet, which finance will want to talk about (15.5).
- **Refund policy has a cost in both directions.** Generous policies are exploited at some rate; strict ones are disputed at some rate. You are choosing which cost to carry, not whether to carry one.
- **The reversal cascade is work proportional to how many systems consumed "paid"**, and it grows every time someone adds a new consumer without adding its inverse.
- **Retention offers erode price integrity.** A discount given at the cancel screen becomes the price that customer expects forever, and eventually the price their peers ask for.
- **Retained data after cancellation is breach surface and regulatory exposure** for revenue you are no longer receiving.
- **Pending cancellation is another state every consumer must handle**, and the ones that forget fail silently and embarrassingly rather than loudly.

## Alternatives

**Let the provider own it.** Provider-managed cancellation plus their customer balance covers the majority of what this article describes, and it is the right starting point. The constraint appears when your access policy and their subscription states need to agree about a moment neither fully models.

**Non-renewing terms.** In enterprise billing the subscription simply expires unless renewed, so there is no cancellation flow at all — there is a renewal motion instead. It removes this entire surface and replaces it with an active sales process, which is a trade worth understanding before assuming self-serve norms apply.

**Downgrade to free instead of cancelling.** Where a viable free tier exists this outperforms cancellation almost every time: the account survives, the data survives, the relationship survives, and reactivation is a click rather than a re-acquisition. The cost is serving people who pay nothing, which you were going to lose entirely anyway.

**No self-serve cancellation.** Listed for completeness and to be argued against: it converts cancellations into chargebacks, generates support load, damages reputation in public, and is increasingly unlawful.

**Refund-by-default as a market position.** A no-questions guarantee is a marketing asset with a measurable cost. It works when the product delivers value quickly and the abuse rate is low, and both of those are things you can measure before committing.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — cancel deletes.** Fix this before anything else in this article.

**L1 — cancel at period end, with undo.** `cancel_at` as a timestamp, access and billing on the same clock, data moved onto the tenant lifecycle's clock. *Signal:* you have paying customers.

**L2 — downstream reads pending cancellation.** One derived `isEnding` helper; upsell, renewal reminders, and sales outreach suppressed; an export path that works after access ends. *Signal:* the first upsell email sent to someone who cancelled.

**L3 — a written refund policy and an in-product refund path.** Refunds recorded in your database because they were issued through your system, not the provider's dashboard, plus the inverse handlers for the two or three most important consumers of "paid." *Signal:* the first month-end where support's refunds and your revenue number disagreed.

**L4 — the credit ledger.** Append-only entries with reason, source, expiry, refundability, and a documented application order. *Signal:* the second source of credits.

**L5 — the cancel flow as a product.** Structured reasons, targeted save offers, pause. *Signal:* enough monthly cancellations that a percentage point of saves is worth a sprint.

**L6 — reactivation and cohort reporting.** Returning customers resume rather than re-register; churn is split by category. *Signal:* somebody presents a churn number and three people in the room define it differently.

## Implementation

Go, because the interesting failure here is concurrency around a balance, and Go's ordinary shape — a goroutine per invoice, finalising in parallel — walks straight into it.

```go
// Append-only. The balance is a fold over entries and never a column that gets
// overwritten: "why is my balance $37" has to be answerable, and $37 cannot
// answer it.
type CreditEntry struct {
	ID          string
	CustomerID  string
	AmountMinor int64      // positive: granted. negative: consumed.
	Currency    string     // credits are currency-scoped — a EUR credit is not USD money
	Reason      Reason     // PRORATION | GOODWILL | PROMOTION | APPLIED
	Refundable  bool       // a promotional credit must never become cash
	ExpiresAt   *time.Time
	SourceRef   string     // the change, invoice, or ticket that caused it
	AppliedTo   string     // invoice id, on consumption entries
	CreatedAt   time.Time
}
```

Applying it is where the bug lives:

```go
func (l *Ledger) ApplyTo(ctx context.Context, tx *sql.Tx, inv Invoice) (int64, error) {
	// Serialise per customer. Two invoices finalising at the same instant will
	// otherwise each read the same balance and each spend all of it — a plain
	// double-spend, and entirely plausible when a renewal and a plan change
	// land in the same second.
	if err := lockCustomer(ctx, tx, inv.CustomerID); err != nil {
		return 0, err
	}

	open, err := l.openEntries(ctx, tx, inv.CustomerID, inv.Currency, inv.Date)
	if err != nil {
		return 0, err
	}

	// Order is policy, not convenience: soonest-to-expire first, then
	// non-refundable before refundable. The customer keeps the cashable credit
	// longest, and the credit you would have written off gets used.
	sort.SliceStable(open, func(i, j int) bool {
		if expiryOf(open[i]) != expiryOf(open[j]) {
			return expiryOf(open[i]).Before(expiryOf(open[j]))
		}
		return !open[i].Refundable && open[j].Refundable
	})

	remaining := inv.AmountDue
	for _, e := range open {
		if remaining == 0 {
			break
		}
		take := min(e.Unapplied(), remaining)
		// Consumption is an append, not an update. The granting entry is never
		// modified; the pair of rows is the audit trail.
		if err := l.append(ctx, tx, consume(e, inv.ID, take)); err != nil {
			return 0, err
		}
		remaining -= take
	}
	return inv.AmountDue - remaining, nil
}
```

The lock is the whole correctness story and it is one line, which is exactly why it goes missing. Note also that credits are applied *inside* the invoice's transaction — applying them in a separate step means a crash between the two either spends credit against an invoice that never finalised or finalises an invoice that should have been reduced.

Cancellation, with the four clocks visible:

```go
func Cancel(ctx context.Context, sub *Subscription, req CancelRequest, now time.Time) error {
	// Billing: they paid for the period, they get the period. Reversible until
	// CancelAt, which is why CanceledAt stays nil.
	sub.CancelAt = sub.CurrentPeriodEnd
	sub.CancelReason = req.Reason // structured — the best churn data you will get

	// Access: ends with billing, plus a window to get their data out.
	access.ScheduleTransition(sub.TenantID, ReadOnly, sub.CancelAt)
	access.ScheduleTransition(sub.TenantID, Suspended, sub.CancelAt.AddDate(0, 0, 30))

	// Data: belongs to the tenant lifecycle and its retention classes (5.3a).
	// Billing schedules a review; billing does not delete anything, ever.
	lifecycle.ScheduleRetentionReview(sub.TenantID, sub.CancelAt)

	// Relationship: nothing ends here. But the messaging has to know.
	comms.Suppress(sub.TenantID, "upsell", "renewal_reminder", "expansion_outreach")

	return subs.Save(ctx, sub)
}
```

The `comms.Suppress` line is the one that looks least like engineering and prevents the complaint customers actually post screenshots of.

## Further Reading

- **Stripe's documentation on credit notes, customer balance, subscription cancellation, and pause collection.** The credit-note object in particular is worth reading as an explanation of why invoices cannot be edited.
- **The EU VAT Directive's invoicing requirements (2006/112/EC, articles on invoice content and sequential numbering)**, or your own jurisdiction's equivalent. Short, dry, and the reason your invoice table needs an append-only correction mechanism rather than an `UPDATE`.
- **Consumer-protection rules on subscription cancellation** — the FTC's negative-option rulemaking in the US, California's automatic renewal law, and the EU consumer rights regime. This area has been actively litigated and amended, so treat any summary as dated and check the current position for the markets you sell into; the direction of travel has been consistent even where specific rules have not survived.
- **Martin Blais, "The Double-Entry Counting Method" (Beancount documentation).** The clearest explanation available of why balances are derived and what a ledger buys you, written by someone implementing one rather than describing one.
- **ASC 606 on refund liabilities and variable consideration.** The accounting reason a credit is a liability and a refund is not simply negative revenue (15.5).
- **Recurly's and Paddle's published work on cancellation flows and save offers.** Vendor material with an obvious interest, still the best public data on which offers recover which reasons.

A **Java sibling** would earn its place on the concurrency half specifically. The Go above takes a pessimistic lock, which is the right default and hides an alternative: JPA's optimistic locking with `@Version` turns credit double-spend into a conflict detected at commit and retried, which behaves better under low contention and worse under high, and interacts with transaction boundaries in ways that matter here — particularly when the credit application shares a transaction with an external payment call, which is the same trap 5.3a set around `@Transactional`. Pessimistic versus optimistic is a genuine fork with different failure modes, and only one side of it is visible from this article. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry past billing: **whenever your system has a boolean that means "this is over," ask what actually stops, and when.** Money, access, data, obligations, and the relationship almost never end on the same day, and the single flag forces them to. The same shape shows up in employee offboarding, in decommissioning a device, in retiring an API version where the traffic stops months after the deprecation notice, and in every account deletion anyone has ever implemented in an afternoon. Endings are built later than beginnings, by fewer people, under more pressure — which is exactly why they are where the money, the trust, and the legal exposure concentrate.
