# Subscription Models

_Module 15.1 — Subscription & Billing Management_

## The Problem

A team ships pricing on launch day. It takes about twenty minutes:

```ts
const PLANS = { free: 0, pro: 2_900, business: 9_900 } as const;
// tenants.plan: 'free' | 'pro' | 'business'
```

A column on the tenant, a constant in the code, a `checkout` call that passes the right Stripe price id. It is correct, it is readable, and it is the right decision at that moment. What follows is eighteen months of it being slowly pulled apart.

**Month 2 — the first custom deal.** A prospect will sign today at $59/month for the Business feature set. Sales says yes. An engineer creates a 40% coupon in Stripe and posts the deal terms in a channel. The `plan` column says `business`. The revenue dashboard, which multiplies plan by headcount, says the customer is worth $99. Nothing in the system knows otherwise.

**Month 5 — seats.** Business becomes $15 per user per month. The price now depends on a number that changes whenever somebody invites a colleague. Someone writes `SELECT count(*) FROM users WHERE tenant_id = $1`. Two weeks later a customer with 200 invitations that were never accepted receives an invoice for 214 seats, and the account manager receives a much shorter email.

**Month 9 — usage.** API calls above 100,000 a month cost $0.50 per thousand. This requires a number that did not previously exist, counted on a hot path, surviving retries, and matching — to the unit — what the customer sees in their dashboard. The dashboard reads a different table through a different query. In the first billing cycle the two disagree, and the customer is the one who notices.

**Month 12 — the price increase.** Pro goes from $29 to $39. Existing customers stay at $29; some because it was promised in writing, most because nobody wants to find out. There is one number in the table. The team ships `pro_legacy`. Then, inevitably, `pro_legacy_annual`.

**Month 14 — the add-on.** SSO is sold separately at $10 per user. The enum becomes a combination lock: `business`, `business_sso`, `business_sso_annual`, `pro_legacy_annual`.

**Month 18 — finance asks what MRR is.** Three systems give three answers. The dashboard counts trials as revenue. The spreadsheet books March's annual prepayment as $12,000 of March. The coupon from month 2 appears in none of them. Everyone is confident, and everyone's number is defensible, which is worse than everyone being wrong.

The thread running through all six: **the `plan` column is four separate things wearing one name.**

```
                    ┌─ what they may DO          → entitlements   (changes with product releases)
tenants.plan ───────┼─ what they PAY and how     → price          (changes with pricing decisions)
                    ├─ what they AGREED TO, when → the agreement  (frozen at signature; never changes)
                    └─ what's on the WEBSITE     → packaging      (changes with marketing)
```

On day one all four had the same value for every customer, so one column held them. Every event above is one of the four moving independently of the others — and each time, the fix is another enum value, because the schema has nowhere else to put the difference.

## What It Is

The choice being made here is a **pricing model**, and the thing the system needs in order to hold it is a **price book** (a catalogue of what may be charged) plus an **agreement** (what this specific customer is actually charged), joined by a reference rather than a copy.

Underneath the marketing names, a subscription is not complicated:

> a recurring agreement to charge, on a **cycle**, one or more **lines** — each line being a **quantity** and a **rate structure**.

Flat, packaged, per-seat, metered, hybrid, freemium, credit-based: all of them are positions on three axes. What is counted, how the count is priced, and how often. That decomposition is the whole article, because it is what turns "we need to support tiered usage-based pricing with seat minimums" from a paragraph of sales language into three separable engineering questions.

Two disambiguations, both cheap and both load-bearing.

**Packaging, pricing, and entitlements are different objects.** "Pro" is packaging. "$15 per seat per month, billed monthly in advance" is pricing. "May export to CSV, capped at 5 projects" is entitlement. They correlate, which is why teams collapse them, and they change on entirely different schedules — a product release moves entitlements, a pricing experiment moves prices, a rebrand moves packaging, and none of those should move the other two. Entitlement resolution itself is Module 5's subject; all this article asks of it is that entitlements be **derived from the agreement** rather than stored as a second copy of a plan name.

**"Tiered" means two unrelated things.** It means packaging tiers — Basic, Pro, Business — and it means rate tiers — the first 10,000 units at one price, the rest at another. A requirements document that says "tiered pricing" has not specified anything. This article says *packages* for the first and *rate tiers* for the second.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Prices are constants in the codebase, so changing one is a deploy.
- A customer's real price exists only inside your payment provider, as a coupon, and in a Slack thread.
- Your plan enum encodes combinations: `pro_annual_legacy_eu`.
- You are afraid that editing the price on the pricing page will change what existing customers are billed — or you know it will.
- `if (tenant.plan === 'pro')` appears in more than a handful of places, and adding a package means finding all of them.
- Your MRR figure disagrees with your billing provider's, and nobody can reconcile them in an afternoon.
- The seat count you bill drifts from the number of humans actually using the product, in either direction.
- Usage shown to the customer and usage charged to the customer come from different queries.
- Billing a customer on a negotiated contract requires a human to run something.
- Nobody can produce the current price list without checking three systems.
- A trial converting to paid is implemented as a migration between records rather than a state change.
- There is no way to answer "how many customers are still on the 2023 seat price?"

## How It Works

### One subscription, several lines

The shape nearly every serious billing system converges on, whether bought or built:

```
PRICE BOOK (catalogue — append-only)        AGREEMENT (per customer)
                                   
Product "Analytics"                          Subscription
  ├ Price seat_pro_v1  $19 /seat/mo           customer, currency, cycle anchor, state
  ├ Price seat_pro_v2  $15 /seat/mo  ◄──┐     ├ Item → seat_pro_v2   qty 12   (declared)
  ├ Price sso_v1       $10 /seat/mo  ◄──┼──── ├ Item → sso_v1        qty 12   (declared)
  └ Price api_calls_v3 graduated     ◄──┘     └ Item → api_calls_v3  qty ?    (measured)
```

Three rules do most of the work.

**Prices are immutable and versioned.** You never edit `seat_pro_v1`; you publish `seat_pro_v2` and mark v1 unsellable. Grandfathering then falls out for free — an old customer is not a special case, just a subscription still pointing at an older row. The question "how many customers are on the old price" becomes a `GROUP BY`.

**The agreement references the price; it never copies the amount.** The copying instinct is right about the problem (history must be preserved) and wrong about the fix: copying the amount gives you a second place prices live, and it destroys the ability to reason about the catalogue as a population.

**Quantity is either declared or measured**, and that distinction is the next axis.

### Axis 1 — what you count

**Nothing.** Flat price per cycle, quantity fixed at one. Costs you nothing to operate. This is a real option and it stays a real option far longer than most teams believe.

**A declared quantity** — seats, projects, environments, connected accounts. The customer or an admin sets it, and it changes by an explicit action you can observe.

Seats look simple and contain one genuinely hard product decision: *what is a seat?* Three answers, all defensible, all shipping different systems.

- **Licensed** — the customer buys N seats and assigns them. Easiest to bill, most predictable for the buyer, and creates the seat-sharing problem: one login, four humans.
- **Active in period** — anyone who did something this cycle. Fairest, best-aligned with value, and requires a definition of "active" precise enough to defend in an invoice dispute. It also makes the customer's bill unpredictable, which procurement departments hate.
- **Invited / provisioned** — count the rows. Trivially countable and quietly punitive: it bills the customer for onboarding, including the twelve people who never accepted.

Whichever you pick, the change events matter more than the count. Someone deactivates a user on day 3 of a 30-day cycle — is that a credit, a reduction from next cycle, or nothing? That decision belongs to 15.3, but you need somewhere to *record* the change, which means seat quantity wants to be an event log, not a column you overwrite.

**A measured quantity** — API calls, gigabytes stored, minutes transcoded, messages sent, rows synced. This is the step that adds an entire subsystem, because the number does not exist until you build a pipeline that produces it:

```
call site ──emit(idempotencyKey, qty, occurredAt)──► usage store ──aggregate by window──┬──► invoice
                                                                                        └──► customer dashboard
```

Four requirements attach to that pipeline the moment money depends on it. It must be **idempotent**, because the hot path has retries and a retried request billed twice is a refund. It must key windows off **event time, not ingest time**, or a queue backlog moves revenue between months. It must be **reproducible** — someone will dispute an invoice from four months ago and you will need to show the same number again. And the invoice and the dashboard must read the **same aggregate**; two pipelines both computing "usage" is the defining bug of metered billing, and it is always discovered by a customer.

Late events are the interesting failure. An event whose `occurredAt` falls in a window you already invoiced has two possible policies: carry it into the next window, or reopen the invoice. Carry-forward is slightly wrong and completely explainable; reopening is accurate and makes invoices mutable, which finance, tax, and every downstream system will hate you for. Carry forward, and put a bound on how late you will accept an event at all.

### Axis 2 — how you price the count

Given a quantity, the rate structure turns it into money:

- **Flat** — a fixed amount per cycle, ignoring quantity.
- **Per unit** — quantity × unit price, linear forever.
- **Graduated** — each rate tier is charged at its own rate, like income tax brackets.
- **Volume** — *all* units are charged at the rate of the tier the total reaches.
- **Package (stairstep)** — sold in indivisible blocks; 10,001 units of a 10,000-unit block is two blocks.

These are not stylistic variants. 25,000 units, with the first 10,000 at $0.10 and everything above at $0.05:

```
graduated   10,000 × $0.10  +  15,000 × $0.05        = $1,750
volume      25,000 × $0.05                            = $1,250
package     ceil(25,000 / 10,000) = 3 blocks × $800   = $2,400
```

Same usage, same tier table, three prices, 92% apart at the extremes. "Tiered usage pricing" in a requirements document is not a requirement.

Two things collapse into this axis rather than needing their own machinery. An **included allowance** ("100,000 calls included, then $0.50 per thousand") is just a graduated first tier priced at zero. A **minimum commitment** is a flat line plus a graduated structure whose early tiers are already paid for. Recognizing those as special cases of something you already have is the difference between a small pricing engine and a large one.

### Axis 3 — the cycle

Monthly or annual, anchored to a date, and — the part that surprises people — **charged in different directions on the same invoice**. Fixed components are billed in advance: you pay for January's seats in January. Metered components are billed in arrears: you pay for January's API calls in February. A single invoice therefore routinely contains next month's seats and last month's usage, and customers read it as a mistake unless the layout explains itself.

Annual changes three things at once: cash arrives up front, cancellation becomes a question about refunds rather than a switch, and MRR has to be amortized rather than booked — the $12,000 annual deal is $1,000 of MRR, and the difference between those two numbers is how revenue dashboards end up lying. That accounting thread continues in 15.5.

### Trials and free tiers are states, not plans

A trial is a subscription in a `trialing` state, with a trial end date and a price already attached. The alternative — a separate `trial` plan — makes conversion a migration, which is how conversion loses data and reports become impossible ("what did this customer trial before they bought?").

The design choices that actually matter are upstream of the schema: card up front (higher conversion rate of a smaller funnel, and the subscription simply starts charging) versus no card (larger funnel, and conversion needs an explicit collection step and a dunning-shaped set of reminders). A free tier is best modelled as a subscription with a zero-amount item rather than as the absence of a subscription, so that every account has one lifecycle, one state machine, and one place to look.

Freemium has a cost the billing model should make visible: free users consume metered resources. If you are measuring usage for billing, measure it for free accounts too — you will want that number the first time someone asks what the free tier costs to run.

### Where the catalogue lives

Three arrangements, and this is a real fork.

**The provider is the source of truth.** Prices, subscriptions, and invoices live in Stripe or equivalent; you store the ids and derive entitlements from webhooks. Least code, and your model is bounded by theirs.

**You are the source of truth.** Your catalogue drives the provider through an API. Necessary when your pricing shape genuinely isn't expressible in theirs, and it means you own invoicing correctness, proration arithmetic, and tax edge cases.

**Split** — catalogue and invoicing at the provider, metering and entitlements local. This is where most systems that charge for usage end up, and it comes with a standing obligation: a **reconciliation job** that compares what you think each customer owes with what the provider thinks, daily. Two systems that both believe they know the answer will diverge, and you want to find out before the customer does.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A single flat price** — one self-serve product, buyers who differ little in size, and no metric that tracks the value they get. Under a few hundred customers this is frequently the highest-revenue option per hour of engineering spent, and it stays viable much longer than teams expect.
- **Packages (good/better/best)** — buyers differ in *which capabilities* they need rather than in volume. Three packages is the common shape because it gives the middle one something to be the middle of; four is fine, seven means you are avoiding a decision.
- **Per seat** — the value is proportional to the number of humans using the product, adding a human is an explicit action, and "user" is uncontroversial in your domain. Collaboration tools, CRMs, design tools.
- **Usage-based** — at least two of: your own cost of goods scales with the same unit; the customer's value scales with it; the customer can predict their own volume. Infrastructure, messaging, payments, anything AI-inference-shaped. Roughly the profile where a 10× customer genuinely costs you close to 10×.
- **Hybrid — platform fee plus usage** — you sell to both a self-serve long tail and to enterprises. The fixed component covers your floor and makes revenue forecastable; the metered component captures the upside from the customers who grow.
- **Committed contracts with minimums** — your own capacity is committed (reserved instances, pre-purchased inference, a carrier contract). You are passing a commitment you already made down to the customer, which is a reason, not a preference.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Building a pricing engine before you have a second price.** Two hardcoded prices with a comment is not technical debt; it is a correct decision with a known expiry.
- **Metering before you know which unit correlates with value.** The failure mode is charging for the wrong thing, which is far more expensive than charging late. Instrument the candidate metrics, then **shadow-bill** for a cycle or two — compute the invoice, show nobody, compare against what you'd have charged — before a cent depends on it.
- **Per-seat pricing where the buyer's instinct is to share a login.** You have invented an enforcement problem, a support problem, and a reason for customers to feel clever at your expense.
- **Usage-based pricing where the buyer is a procurement department.** They need a number for a purchase order, up front, that will not move. Variable pricing does not fail technically here; it fails the approval process.
- **Multi-currency, tax engines, and multi-entity invoicing before your first customer outside your home jurisdiction.**
- **A configurator that supports any pricing sales can imagine.** The productive version is a small set of supported shapes plus a deliberate escape hatch for genuinely negotiated contracts, and a rule about who is allowed to create one. Without the rule, the escape hatch becomes the main path within a year.

## Trade-offs

- **Every dimension you price on is a permanent measurement obligation.** Not "we have a counter" — a number that is correct, reproducible months later, and defensible to a customer who is annoyed. You did not think of it as an SLA when you added the meter.
- **Invoices are customer-facing artefacts with legal weight.** A bug here is a refund, an apology, and occasionally a restatement, not a line in your error tracker. The blast radius of a rounding mistake is the entire customer base at once.
- **The catalogue only grows.** You can stop selling a price; you cannot delete it while any subscription references it, and some will for years. Plan for a price book that is mostly historical.
- **The test surface is combinatorial** — packages × cycles × currencies × add-ons × rate structures × lifecycle states — and the expensive bugs live in the combinations, not the cases.
- **Usage pricing transfers forecasting risk onto you.** Your revenue becomes as spiky as your customers' traffic, and someone will eventually ask engineering to explain a revenue dip that turns out to be a customer's cron job being rescheduled.
- **Bill shock becomes a support and trust cost.** The moment the bill is computed rather than agreed, you owe customers spend alerts, in-product visibility, and usually hard caps — none of which existed in the flat-price version.
- **Pricing changes become engineering work, permanently.** The mitigation is making the common shapes configuration rather than code; the thing you cannot mitigate is that someone from the pricing meeting will now always need someone from your team.
- **Reconciliation is ongoing operational cost.** Split ownership means a daily job, an alert, and a person who understands both models.

## Alternatives

**One flat price for everything.** Basecamp's position. It deletes this entire subsystem — no rate tiers, no metering, no proration, no seat definition. It leaves money on the table at the top of the market and excludes the bottom, and for a surprising number of products that trade is worth making for several years.

**Prices negotiated per contract, stored as documents.** Common in enterprise-only businesses: the price book is a folder of order forms and the "engine" is a finance team. Honest at ten customers, catastrophic at two hundred, and the transition between those states is the project nobody budgets for.

**Non-subscription revenue models.** A take rate on transactions makes your unit of measure money rather than events, which changes the correctness bar and drags in reconciliation against someone else's ledger. Prepaid credits or packs move cash up front and replace dunning with balances and expiry rules — a genuinely different system, often simpler, and a good fit when consumption is bursty and irregular. Perpetual licence plus maintenance still exists and still works for on-premise software.

**Buy the model rather than building it.** Stripe Billing, Chargebee, and Recurly for subscriptions; Orb, Metronome, m3ter, and Lago for metering-heavy pricing. What you gain is the rate structures, proration, invoicing, tax integration, and dunning, all already correct. What you give up is that their model becomes your model — a pricing shape they do not support is one you do not sell, or one you maintain an escape hatch for. That is a smaller loss than it sounds while your pricing is ordinary, and a real one the day pricing becomes a competitive weapon.

**Build.** Justified when the metering *is* the product, when your unit of measure is unusual enough that no vendor expresses it, or at volumes where per-invoice pricing dominates. Rarely justified at the point teams first consider it.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — prices in code, one enum.** Where every system starts, and where it should start.

**L1 — get the price out of the code and split the four meanings.** A `prices` table; `subscriptions.price_id` on the tenant; entitlements resolved from the subscription rather than from a string comparison. Nothing new is sellable yet. Everything afterwards becomes a row rather than a deploy. *Signal to start:* the second price, or the first discount.

**L2 — make prices immutable and versioned.** Publishing supersedes editing. Grandfathering stops being a schema problem. *Signal:* the first price change with existing customers on the old one — which is to say, before it happens, because after it happens you are migrating.

**L3 — quantity as a first-class line item.** Multiple items per subscription, each with a price reference and a quantity, so add-ons stop multiplying the enum. Pick and write down a seat definition. Record quantity changes as events. *Signal:* the first add-on, or the first per-seat package.

**L4 — meter one dimension, and shadow-bill it.** Emit events with idempotency keys from the authoritative call site, aggregate by event-time window, serve the dashboard from the same aggregate. Run it for a full cycle without charging anyone, and compare. *Signal:* a pricing conversation where someone says "per" followed by a noun you do not currently count.

**L5 — commitments, minimums, and negotiated contracts.** Custom prices as unlisted rows in the same catalogue, not as code paths. *Signal:* enterprise deals that a coupon can no longer express.

Most products should stop at L3. Products whose costs scale with a measurable unit will end up at L4 whether they plan to or not; the only real choice is whether the meter is built before or after the pricing is announced.

## Implementation

TypeScript, for the catalogue types and the rate arithmetic, which is where the decomposition above stops being conceptual.

```ts
// Integer minor units. Never a float: 0.1 + 0.2 === 0.30000000000000004,
// and an invoice that is one cent wrong is a support ticket that costs
// more than the cent.
type Money = number;

type Tier = { upTo: number | "inf"; unitAmount: Money; flatAmount?: Money };

type RateStructure =
  | { kind: "flat"; amount: Money }                    // quantity ignored
  | { kind: "per_unit"; unitAmount: Money }
  | { kind: "graduated"; tiers: Tier[] }               // each tier at its own rate
  | { kind: "volume"; tiers: Tier[] }                  // all units at the reached tier
  | { kind: "package"; size: number; amount: Money };  // indivisible blocks

type Dimension =
  | { kind: "fixed" }
  | { kind: "declared"; unit: "seat" | "project" }
  | { kind: "measured"; meter: string };

type Price = {
  id: string;            // immutable and versioned: "seat_pro_v2"
  productId: string;
  currency: string;
  cycle: "monthly" | "annual";
  dimension: Dimension;
  rate: RateStructure;
  sellable: boolean;     // whether NEW subscriptions may reference it.
                         // Existing ones ignore this flag entirely — that is
                         // what grandfathering is, and why prices are never deleted.
};
```

The arithmetic:

```ts
function amountFor(rate: RateStructure, qty: number): Money {
  switch (rate.kind) {
    case "flat":
      return rate.amount;

    case "per_unit":
      return rate.unitAmount * qty;

    case "package":
      // 10,001 units of a 10,000 block is two blocks. Customers will
      // discover this on the invoice; say so on the pricing page first.
      return Math.ceil(qty / rate.size) * rate.amount;

    case "volume": {
      const tier = rate.tiers.find(t => t.upTo === "inf" || qty <= t.upTo)!;
      return (tier.flatAmount ?? 0) + tier.unitAmount * qty;
    }

    case "graduated": {
      let remaining = qty, floor = 0, total = 0;
      for (const t of rate.tiers) {
        const ceiling = t.upTo === "inf" ? Infinity : t.upTo;
        const inTier = Math.min(remaining, ceiling - floor);
        total += (t.flatAmount ?? 0) + t.unitAmount * inTier;
        remaining -= inTier;
        floor = ceiling;
        if (remaining <= 0) break;
      }
      return total;
    }
  }
}
```

Two asides the code surfaces on its own.

An **included allowance** needs no feature: it is a graduated first tier with `unitAmount: 0`. If you find yourself adding an `includedUnits` field next to the tier table, you have two mechanisms for one idea and they will disagree at the boundary.

`unitAmount: Money` as an integer is a lie that holds only while your cheapest unit costs at least one cent. The day you sell API calls at $0.0004 each, the unit rate needs more precision than the invoice line does, and you must decide *where rounding happens*: per unit (wrong — 25,000 roundings of $0.0004 up to $0.01 is a 25× overcharge), per line (normal and defensible), or per invoice (accurate, and makes a line's own arithmetic un-checkable by the customer). Round once, at the line, and keep the unrounded value alongside it so disputes are answerable.

Metering, reduced to the two functions that matter:

```ts
async function record(e: UsageEvent) {
  // The idempotency key comes from the cause — the request id, the job id —
  // never generated at emit time. A retried request that emits twice is
  // a double charge, and retries are the normal case, not the edge case.
  await usage.insertIgnoreConflict({
    key: e.idempotencyKey,               // unique index does the work
    subscriptionId: e.subscriptionId,
    meter: e.meter,
    quantity: e.quantity,
    occurredAt: e.occurredAt,            // event time
    window: windowOf(e.occurredAt),      // ...so a queue backlog cannot
  });                                    //    move revenue between months
}

// The invoice run and the customer's usage page both call this.
// Two code paths computing "usage" is the defining bug of metered billing,
// and the customer always finds it first.
function billableQuantity(subscriptionId: string, meter: string, w: Window) {
  return usage.sum({ subscriptionId, meter, window: w });
}
```

Assigning `window` from `occurredAt` is what makes the late-event policy an explicit choice rather than an accident: an event arriving after its window is invoiced now lands, visibly, in a closed window. Carry it to the next invoice with a note, refuse it beyond some age, and do not reopen the invoice.

## Further Reading

- **Stripe's Billing documentation — products, prices, subscription items, and tiers.** Worth reading even if you never use Stripe. It is the vocabulary the industry converged on, and the graduated/volume/package distinction is documented there with worked numbers.
- **Stripe's billing meters / usage-based billing guides**, for the event-ingestion model specifically, including their position on late events and aggregation windows.
- **Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, chapter 11** — event time versus processing time, windowing, and stragglers. Metered billing is that chapter with money attached, and reading it first saves discovering the same problems by invoice.
- **Fowler, _Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture_ — the Money pattern**, and his "Accounting Patterns" writing. The argument for a money value object with explicit currency and rounding, and for ledgers over mutable balances.
- **Ramanujam & Tacke, _Monetizing Innovation_.** Not an engineering book; the one that makes the case for choosing the value metric before building anything to measure it. The engineering here is downstream of that choice.
- **David Skok, "SaaS Metrics 2.0."** Cited in 1.1 for the business frame; here for the MRR and ARR definitions your dashboard should be implementing, including why annual contracts are amortized.
- **An overview of ASC 606 / IFRS 15.** You will not implement it, but it explains why finance asks for data your schema does not have, and it is the reason 15.5 exists.
- **Lago's or OpenMeter's documentation and source.** Open implementations of a metering model, useful as a shape to compare yours against without committing to a vendor.

A **Java sibling** for this article would be worth writing, and it would not be a translation. The TypeScript above quietly papers over the hardest part of billing arithmetic by declaring money to be an integer and moving on; Java's `BigDecimal` and the JSR-354 money API make currency, scale, and `RoundingMode` into things you cannot leave implicit — you must name the rounding mode at every operation, which turns the sub-cent unit-rate problem from an aside into a compile-time obligation. It also brings the multi-currency case into the type system, where an accidental addition of USD to EUR fails rather than producing a number. That is a genuinely different answer to the same problem, not the same answer in different syntax. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying past billing: **whenever a value is both "what we offer today" and "what this party agreed to," those are two objects and one reference, not one column.** Prices are the loudest instance because the disagreement shows up on an invoice, but the same shape is everywhere once you look — terms-of-service versions a user accepted, the tax rate in force on the date of a sale, the API version a client integrated against, the consent text someone actually saw. In each case the catalogue moves forward and the agreement must not. Systems that store only the current value can always tell you what is true now, and can never tell you what was promised.
