# Spreading the Load: How Load Balancers Choose, Check, and Let Go

_Module 23.1b — Scalability Patterns_

## The Problem

Suppose you run the backend for a restaurant-ordering platform: six identical Go instances behind a load balancer using round robin, the default on most balancers. Round robin hands each new request to the next instance in turn, so each instance gets exactly one-sixth of the requests. Three incidents in one quarter show that one-sixth of the requests is not the same as one-sixth of the load.

**Equal requests, unequal work.** About 900 requests per second arrive, and nearly all are cheap: a menu read takes about 10 ms. A few times a minute, though, a restaurant exports a month of orders as CSV, which holds a full core for about 6 seconds. Every so often one instance happens to get three exports at once. Both its cores are busy, and round robin keeps handing it one-sixth of the menu reads anyway. On that instance, menu-read p99 reaches 1.8 seconds while the fleet-average CPU graph shows 40%. Round robin counts requests. It has no idea what they cost.

**Sick, but passing its health check.** After a brief network blip, all of instance 4's database connections are dead, and its pool doesn't notice. Every checkout sent there waits 5 seconds and fails. The balancer's health check calls `/health`, which returns 200 as long as the process is running, so instance 4 stays in rotation. One checkout in six fails for 40 minutes, until someone restarts it.

The team overcorrects: `/health` now runs `SELECT 1` against the database. The next week, the database stalls for 20 seconds during a failover. Health checks run every 5 seconds, mark an instance down after 2 failures, and bring it back after 3 passes. All six instances drop out together about 10 seconds into the stall and return about 15 seconds after it ends. For those 25 seconds, every request gets a 503, including menu pages served from cache that never needed the database.

**Connections that never move.** The order-tracking screen keeps a WebSocket open for as long as the customer watches, often hours. There are about 30,000 open sockets, 5,000 per instance. A rolling deploy restarts instances one at a time, and each restart's clients reconnect spread evenly across all six. The instances restarted first keep collecting a share of every later restart's reconnections:

```
sockets after one rolling deploy (round robin)
inst 1  ███████████████  7,600
inst 2  ██████████████   6,800
inst 3  ████████████     5,800
inst 4  █████████        4,700
inst 5  ███████          3,300
inst 6  ████             1,800
```

Nothing moves them back, because a connection is balanced once, when it opens. Instance 1 is now near its memory limit, and the next deploy starts from this imbalance and makes it worse.

## What It Is

A **load balancer** sits between callers and a pool of interchangeable instances (usually called *backends*) and decides, per request or per connection, which one handles the work. 23.1a made the instances interchangeable. This article is about the component that exploits that, and it has three jobs:

- **Choose:** pick a backend for each request or connection. The rule it uses is the *balancing algorithm*.
- **Check:** keep broken backends out of rotation, without removing healthy ones by mistake.
- **Let go and let in:** remove a backend without dropping its work, and add one without overwhelming it.

Each incident above was one of these jobs done naively.

Three neighbors get confused with it. A **reverse proxy** is the kind of software that usually does the balancing (NGINX, HAProxy, Envoy); it can also do other things, like TLS termination and caching. An **API gateway** is a reverse proxy with product-level concerns added, such as authentication, rate limiting and per-route policy (36.2). A **service mesh** moves balancing out of a central box and into a proxy next to every caller (36.1). All three balance load; this article covers the balancing itself.

## Recognizing the Problem

- One instance's p99 or CPU runs far above the others, while the balancer reports an even request split.
- One instance's error rate is high while its health check stays green.
- Every instance goes unhealthy at the same moment, during a blip in something they all share.
- 502s or 503s cluster around deploys, restarts or scale-in events.
- Open-connection counts drift apart over days and jump apart after each deploy; the newest instance is nearly idle.
- A gRPC or HTTP/2 caller sends all its traffic to one backend.
- Newly added instances time out or error for their first minute.
- Duplicate orders, emails or charges line up with timeouts at the balancer.

## How It Works

### Where the decision is made

A balancer can work at two layers, and the layer decides what it can see.

**Layer 4 (transport)** balancers see TCP connections: addresses and ports, nothing inside. They're fast and work for any protocol, but they balance *connections*, not requests.

**Layer 7 (application)** balancers terminate HTTP, so they see each request: path, headers, cookies, status codes. That lets them balance per request, route by path, retry, and notice 500s. The costs are more CPU per request and, usually, holding your TLS certificates.

The difference matters most for protocols that multiplex. HTTP/2 and gRPC send many requests over one long-lived connection, so an L4 balancer pins all of them to whichever backend got that connection:

```
L4 + HTTP/2:  caller ══one connection══▶ backend A  (800 req/s)
                                         backend B  (idle)

L7:           caller ══▶ balancer ─┬──▶ backend A  (400 req/s)
                                   └──▶ backend B  (400 req/s)
```

The balancer can also live in different places:

- **A dedicated balancer** (a managed cloud load balancer, or HAProxy, NGINX, Envoy) sits in the path for every caller. It's simple to reason about, and it must itself be redundant: managed ones are, and self-hosted ones run in pairs sharing a floating IP.
- **Client-side balancing** puts the backend list inside each caller, which picks directly with no extra hop. gRPC libraries support this, and a service mesh does it through a proxy beside each caller. The cost is balancing logic in every client, and each client sees only its own traffic.
- **DNS** can return several addresses, but resolvers and clients cache the answers, so it reacts in minutes rather than seconds. It suits coarse, global steering (24.2), not per-request balancing.

### Choosing a backend

**Round robin** sends requests to each backend in turn, and **weighted round robin** gives bigger instances a bigger share. It's the right default when requests cost about the same and instances are the same size. **Random** choice behaves the same way in aggregate and needs no shared counter, which helps when several balancer nodes run independently.

**Least outstanding requests** (often called least connections) sends each request to the backend with the fewest requests in flight. It handles uneven cost without knowing about it: the instance busy with three exports has more requests open, so it gets fewer new ones. It has two weaknesses:

- **Partial views.** Each of several balancer nodes counts only its own requests.
- **Herding.** When counts are stale, every node picks the same least-loaded backend at once and overloads it.

**Power of two choices** picks two backends at random and sends the request to the less loaded of the pair. It sounds too simple, but it captures most of the benefit of checking every backend, costs the same at any pool size, and tolerates stale or partial counts, because random pairs stop the nodes from piling onto one backend. Several modern proxies use it by default, and it's a good first upgrade from round robin.

**Latency-aware** algorithms keep a moving average of each backend's response time (often an exponentially weighted moving average, EWMA) and prefer fast backends. They react to slowness that counts miss, like a noisy neighbor or a slow disk.

The last two share a trap: the **black hole**. A broken backend that fails in 2 ms looks like the fastest, least busy instance in the pool, so the algorithm sends it *more* traffic. Any load-aware algorithm must treat an error as a slow, expensive response, never as a fast one.

**Hash-based** choice sends the same key to the same backend every time: a client IP, a user ID, a document ID. **Consistent hashing** arranges backends so that adding or removing one moves only about 1/N of the keys, instead of nearly all of them. Use it when landing on the same backend has value, such as a cache tier, or a collaborative editor where everyone editing one document must reach the same process. The cost is hot keys, since one popular document overloads its backend. *Bounded-load* variants cap each backend's share and spill the excess onward.

### Checking health

**Active checks** poll an endpoint every few seconds. Thresholds prevent flapping: down after N failures, back after M passes. Faster detection means more false alarms, so the thresholds are always a trade.

**Passive checks**, also called *outlier detection*, watch real traffic instead. After, say, five consecutive 5xx responses or timeouts, the backend is ejected for 30 seconds and then tried again. Passive checks would have caught instance 4, because they see the checkouts failing whatever `/health` says.

What a health endpoint should check comes down to one question: *would removing this instance help?*

```
instance-local → check it   (its own pool, disk, warm-up)
shared by all  → don't      (the database, Redis, a partner API)
```

If instance 4's pool is broken, removing instance 4 helps. If the database is down, removing every instance helps nobody. So a readiness check should confirm that *this instance's* connections work, not that the database is healthy in general. Kubernetes applies the same logic to its two probes:

- **Readiness** failure removes the instance from rotation. Point it at instance-local readiness.
- **Liveness** failure restarts the process. Point it only at "this process is stuck", or a dependency outage becomes a fleet-wide restart loop.

Two safety valves back this up:

- **Fail open.** When every backend looks unhealthy, the checks are more likely wrong than the whole fleet broken, so many balancers route to all of them anyway. Envoy calls this the *panic threshold*, and several managed cloud balancers behave the same way by default. Check whether yours does.
- **Cap ejections.** Passive checks should never eject more than a fixed share of the pool, such as a third, for the same reason.

### Letting go and letting in

**Draining** removes a backend without dropping work: stop sending it new requests, let in-flight ones finish, then remove it, within a time limit. It's the balancer's half of 23.1a's shutdown sequence, and the drain limit must exceed your slowest normal request.

**Slow start** ramps a new backend's share up over a period, such as 60 seconds, instead of giving it a full share at once. It matters when a fresh instance is slower than a warm one: empty caches, cold connection pools and, on the JVM, code that hasn't been compiled yet.

**Long-lived connections** break the assumption that balancing happens often. A WebSocket or HTTP/2 connection is balanced once, when it opens, so the imbalance from a deploy or scale-out persists. The fix is to end connections on purpose:

- **Give connections a maximum age.** The server closes each one after, say, 30 to 40 minutes, with random jitter so they don't all close together. HTTP/2 and gRPC do this gracefully with a `GOAWAY` frame; WebSocket servers send a close frame.
- **Reconnect with jittered backoff** on the client, so closing connections doesn't cause a reconnect stampede.
- **Place new connections by least connections**, so reconnects fill the emptiest instances first.

With a maximum age in place, any deploy's imbalance fades within one age period.

### Sticky sessions

**Sticky sessions** (session affinity) keep sending a client to the same backend. The balancer either sets a cookie naming the backend or hashes the client's IP. IP hashing breaks behind NAT, where a whole office or mobile carrier shares one address, so cookies are usually the better choice.

Affinity makes per-instance state appear to work, and its costs follow from that: uneven load, lost state when the instance dies, and disruptive deploys and scale-in. Treat it as a stepping stone while moving state out (23.1a), or as a requirement of a specific protocol. For example, Socket.IO needs affinity when clients fall back to HTTP long-polling across several servers.

### Retries at the balancer

An L7 balancer can retry a failed request on another backend. Whether that's safe depends on how the request failed:

- **The connection failed before the request was sent.** Always safe to retry.
- **A timeout or 5xx after the request was sent.** The backend may have done the work, so retrying `POST /orders` can create a second order. Retry only idempotent methods, or requests carrying an idempotency key (17.1).

Retries also multiply. Three layers that each try three times turn one failing call into 27 attempts at the bottom. A **retry budget**, such as retries never exceeding 10% of requests, keeps retries meant to help a struggling backend from finishing it off.

## When It's Appropriate

Each technique answers a specific pressure:

- **Round robin, and nothing more,** when requests are uniform and instances match. An example is a JSON API where 95% of requests take 20 to 60 ms, running on identical instances.
- **Least outstanding requests or power of two choices** when request cost varies widely, such as 10 ms reads mixed with 6-second exports, or tenants whose queries differ a hundredfold. With several balancer nodes, or client-side balancing across 200 callers, prefer power of two choices.
- **L7 or client-side balancing** for gRPC and other HTTP/2 traffic between services, which an L4 balancer pins to single backends.
- **Consistent hashing** when the same key should reach the same backend. One case is a 12-node cache tier, where remapping every key on each change would empty every cache. Another is a collaboration server holding each document's live state.
- **Passive health checks** once the fleet is big enough that a quietly broken instance is routine. If each instance develops such a fault once per 1,000 hours, a 20-instance fleet sees one about every two days.
- **Maximum connection age** whenever connections outlive a deploy: WebSockets, server-sent events, gRPC streams, or pooled HTTP/2 connections between services.
- **Slow start** when an instance's first minute is measurably worse than its steady state, as with JVM services or instances whose caches take a minute to fill.
- **Retries at the balancer** for idempotent reads, so a connection failure on one backend never reaches the user.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **One or two identical instances with uniform traffic.** Round robin and a shallow readiness check are enough, and tuning the algorithm buys nothing measurable.
- **Writing your own balancer for a dedicated tier.** A managed balancer, HAProxy, NGINX or Envoy already does everything in this article, with far more testing. The Implementation below exists to show the mechanism and to cover the client-side case.
- **Latency-aware balancing on a small, uniform fleet.** The moving averages mostly chase noise, and the routing becomes harder to explain.
- **Consistent hashing without a locality benefit.** If any backend serves any request equally well, hashing only adds hot-key risk.
- **Sticky sessions as a permanent answer to in-memory state.** That's 23.1a's problem, postponed.
- **Deep health checks that test shared dependencies.** They turn a partial outage into a total one.
- **Retrying non-idempotent requests**, at any layer.

## Trade-offs

- **The balancer sits in the path.** It adds a hop, well under a millisecond on a local network for L4 and more for L7 with TLS. It also has its own capacity limit, and it's one more component to keep redundant, patched and monitored.
- **L7 costs more than L4.** Parsing HTTP and terminating TLS use CPU, and the balancer holds your certificates. In exchange you get per-request decisions, retries and error visibility.
- **Smarter algorithms are harder to explain.** "Why did this request go to instance 3?" has no simple answer under power of two choices or EWMA. Load-aware algorithms also create feedback loops: black holes, and herding on stale data.
- **Health checks trade speed for accuracy.** Tight thresholds remove healthy instances during a garbage-collection pause, while loose ones leave a broken instance in rotation for minutes.
- **Draining and slow start make everything slower.** With a 60-second drain and a 60-second ramp, a one-at-a-time rolling deploy of 20 instances takes the better part of an hour. With long-lived connections, draining can take hours unless connections have a maximum age.
- **A maximum connection age adds steady reconnect traffic.** 30,000 sockets on a 30-minute age means about 1,000 reconnects a minute, each with its own handshake and authentication.
- **Affinity costs evenness,** as described under sticky sessions.
- **Retries trade availability for load and duplication risk.** Without a budget and idempotency, they amplify outages and repeat side effects.
- **Client-side balancing spreads the logic around.** Every language's client library needs the same algorithm, health handling and configuration, and each client sees only part of the picture.

## Alternatives

- **Separate expensive work into its own pool.** Route `/exports/*` to a separate set of instances with an L7 path rule, and cheap reads never queue behind exports, whatever the algorithm. For the first incident, this is often a better fix than a smarter algorithm.
- **Let workers pull instead of pushing work to them.** When work goes through a queue, each worker takes a job only when it has room, so the queue balances perfectly with no algorithm at all. It doesn't suit requests that need an immediate answer, but the CSV export doesn't need one: it can become a background job with a download link (Module 10, 23.4).
- **Partition by key and route deterministically.** Assign each tenant or region to a fixed group of instances (a *cell*) and route by that key. Balancing then only happens inside a cell, and a bad cell affects only its own tenants (23.6).

## How to Achieve It

**1. Start with a managed or off-the-shelf balancer, round robin, and a shallow readiness check.** The check confirms that the process is ready and that its own connections work. Add per-instance dashboards for latency, errors and open connections, because a fleet average hides the one bad instance. *Move on when* the per-instance graphs start to diverge.

**2. Match draining to your shutdown sequence (23.1a).** Set the drain limit above your slowest normal request. *Move on when* deploys and scale-in produce no 5xx.

**3. Switch to least outstanding requests or power of two choices.** On most balancers this is a one-line configuration change. *Move on when* one instance still suffers under uneven work. At that point, split the expensive routes into their own pool, or move them to a queue.

**4. Add passive health checks with an ejection cap,** and make sure errors count as load. *Do this when* an incident shows a broken instance staying in rotation, or per-instance error rates diverge.

**5. Give long-lived connections a maximum age,** with jitter on both the server's close and the client's reconnect. *Do this when* connection counts drift apart after deploys.

**6. Add slow start** if new instances error or time out during their first minute.

**7. Set a retry policy.** Start with connection failures only. Add idempotent requests later, with a retry budget.

**8. Move service-to-service traffic to client-side or mesh balancing** when gRPC between services grows, or when a central balancer becomes a bottleneck or a cost problem (36.1).

## Implementation

The code is Go, because proxies are one of Go's strengths: the standard library ships a reverse proxy, and atomic counters make per-backend load tracking cheap. Treat this as a teaching or client-side balancer, not a replacement for Envoy.

### Power of two choices over a reverse proxy

```go
type backend struct {
	proxy        *httputil.ReverseProxy
	inflight     atomic.Int64
	fails        atomic.Int32
	ejectedUntil atomic.Int64 // unix nanoseconds
}

func (b *backend) available(now int64) bool { return now >= b.ejectedUntil.Load() }

type pool struct{ backends []*backend }

func (p *pool) pick() *backend {
	now := time.Now().UnixNano()
	var candidates []*backend
	for _, b := range p.backends {
		if b.available(now) {
			candidates = append(candidates, b)
		}
	}
	if len(candidates) == 0 {
		candidates = p.backends // fail open: the checks are probably wrong
	}
	a := candidates[rand.IntN(len(candidates))]
	c := candidates[rand.IntN(len(candidates))] // may be the same backend; that's fine
	if c.inflight.Load() < a.inflight.Load() {
		return c
	}
	return a
}

func (p *pool) ServeHTTP(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
	b := p.pick()
	b.inflight.Add(1)
	defer b.inflight.Add(-1)
	b.proxy.ServeHTTP(w, r)
}
```

**Where this breaks.** `inflight` counts only this process's requests. With three proxy instances, each one sees about a third of every backend's load. Power of two choices tolerates that. "Always pick the global minimum" would not, because all three proxies would agree on the same stale minimum and pile onto it.

### Passive ejection, capped

```go
const (
	maxFails = 5
	ejectFor = 30 * time.Second
)

func (p *pool) ejectedCount() int {
	now, n := time.Now().UnixNano(), 0
	for _, b := range p.backends {
		if !b.available(now) {
			n++
		}
	}
	return n
}

func (p *pool) record(b *backend, ok bool) {
	if ok {
		b.fails.Store(0)
		return
	}
	if b.fails.Add(1) < maxFails {
		return
	}
	b.fails.Store(0)
	if (p.ejectedCount()+1)*3 > len(p.backends) {
		return // never eject more than a third: a shared failure is likelier
	}
	b.ejectedUntil.Store(time.Now().Add(ejectFor).UnixNano())
}

func (p *pool) wire(b *backend) {
	b.proxy.ModifyResponse = func(resp *http.Response) error {
		p.record(b, resp.StatusCode < 500)
		return nil
	}
	b.proxy.ErrorHandler = func(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request, err error) {
		if !errors.Is(err, context.Canceled) { // the caller left; not the backend's fault
			p.record(b, false)
		}
		w.WriteHeader(http.StatusBadGateway)
	}
}
```

**Three things to notice.**

- **The black hole.** Delete the `record` calls and this pool black-holes: a backend returning 500 in 2 ms has the fewest requests in flight, so it wins almost every comparison in `pick`. Ejection is what turns fast errors into lost traffic instead of attracted traffic.
- **The canceled-request check.** Without it, customers closing a browser tab would count as backend failures, and a busy evening could eject healthy instances.
- **The cap isn't atomic.** The count and the ejection happen separately, so simultaneous failures can briefly push past a third by one or two backends. A mutex around `record` fixes that if it matters.

### Ending long-lived connections on purpose

For gRPC, a server option does the work. grpc-go adds its own jitter to the age and closes with `GOAWAY`:

```go
srv := grpc.NewServer(grpc.KeepaliveParams(keepalive.ServerParameters{
	MaxConnectionAge:      30 * time.Minute, // then GOAWAY; clients reconnect and rebalance
	MaxConnectionAgeGrace: 5 * time.Minute,  // let in-flight calls finish
}))
```

For WebSockets with gorilla/websocket, each connection schedules its own polite close:

```go
age := 30*time.Minute + rand.N(10*time.Minute) // jitter spreads the reconnects out
t := time.AfterFunc(age, func() {
	msg := websocket.FormatCloseMessage(websocket.CloseServiceRestart, "rebalance")
	_ = conn.WriteControl(websocket.CloseMessage, msg, time.Now().Add(time.Second))
	_ = conn.Close()
})
defer t.Stop()
```

**The client has to cooperate.** It should treat close code 1012 (service restart) as "reconnect shortly, with jittered backoff" rather than as an error. Otherwise every rebalance flashes "connection lost" on the order-tracking screen, and customers start refreshing, which reconnects them all at once anyway.

## Further Reading

- **Michael Mitzenmacher, "The Power of Two Choices in Randomized Load Balancing"** (IEEE Transactions on Parallel and Distributed Systems, 2001). The result behind picking two at random, and why it comes so close to checking every backend.
- **Google, _Site Reliability Engineering_** (O'Reilly, 2016), chapters 19 to 22: load balancing at the frontend and in the datacenter, handling overload, and cascading failures. The source for retry budgets and for why "least loaded" misbehaves on stale data.
- **AWS Builders' Library: "Implementing health checks"** (David Yanacek) and **"Timeouts, retries, and backoff with jitter"** (Marc Brooker). The clearest treatment of shallow versus deep checks, fail-open behavior, and safe retries.
- **Envoy documentation:** the load balancing overview (least request, panic threshold, slow start) and outlier detection. A concrete, well-documented version of nearly every mechanism in this article.
- **Karger et al., "Consistent Hashing and Random Trees"** (STOC 1997), and **Mirrokni, Thorup & Zadimoghaddam, "Consistent Hashing with Bounded Loads"** (SODA 2018). The original idea, and the fix for hot keys.
- **Buoyant, "Beyond Round Robin: Load Balancing for Latency"** (2016). Measured comparisons of round robin, least loaded and peak EWMA under realistic latency distributions.
- **gRPC documentation on load balancing**, and grpc-go's keepalive parameters. Why L4 balancing fails for gRPC, and what client-side balancing looks like.
- **Kubernetes documentation: "Configure Liveness, Readiness and Startup Probes."**
- **In this collection:** 23.1a (interchangeable instances and shutdown order), 17.1 (idempotency keys), 9.1 (WebSockets), 23.4 (queues), 24.2 (multi-region failover), 36.1 and 36.2 (service mesh and API gateway).

A **Java** sibling would be worth writing, because the JVM is why slow start exists. A fresh JVM runs its first requests through the interpreter and early compiler tiers, so a new instance can be several times slower than a warm one for its first minute, and a full traffic share at that point produces exactly the timeouts slow start prevents. The sibling would also map Spring Boot's availability states and actuator probe groups onto the readiness/liveness split, including keeping database health indicators out of the liveness group. Go starts fully compiled, so a Go article can't show the warm-up problem. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: **a load balancer only balances what it can see.** It can't see what a request costs, whether a connection will outlive the deploy, or whether its health check tests the right thing, unless you set it up to.
