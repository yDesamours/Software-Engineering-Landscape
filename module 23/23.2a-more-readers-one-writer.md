# More Readers, One Writer: Connection Budgets, Read Replicas, and Replication Lag

_Module 23.2a — Scalability Patterns_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a job board. Employers post and edit listings, candidates search and apply, and everything lives in one Postgres primary with 16 vCPUs. At peak it serves about 3,000 queries a second, and 94% of them are reads: searches, listing pages, "my applications."

**The first wall is connections, not queries.** On the first Monday of hiring season, the app tier autoscales from 8 instances to 30. Each instance keeps a pool of 20 connections, so the fleet now wants 600, and Postgres starts refusing them with `FATAL: sorry, too many clients already`. Someone raises `max_connections` to 700. The errors stop, and throughput *drops*. Postgres runs a separate process for every connection, and 600 of them compete for 16 cores and shared internal locks. Most are idle at any given moment, but each still costs memory and bookkeeping.

The fix is a connection pooler in front of the database. PgBouncer in transaction mode lets the 600 client connections share 60 real ones, and throughput recovers. A week later, the nightly job that expires old listings stops running. It takes a session-level `pg_advisory_lock` so that only one copy runs. In transaction mode, its unlock went out on a different server connection than the lock, so the unlock failed and the lock stayed held on a connection PgBouncer keeps lending to other clients. Every night since, the job has found the lock taken and skipped.

**The second wall is read load.** With connections under control, primary CPU sits at 80% at peak, nearly all of it spent on reads. The team adds two read replicas: copies of the database that continuously apply the primary's changes and accept read-only queries. Searches and listing pages move to the replicas, and primary CPU falls to 35%.

Then the support tickets start:

- An employer fixes a typo in a listing title, and the page that loads after saving still shows the typo. They assume the save failed and edit it again.
- A candidate applies, opens "My applications", and the job isn't there. They apply again, and the employer receives a duplicate.
- A candidate refreshes a listing and sees "12 applicants", refreshes again and sees "9".
- At 2 a.m. a partner feed imports 200,000 listings. Replica lag, normally about 20 ms, climbs to 40 seconds, and every bug above becomes easy to reproduce.
- The weekly analytics query on a replica keeps failing with `canceling statement due to conflict with recovery`.

The first ticket, as a timeline:

```
t = 0 ms     POST /jobs/42  → primary   commit ✔
t = 25 ms    GET  /jobs/42  → replica   old title (lag 120 ms)
t = 120 ms   replica applies the change
```

None of these queries is wrong. Each one ran against a copy of the data that was correct a moment ago.

## What It Is

These are the first two tools for scaling a database before splitting it apart.

**Connection pooling** lets many application connections share a small number of real database connections. Each app instance usually has its own in-process pool (HikariCP in Java, for example). An **external pooler** such as PgBouncer sits between the whole fleet and the database and caps the total.

A **read replica** is a copy of the database that continuously applies the primary's change log and serves read-only queries. In Postgres, that log is the write-ahead log (WAL). All writes still go to one **primary**. **Replication lag** is how far a replica trails the primary. With the default, asynchronous replication, a transaction can be committed on the primary and still be invisible on a replica.

The tickets above each break a **session guarantee**, a promise about what one user sees across requests:

- **Read-your-writes:** after you change something, your later reads include the change. The typo and the missing application broke this.
- **Monotonic reads:** once you've seen a version of the data, you never see an older one. "12 applicants" followed by "9" broke this.

Replicas don't corrupt data. They silently drop these promises, and you have to add them back on purpose.

Two neighbors are worth separating. Replicas also provide **high availability**: a replica can be promoted if the primary fails (24.2). It's the same mechanism with a different goal, and often different settings. And replicas are not **sharding**: every replica holds all the data and none of them accept writes, so they do nothing for write load. Splitting the data is 23.2b and 38.2.

## Recognizing the Problem

- "Too many clients" or connection-timeout errors appear when the app tier scales out, even though traffic hasn't grown much.
- Database CPU or open connections climb with instance count while queries per second stay flat.
- Most database CPU goes to `SELECT`s; `pg_stat_statements` shows it.
- Users report "I saved but nothing changed", or duplicates appear right after a first attempt.
- Counts or lists go backward on refresh.
- Bugs appear during bulk imports or batch jobs and can't be reproduced in development.
- Advisory locks, `SET` commands, `LISTEN`, or temporary tables misbehave after a pooler is added.
- Long queries on a replica are canceled with "conflict with recovery".

## How It Works

### Connection budgets

Postgres runs one operating-system process per connection. Each costs memory, and every open connection adds a little to internal work such as tracking which transactions are running, even while idle. Throughput typically peaks when the number of *active* connections is a small multiple of the CPU cores, and past that point more connections mostly add contention. HikariCP's pool-sizing guide suggests starting near cores × 2 and measuring from there.

Scaling out the app tier multiplies connections without anyone deciding to, so budget them explicitly:

```
app instances × pool size  = client connections   30 × 20 = 600
external pooler            = real connections              60
database                   runs well near a small multiple of its cores
```

An external pooler has three modes (these are PgBouncer's names; other poolers are similar):

- **Session pooling.** A client keeps one server connection until it disconnects. Everything works, but it only helps when clients connect and disconnect often, not when a fleet holds idle pools open.
- **Transaction pooling.** A server connection is lent for one transaction, then returned. This is where the big multiplexing comes from. It breaks anything tied to a connection beyond one transaction: session-level advisory locks, `SET` without `LOCAL`, `LISTEN`, and temporary tables that outlive a transaction. Older PgBouncer versions also broke named prepared statements; version 1.21 added support for protocol-level prepared statements in this mode.
- **Statement pooling.** The connection returns after every statement, so multi-statement transactions aren't allowed. It's rarely used.

The expiry job has two fixes. It can use `pg_advisory_xact_lock`, which is released at commit and works in transaction mode. Or that one job can connect to the database directly, bypassing the pooler.

Where the pooler runs matters too. A shared pooler service (PgBouncer, PgCat, Supavisor, or a managed proxy such as RDS Proxy) caps the fleet's total. A pooler running beside each app instance reduces connection churn but doesn't cap the total, because every instance still has its own.

### Replication, and where lag comes from

```
app ──writes──▶ primary ──WAL──▶ replica 1 ──reads──▶ app
                        └──WAL──▶ replica 2 ──reads──▶ app
```

The primary commits a transaction and writes it to the WAL. Replicas receive the WAL and replay it. With asynchronous replication, the default, the commit returns to the application without waiting for any replica. Lag comes from three places:

- **Write bursts.** A bulk import produces WAL faster than a replica can replay it. In Postgres, replay runs in a single process, so a replica can fall behind with cores to spare.
- **Conflicts with replica queries.** A long read may need old row versions that replay wants to remove. Postgres either delays replay, up to `max_standby_streaming_delay`, which grows lag, or cancels the query, which produced the analytics error. Setting `hot_standby_feedback = on` stops the cancellations by asking the primary to keep those rows, at the cost of bloat on the primary.
- **Overloaded replicas.** A replica starved for CPU or disk replays slowly.

**Synchronous replication** makes commits wait for a standby. The level matters. With `synchronous_commit = remote_apply`, the commit waits until the standby has *applied* the change, which gives read-your-writes on that standby. Every commit then pays the network round trip plus replay, and writes stall if no synchronous standby is available. The lower levels (`on`, `remote_write`) protect durability but don't make the change visible on the replica.

Measure lag in two ways. On the primary, `pg_stat_replication` shows each replica's WAL positions and `replay_lag`. On a replica, `now() - pg_last_xact_replay_timestamp()` looks convenient, but it keeps growing whenever the primary is idle, so alert on WAL-position difference or on a heartbeat row the primary updates every second.

### Where routing happens

- **In the application.** Use two connection sources and choose per transaction, as in the Implementation below. This gives the most control and takes the most code.
- **In the driver.** pgJDBC accepts several hosts with `targetServerType=preferSecondary`, and MySQL Connector/J has replication connections that route when a connection is marked read-only. This means less code, but the driver can't know which reads need fresh data.
- **In a proxy.** ProxySQL (MySQL) and Pgpool-II (Postgres) can split reads from writes by inspecting SQL. It's transparent, but it's a guess: a `SELECT` that calls a function with side effects fools it.
- **Managed reader endpoints.** Many cloud databases offer one DNS name that spreads connections across replicas. It balances per connection, not per query, which is 23.1b's lesson again.

### Getting the guarantees back

There are four ways to restore **read-your-writes**, from simplest to most exact:

1. **Stick to the primary after a write.** When a user writes, send that user's reads to the primary for a window, such as 10 seconds, tracked with a timestamp cookie. It's simple, but the window must exceed the worst lag, and the 2 a.m. import reached 40 seconds.
2. **Always read "my" data from the primary.** Queries about the user's own things (my applications, my listings) go to the primary, and everything else goes to replicas. This works when those reads are a small share of the load.
3. **Carry a position token.** After a write, store the primary's WAL position (`pg_current_wal_lsn()`) in the session. Before reading from a replica, check that its `pg_last_wal_replay_lsn()` has reached that position; if not, wait briefly or use the primary. It's exact, and a lag spike degrades to reading from the primary rather than to wrong answers. MySQL supports the same idea with GTIDs and `WAIT_FOR_EXECUTED_GTID_SET`.
4. **Apply synchronously** with `remote_apply`. The database guarantees it, and every write pays for it.

There are two ways to restore **monotonic reads**:

- **Pin each user to one replica,** for example by hashing the user ID, so they only ever see one replica's timeline. This breaks when that replica is removed.
- **Remember the newest position the user has seen,** and only read from replicas at least that fresh. This is the position token again.

One rule has no exceptions: **never check-then-insert against a replica.** "Has this candidate already applied?" answers no during lag. The duplicate application needs a unique constraint on the primary no matter where reads go (6.5).

### Classify reads by freshness

The routing decision is really a question about each read: how stale may it be?

| Read | Acceptable staleness | Send to |
|---|---|---|
| Job search results | seconds | replica |
| Listing page, anonymous visitor | seconds | replica |
| Listing page, its employer right after an edit | none | primary or token |
| "My applications" right after applying | none | primary or token |
| Applicant count on a listing | seconds, but never backward | pinned replica or token |
| "Already applied?" before an insert | none, ever | primary, plus a unique constraint |
| Weekly analytics | minutes | dedicated reporting replica |

A dedicated reporting replica keeps long queries away from user-facing reads. It can tolerate a long `max_standby_streaming_delay`, since lag there only delays reports. Enabling `hot_standby_feedback` on it instead would move the cost to bloat on the primary.

### Replicas and failover

The same replicas often double as failover targets. Promoting an asynchronous replica can lose the last transactions the primary committed but hadn't yet shipped, and every routing layer above has to follow the promotion. 24.2 covers this.

## When It's Appropriate

- **An external pooler** when instances × pool size runs into the hundreds, when the app tier autoscales, or when serverless functions each open their own connection. The job board's 30 × 20 = 600 connections against a 16-core database is the case.
- **Read replicas** when reads dominate the primary's load and most of those reads can be a few seconds stale. The job board qualifies: 94% reads, primary at 80% CPU, and search and listing pages that are fine with slightly old data.
- **A dedicated reporting replica** when long analytical queries slow user-facing reads or keep getting canceled.
- **Position tokens** when user-facing reads often follow writes and lag spikes are real, as with bulk imports and batch jobs. A stick-to-primary window is enough when lag is reliably small and each user writes rarely.
- **Synchronous apply** when a small set of writes must be visible on a specific replica immediately and the write rate is low enough to absorb the added latency.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **The primary has room.** At 30% CPU, a replica adds routing work and a whole class of bugs for nothing.
- **A few bad queries cause the load.** A missing index or an N+1 pattern (6.4, 22.3) is cheaper to fix than to replicate.
- **The workload is write-heavy.** Replicas don't take writes, and they add WAL shipping work to the primary.
- **Most reads need fresh data.** If nearly every read follows the user's own write, most traffic ends up on the primary anyway.
- **The fleet is small and fixed.** Four instances with pools of 10 open 40 connections. An external pooler would add a hop and break session features for nothing.
- **The real goal is serving the same hot results fast.** A cache (Module 13, 23.3) is usually cheaper than another full copy of the database.

## Trade-offs

**Poolers cost:**

- **Another hop and another component to make redundant.** If the pooler is down, so is the database, as far as the app can tell.
- **Lost session features, failing silently.** Transaction mode removes them without an error, as the lock that never released showed.
- **Harder tracing.** The database sees the pooler's connections, not which app instance sent a query. Tag queries, for example with SQL comments, to trace them back.
- **A new place to wait.** When 600 clients want 60 connections, requests queue at the pooler, and that wait has to fit inside request timeouts (22.2b).

**Replicas cost:**

- **A new class of bugs.** Stale reads depend on timing, rarely show up in development, and become common under load.
- **A freshness decision for every read,** plus routing code or configuration that every new query has to fit into.
- **Harder testing,** because development databases have no lag. Postgres's `recovery_min_apply_delay` creates a replica that deliberately trails by a set time, which makes these bugs reproducible on purpose.
- **A full copy per replica,** with its own compute and storage bill.
- **Tuning with no free option.** Long replica queries force a choice: cancel them, delay replay, or bloat the primary.
- **Mandatory lag monitoring and alerting.**
- **More complex failover,** as described above.

## Alternatives

- **Fix the queries first** (22.3). Indexes and fewer round trips often remove the pressure entirely.
- **Scale the primary up** (23.1a). While headroom remains, a bigger primary has no staleness bugs at all.
- **Cache hot reads** (Module 13, 23.3). A cache is stale too, but you decide exactly when and how.
- **Move search to a search engine** (Module 12). On a job board, search is often the largest single read load, and a dedicated engine takes it off the database. It's eventually consistent as well, so the freshness questions come with it.
- **Build read models shaped for specific queries,** such as materialized views or a CQRS read side (23.2b).
- **Partition the data** when the pressure is writes or total size rather than reads (23.2b, 38.2).

## How to Achieve It

**1. Find out what the primary spends its time on.** `pg_stat_statements`, sorted by total time, shows whether reads dominate and whether a few queries account for most of it. Fix those few first. *Move on when* the load is broad read traffic, not a handful of queries.

**2. Write down the connection budget:** instances × pool size, at the autoscaler's maximum. Add an external pooler when that number approaches what the database runs well with. Before switching to transaction mode, search the code for advisory locks, `SET`, `LISTEN` and temporary tables. *Move on when* scaling the app tier no longer changes the database's connection count.

**3. Add one replica, and route only reads that are clearly stale-tolerant and not user-specific:** search, anonymous pages, reports. Alert on lag. *Move on when* the primary's load has dropped and lag has stayed low for a couple of weeks.

**4. Make lag reproducible.** Point a staging environment at a delayed replica and run every flow that writes and then reads.

**5. Classify the remaining reads** with the freshness table, and add read-your-writes for user-facing reads that follow writes. Start with a stick-to-primary window. *Move to position tokens when* lag spikes outgrow the window.

**6. Grow as read load grows.** Add replicas, pin users or carry tokens for monotonic reads, and give long reports their own replica.

**7. Stop when the primary's writes are the limit.** Replicas can't help any further, and that's the subject of 23.2b.

## Implementation

The code is Java with Spring, because routing reads by transaction type is idiomatic there. Services already mark read-only work with `@Transactional(readOnly = true)`, and Spring ships a data source that can route on that flag. The code also shows a trap that catches nearly everyone.

### Routing by transaction type

```java
final class ForcePrimary {
    private static final ThreadLocal<Boolean> FLAG = ThreadLocal.withInitial(() -> false);
    static boolean isActive() { return FLAG.get(); }
    static <T> T call(Supplier<T> work) {
        FLAG.set(true);
        try { return work.get(); } finally { FLAG.set(false); }
    }
}

class ReplicaRoutingDataSource extends AbstractRoutingDataSource {
    @Override
    protected Object determineCurrentLookupKey() {
        if (ForcePrimary.isActive()) return "primary";
        return TransactionSynchronizationManager.isCurrentTransactionReadOnly()
                ? "replica" : "primary";
    }
}

@Configuration
class DataSourceConfig {
    @Bean @Primary
    DataSource dataSource(@Qualifier("primaryPool") DataSource primary,
                          @Qualifier("replicaPool") DataSource replica) {
        var routing = new ReplicaRoutingDataSource();
        routing.setTargetDataSources(Map.of("primary", primary, "replica", replica));
        routing.setDefaultTargetDataSource(primary);
        routing.afterPropertiesSet();
        return new LazyConnectionDataSourceProxy(routing);   // the line everyone forgets
    }
}
```

**Where this breaks without the last line.** The transaction manager fetches a connection when the transaction *begins*, and Spring only marks the transaction read-only after that. Without `LazyConnectionDataSourceProxy`, the router always sees `readOnly == false`, and every query quietly goes to the primary. Nothing fails; the replica just sits idle. The proxy delays fetching the real connection until the first statement runs, and by then the flag is set. Newer Spring versions can also route read-only transactions inside `LazyConnectionDataSourceProxy` itself, so check your version's documentation. Either way, add a test asserting that `SELECT pg_is_in_recovery()` returns true inside a read-only transaction.

One more limit: `ForcePrimary` is a thread-local, so it doesn't follow work handed to another thread (`@Async`, reactive pipelines).

### Read-your-writes with a position token

```java
@Service
class ApplicationQueries {
    private final JdbcTemplate jdbc;                  // on the routing DataSource
    private final ApplicationRepository applications;

    ApplicationQueries(JdbcTemplate jdbc, ApplicationRepository applications) {
        this.jdbc = jdbc;
        this.applications = applications;
    }

    @Transactional(readOnly = true)
    public List<Application> forCandidate(long candidateId, String writeToken) {
        if (writeToken != null && !caughtUp(writeToken)) {
            throw new ReplicaBehindException();       // the caller retries on the primary
        }
        return applications.findByCandidateId(candidateId);
    }

    private boolean caughtUp(String token) {
        // NULL on a server that was never a replica, so treat NULL as caught up
        return Boolean.TRUE.equals(jdbc.queryForObject(
            "SELECT coalesce(pg_last_wal_replay_lsn() >= ?::pg_lsn, true)",
            Boolean.class, token));
    }
}
```

**Why the check sits inside the read transaction.** With the lazy proxy, the check is the transaction's first statement, so it decides which replica connection the whole transaction uses. A check made through a separate pool could pass on replica 1 while the query runs on replica 2, which is still behind.

```java
@PostMapping("/jobs/{jobId}/applications")
ResponseEntity<Void> apply(@PathVariable long jobId, HttpSession session) {
    commands.apply(currentCandidate(), jobId);            // read-write transaction, primary
    session.setAttribute("walToken", primaryJdbc.queryForObject(
        "SELECT pg_current_wal_lsn()::text", String.class)); // taken after commit
    return ResponseEntity.status(HttpStatus.SEE_OTHER)
            .location(URI.create("/me/applications")).build();
}

@GetMapping("/me/applications")
List<Application> mine(HttpSession session) {
    var token = (String) session.getAttribute("walToken");
    try {
        return queries.forCandidate(currentCandidate(), token);
    } catch (ReplicaBehindException e) {
        return ForcePrimary.call(() -> queries.forCandidate(currentCandidate(), null));
    }
}
```

**Notes on the write side.**

- **The token is read after the commit,** so it points at or past the application just written.
- **Where the token lives.** Here it's in the session, which lives in the shared store from 23.1a; a signed cookie works too.
- **The duplicate-application bug still needs its own fix.** A unique constraint on (candidate, job) on the primary is what stops duplicates. The token only stops the confusion that led the candidate to apply twice.

## Further Reading

- **Martin Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_,** chapter 5 (Replication), especially the section on problems with replication lag. It covers read-your-writes, monotonic reads and consistent prefix reads with clear diagrams.
- **Terry et al., "Session Guarantees for Weakly Consistent Replicated Data"** (PDIS 1994). The paper that named these guarantees and showed how to provide them with version tokens.
- **PostgreSQL documentation:** the high availability and replication chapter, especially "Hot Standby" and its section on query conflicts, plus the reference entries for `synchronous_commit`, `hot_standby_feedback` and `recovery_min_apply_delay`.
- **PgBouncer documentation:** the pooling-mode descriptions and the table of which Postgres features each mode supports. Read the table before switching to transaction mode.
- **HikariCP wiki, "About Pool Sizing."** Why a smaller pool is usually faster, with a starting formula.
- **Andres Freund, "Analyzing the Limits of Connection Scalability in Postgres"** (2020). What idle connections actually cost, from the engineer who reduced that cost.
- **Vlad Mihalcea, "Read-write and read-only transaction routing with Spring."** The routing pattern above, including the lazy-connection trap.
- **MySQL documentation: `WAIT_FOR_EXECUTED_GTID_SET`,** the MySQL form of the position token. Also the **pgJDBC documentation** on multi-host URLs and `targetServerType`.
- **In this collection:** 23.1a (why the connection count grew), 22.3 (query plans), 6.4 and 6.5 (N+1 queries and isolation), Module 13 and 23.3 (caching), Module 12 (search), 23.2b (read models and partitioning), 24.2 (failover).

A **TypeScript** sibling would be worth writing for serverless functions (Module 41). There, each concurrent invocation is its own process with its own connection, so an in-process pool can't help at all. A burst of 2,000 invocations means 2,000 connection attempts, and an external pooler or a managed data proxy stops being an optimization and becomes a requirement. It could also cover the HTTP-based database drivers some providers offer for this case, and what they give up. Nothing in a long-running Java service shows this. Not yet written.
