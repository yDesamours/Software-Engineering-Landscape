# Between the Rule and the Person

_Module 19.3b — Logging, Monitoring & Observability_

## The Problem

A payroll platform for small businesses. Six engineers share a weekly on-call rotation. Last month the alerting system sent 1,900 notifications, and 31 of them woke someone between midnight and 06:00. When the team reviewed those 31 afterward, 27 had needed no action at all.

Five moments from that month:

**03:40, three nights a week.** `HighCPU: api-7 above 85%`. The engineer wakes, opens a laptop, and finds nothing wrong for any user: a nightly report runs at that hour. By the third week she has set her phone to silence that alert name.

**Thursday, 02:14: the storm.** The primary database's disk fills up. In four minutes, 212 notifications arrive: an error-rate alert for every service, every synthetic check, a readiness failure for each of 60 pods (19.2a), and a 5xx alert per pod. She acknowledges them in bulk and starts scrolling.

The alert that names the cause, `PostgresDiskUsage above 95%`, is number 147. Its warning version, set at 80%, fired three weeks earlier into a chat channel with 4,000 unread messages. It takes 25 minutes to find the cause of an outage that was predictable.

**Friday: the silence.** On Tuesday, a configuration change disabled the weekly payroll export for 140 customers. No alert fired. The job hadn't failed; it had never started, and a job that never starts produces no errors. Customers noticed on Friday, the day their files were due at their banks.

**Sunday: the blindness.** The telemetry gateway (19.3a) was out of memory and down for 50 minutes. Dashboards showed flat lines. Every error-rate rule evaluated against no data, returned nothing, and stayed quiet. Nobody learned the monitoring had been blind until Monday.

**Saturday, 05:10: the acknowledgment.** A page arrives, and the on-call engineer acknowledges it from bed and falls back asleep. The acknowledgment stops the escalation to the secondary. Nothing happens for 90 minutes, until a customer phones support.

Each alert rule, taken on its own, was reasonable. CPU does matter, disks do fill up, and error rates are worth watching. The failures were all in what sits *between* the rules and the people: what earns a page, how related alerts combine, what happens when the signal is silence, and what happens after someone says "got it."

## What It Is

This is **alerting**: automatically detecting conditions that need a person's attention, and delivering each one to the right person with the right urgency.

It has two halves:

- **Alert rules** evaluate conditions over metrics, logs, or traces, including the SLO burn rates from 16.3. Prometheus rules, Grafana Alerting, and every vendor backend provide these.
- **Alert management** takes the alerts those rules produce and decides who hears about them, how, and how many times. That covers grouping, deduplication, suppression, routing, on-call schedules, and escalation. Prometheus Alertmanager handles the first part, and on-call platforms such as PagerDuty, incident.io, and Splunk On-Call handle schedules and escalation.

(If you use Opsgenie, note that Atlassian stopped selling it on June 4, 2025, ends support on April 5, 2027, and points customers to Jira Service Management and Compass.)

Two distinctions matter throughout.

**An alert is not an incident.** An alert is a signal. An incident is a declared state with a response process around it (Module 24.3). One incident usually produces many alerts, and handling that many-to-one relationship is half this article.

**Urgency comes in three levels**, following the Google SRE book's framing:

| Level | Meaning | Delivered as |
|---|---|---|
| **Page** | A person must act within minutes, at any hour | Push notification, then SMS, then a phone call, with escalation |
| **Ticket** | A person must act within days, during working hours | A ticket in the team's queue |
| **Record** | Nobody needs to act; useful context later | A dashboard or log, not a notification |

Most alerting problems come from putting a signal in the wrong row.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Pages resolve themselves before anyone does anything.
- On-call engineers acknowledge alerts in bulk during incidents.
- The alert that names the cause is buried among dozens of symptom alerts.
- Alerts reach the wrong team, or a default team, because a label is missing.
- Alert channels are muted, or notifications are filtered into an unread folder.
- Customers discover failures that produced no alert: a job that didn't run, data that stopped arriving.
- An alert fires and resolves every few minutes, over and over.
- Responders ask "what does this alert mean?"
- Nobody knows how many times the on-call engineer was paged last week.
- Alert rules exist for services that no longer exist.
- An acknowledged page sat untouched for an hour.
- Your alerting runs in the same cluster or region as the systems it watches.

## How It Works

### The path from rule to person

```
signals ──► rule evaluation ──► alert ─────────► alert manager ─────────► on-call platform ──► person
            (every 30–60s;      labels +         group · deduplicate ·     schedule · escalate ·
             `for` duration)    annotations;     suppress · silence ·      acknowledge · notify
                                firing or        route by label            (push → SMS → call)
                                resolved
```

Each stage has its own failure modes. The Problem's storm happened at the grouping and suppression stage, the unwatched acknowledgment at the escalation stage, and the silent Sunday before evaluation even began.

### What deserves a page

A page is justified when the condition meets all four tests:

- **Urgent.** Waiting until morning makes things measurably worse for users, data, or money.
- **Actionable.** There is something a person can do.
- **Real.** It isn't a transient blip that clears on its own.
- **New.** Nobody is already handling it.

A signal that fails any one of the four becomes a ticket or a dashboard panel. `HighCPU` on api-7 failed "urgent" and "real," and three weeks of pages taught the on-call engineer to ignore it.

The main design rule follows from those tests: **page on symptoms, not causes.** Symptoms are what users experience: the SLO burn rates from 16.3, a failing synthetic journey, the age of the oldest message in a user-facing queue. Causes (CPU, memory, a pod restarting, one replica down) are useful for diagnosis and belong on dashboards and in tickets. Paging on causes pages you for things that don't hurt anyone, and a system with good redundancy has many of those every day.

There's one important exception: **causes that predict a symptom with enough lead time that waiting guarantees an outage.** A disk that will be full in four hours, a TLS certificate that expires in five days, an API quota that will run out before its reset. These should be written as *projections* ("full within four hours at the current rate"), not as static thresholds ("above 80%"). A projection fires when it's urgent, and stays quiet on a disk that has sat at 81% for a year. Thursday's outage was a projection that nobody wrote.

Some pages only need to reach people during working hours. An internal tool used between 9 and 5 can page during the day and open a ticket at night.

### Alerts are data: labels and annotations

Every alert carries two kinds of metadata, and both deserve care.

**Labels identify and route** the alert: `alertname`, `service`, `team`, `severity`, `environment`, `cluster`. Routing, grouping, suppression, and silences all match on labels. An alert with no `team` label falls through to a default route, which is how pages reach the wrong people.

**Annotations inform** the person who receives it: a summary, a description that includes the current value, a `runbook_url`, and links to the relevant dashboard and to a log or trace query already filtered to the right service.

A good alert text answers four questions without anyone clicking anything:

```
Bad:    PostgresDiskUsage firing

Good:   [PAGE] payroll-db-1: disk full in ~3h at current growth (now 91%, +2.1%/h)
        Impact: writes fail when full; payroll runs and logins stop.
        First look: dashboard ▸ Postgres storage · runbook ▸ disk-full
```

What's wrong, how bad it is, how soon it matters, and where to look first. Enforce the required labels and annotations mechanically: a CI check that rejects any rule without `team`, `severity`, and `runbook_url` costs an afternoon to write.

### Grouping, deduplication, suppression, and silences

These four mechanisms turn a storm into a handful of useful notifications.

**Deduplication** collapses identical alerts. Two Prometheus replicas running the same rules for redundancy produce one notification, not two, and an alert that stays firing isn't re-sent until its repeat interval passes.

**Grouping** bundles related alerts into one notification, using the labels you choose to group by. Grouped by `cluster` and `alertname`, 60 pod-level readiness alerts become one notification that lists 60 pods. Three timers control grouping: how long to wait for more alerts before sending the first notification (`group_wait`, 30 seconds by default in Alertmanager), how often to send updates as new alerts join the group (`group_interval`, 5 minutes), and how often to repeat a notification that's still firing (`repeat_interval`, 4 hours).

**Inhibition** (suppression) mutes some alerts while another is firing. If `PostgresPrimaryDown` is firing in a cluster, the error-rate alerts of services that depend on that database, in the same cluster, are muted. The person receives the cause, not 200 consequences of it.

Inhibition hides things, so use it carefully. The muting alert must be reliable, the rule should be as narrow as possible, and it must not hide an unrelated second problem that happens to start at the same time.

**Silences** mute matching alerts for a set period, for planned maintenance or a known issue. Every silence should have an author, a reason, and an expiry. A silence that never expires is a deleted alert that nobody reviewed.

Applied to Thursday's storm:

```
without:  212 notifications in 4 minutes, cause at position 147

with:     PostgresDiskWillFill      page    (predictive; would have fired the previous afternoon)
          PostgresPrimaryDown       page    (the cause)
          ├─ inhibits: 14 service error-rate alerts in cluster payroll-prod
          ├─ inhibits: 6 synthetic journey failures in payroll-prod
          grouped:  60 pod readiness alerts → 1 ticket-level notification
```

### Flapping and timing

An alert that fires and resolves every few minutes sends a notification each time, and it trains people to ignore it faster than any other kind.

- **A `for` duration** requires the condition to hold continuously for N minutes before the alert fires. It's the simplest way to reduce noise, and every minute of it is a minute of extra delay on real problems.
- **Holding the firing state** after the condition clears (Prometheus's `keep_firing_for`) stops a value that hovers around its threshold from generating a notification every scrape.
- **Longer evaluation windows** smooth out noise and react more slowly. The multi-window burn-rate alerts from 16.3 exist to resolve exactly this tension for SLOs: a short window for speed, a long window for confirmation.
- **An alert can't be faster than its data.** With a one-minute scrape interval, a one-minute evaluation interval, and a five-minute `for` duration, detection takes at least seven minutes, before the notification pipeline adds its own delay.

### Alerting on absence

Most alerting assumes that failure produces a signal. Three kinds of failure produce silence instead, and each needs its own mechanism.

**The data is missing.** A rule like `error_rate > 0.05` over a series that doesn't exist returns *nothing*, and nothing doesn't fire. That's the Sunday blindness. For series that must always exist, add an explicit absence check (Prometheus's `absent()` and `absent_over_time()`). In Grafana Alerting, set what a rule does when it gets no data, rather than accepting the default. Treat "no data" as a state you've decided how to handle, not as "OK."

**The work didn't happen.** A job that never runs produces no errors, which was Friday's problem. Alert on *time since the last success* instead. The job records a timestamp when it succeeds (for batch jobs in Prometheus, usually pushed through the Pushgateway), and a rule fires when that timestamp gets too old. Hosted cron monitors such as Healthchecks.io and Cronitor work the same way: the job pings a URL when it finishes, and the service alerts when a ping is late.

**The monitoring itself is down.** If the rule evaluator, the alert manager, or the pipeline in front of them fails, every alert goes silent at once. The standard defense is a **dead man's switch**: a rule that is *always* firing (kube-prometheus ships one called `Watchdog`), routed to an external service that pages you when those notifications *stop* arriving. It inverts the usual logic, so silence becomes the alarm.

```
normal:   Watchdog ──► Alertmanager ──► heartbeat service   "heard from you 40s ago" ✓
broken:   pipeline down ─╳ no evaluations ─╳ no heartbeat ──► heartbeat service   "silent for 10m" ──► page
```

That external service must not share fate with what it watches: a different region, and ideally a different provider (19.3a).

### Routing, schedules, and escalation

**Routing** matches labels against a tree of rules: by `severity` first, then by `team`, sometimes by `service` or `environment`. The default route at the top of the tree catches everything with missing or unexpected labels. Send it to someone whose job is to fix the labels, and count what arrives there, because a steady trickle means some rules are misconfigured.

**On-call schedules** define who is responsible and when. They include weekly rotations, follow-the-sun handoffs between time zones, a secondary for every primary, overrides for holidays, and a written handoff at each shift change listing open issues and anything unusual.

**Escalation policies** define what happens when nobody responds: notify the primary, and if they haven't acknowledged within N minutes, notify the secondary, then a manager. Channels escalate too, from push notification to SMS to a phone call.

**Acknowledgment stops escalation, and that's where Saturday went wrong.** An acknowledgment means "I've got this," but nothing verifies that anyone is actually working on it. On-call platforms offer an acknowledgment timeout that re-triggers the page if it hasn't been resolved or updated within a set time. Turn it on.

Chat channels are not a paging mechanism. People mute them, and they should be able to.

**On-call is a human system with capacity limits.** The SRE book suggests a ceiling of about two incidents per 12-hour shift, because each one takes hours including follow-up. The Problem's team was well past that before any real incidents. Track the load, pay people for it, and protect time after a bad night.

### Runbooks

Every page links to a runbook, and a runbook answers specific questions:

- What does this alert mean, and what is the user impact?
- What should I check first? (Links, not descriptions.)
- How do I mitigate it? (Steps, not "investigate.")
- Who do I escalate to, and when?
- What false positives are known?

Keep runbooks next to the rules, in the same repository, so they change together. Watch for one pattern: a runbook whose steps are fully mechanical, the same every time, describes a job for automation, not for a person woken at 03:00. Automate it, and alert when the automation fails or runs unusually often.

### Measuring alert quality

Alerting decays unless someone measures it. Track these per rule:

- how many pages it sent;
- the share that needed action, with responders tagging each page as actionable, not actionable, or a duplicate;
- the share that resolved before anyone acknowledged it;
- how many fired at night;
- time to acknowledge.

Review the numbers weekly, and demote or delete rules that fall short. A useful standing rule: **every page ends with either a fix or a change to the alert.**

Treat rules as code: version-controlled, reviewed, unit-tested (Prometheus's `promtool` can test rules against synthetic series), and deployed by CI. Give each rule an owner, and periodically check that it's still needed.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Anything with users outside working hours,** or with a contract that promises availability.
- **Teams with a real on-call rotation.** Once more than one person carries the pager, routing, escalation, and handoffs stop being optional.
- **Many services or many teams.** Label-based routing is what gets a page to its owner.
- **Scheduled work with deadlines.** Payroll files, billing runs, reports, and backups all need absence alerts, because their typical failure is silence.
- **A history of alert storms.** Grouping and suppression turn an unreadable incident into a readable one.
- **Monitoring you depend on.** Once people trust "no alerts" to mean "no problems," you need a dead man's switch.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A paging platform for a side project.** Email or chat is enough. If the project matters, a free external heartbeat check is still worth having.
- **Multi-level escalation for a two-person team.** A primary and a secondary is the whole policy.
- **A complex suppression tree before grouping is in place.** Grouping handles most storms with far less risk of hiding something.
- **24/7 paging for tools that are only used during working hours.**
- **"Just in case" tickets.** A queue of tickets nobody reads is a muted channel with extra steps.
- **Building your own alert manager or on-call scheduler.** Mature open-source and commercial tools exist, and the hard parts (deduplication, reliable notification delivery, escalation timers) are exactly what they've already solved.

## Trade-offs

- **Fewer false pages means some real problems are detected later.** Every `for` duration, longer window, and demotion to ticket trades detection speed for sleep.
- **Grouping and suppression can hide a second problem.** An unrelated failure that starts during an outage may be muted along with the expected consequences.
- **Paging on symptoms means diagnosis starts from scratch.** The page says users are hurting, not why. That only works if dashboards, traces (19.2b), and runbooks get the responder to the cause quickly.
- **Predictive alerts are approximate.** A linear projection is wrong for bursty growth, and tuning its window takes iteration.
- **Absence alerts need maintenance.** Retiring a job or a series on purpose makes its absence alert fire, unless the alert is retired at the same time.
- **On-call has a human cost.** Burnout and attrition are the real price of noisy alerting, and they're paid long after the alert is fixed.
- **External heartbeat services add a dependency,** and that dependency has to be more reliable than what it watches.
- **On-call platforms are priced per user** and hold your schedules and escalation configuration, which makes them slow to switch away from.
- **Routing trees fail quietly.** A reordered route or a changed label sends pages to the wrong place without any error. Test them (Alertmanager's `amtool` can check which route an alert would take) and watch the default route.

## Alternatives

- **Paging only on SLO burn rates** (16.3), plus a few predictive causes. Many teams converge on this, since it matches pages to user impact by construction.
- **Anomaly detection.** Statistical or machine-learning models that learn what's normal and alert on deviations. They catch problems nobody wrote a threshold for, and they're often noisy, hard to explain, and confused by seasonal patterns.
- **Synthetic monitoring as the main pager** (16.3). A scripted user journey that fails is a clear, symptom-level signal, though it covers only the journeys you scripted.
- **Event-correlation platforms.** Tools that group alerts from many sources into incidents automatically. Useful when alerts come from many tools, at the cost of opacity and another vendor.
- **Customer reports as the signal.** Honest at small scale, and too slow once customers depend on you.
- **Automated remediation instead of pages.** Restart, scale out, or fail over automatically, and alert only when the automation fails or runs too often.
- **Incident management** (Module 24.3). What happens after the page: declaring an incident, coordinating the response, and learning from it afterward.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — measure the current state.** Pages per week, the share that were actionable, pages at night, and rules without an owner.
_Signal you're here:_ nobody knows those numbers.

**Level 1 — three severities, and page on symptoms.** Move cause alerts to tickets and dashboards, and require `team`, `severity`, and `runbook_url` on every rule, enforced in CI.
_Signal you need it:_ pages that resolve themselves, or silenced alert names on people's phones.

**Level 2 — grouping, deduplication, and targeted suppression.** Add `for` durations and a routing tree with a monitored default route.
_Signal you need it:_ an alert storm, or a page that reached the wrong team.

**Level 3 — alerts on absence.** An external dead man's switch, absence checks for critical series, and last-success alerts for every scheduled job.
_Signal you need it:_ a silent failure that a customer found, or an outage of the monitoring itself.

**Level 4 — escalation and support for people.** Escalation with acknowledgment timeouts, secondaries, written handoffs, a runbook for every page, and a weekly alert review.
_Signal you need it:_ a page that was acknowledged and then forgotten, or responders asking what an alert means.

**Level 5 — alerting as tested, measured code.** Unit-tested rules, per-rule quality metrics, predictive alerts for capacity, certificates, and quotas, and automation in place of mechanical runbooks.
_Signal you need it:_ on-call load that's stable but still above target, or the same manual fix applied every week.

## Implementation

**Prometheus rules and Alertmanager configuration, in YAML with PromQL.** As in 19.3a, the implementation of this layer is configuration. The SLO burn-rate recording rules from 16.3 are assumed to exist.

The rules: one symptom page, one predictive page, a demoted cause, the two absence checks for the payroll export, and the watchdog.

```yaml
# payroll.rules.yml
groups:
  - name: payroll
    rules:
      - alert: PayrollApiFastBurn                  # symptom: users are failing right now (16.3)
        expr: slo:payroll_api_errors:burnrate1h > 14.4 and slo:payroll_api_errors:burnrate5m > 14.4
        labels: { severity: page, team: payroll }
        annotations:
          summary: "Payroll API is burning its error budget ~14x too fast"
          runbook_url: https://runbooks.internal/payroll/api-fast-burn

      - alert: PayrollExportOverdue                # the work didn't happen
        expr: time() - payroll_export_last_success_timestamp_seconds > 26 * 3600
        labels: { severity: page, team: payroll }
        annotations:
          summary: "Payroll export has not succeeded in over 26 hours"
          runbook_url: https://runbooks.internal/payroll/export-overdue

      - alert: PayrollExportMetricMissing          # without this, the rule above can never fire
        expr: absent(payroll_export_last_success_timestamp_seconds)
        for: 30m
        labels: { severity: page, team: payroll }
        annotations:
          summary: "No payroll export success metric exists at all"
          runbook_url: https://runbooks.internal/payroll/export-metric-missing

  - name: platform
    rules:
      - alert: PostgresDiskWillFill                # a cause worth paging on: it predicts an outage
        expr: |
          predict_linear(node_filesystem_avail_bytes{mountpoint="/var/lib/postgresql"}[6h], 4 * 3600) < 0
          and
          node_filesystem_avail_bytes{mountpoint="/var/lib/postgresql"}
            / node_filesystem_size_bytes{mountpoint="/var/lib/postgresql"} < 0.25
        for: 15m
        labels: { severity: page, team: platform }
        annotations:
          summary: "{{ $labels.instance }}: database disk projected to fill within 4 hours"
          runbook_url: https://runbooks.internal/platform/disk-will-fill

      - alert: HighCPU                             # demoted: a cause, not a symptom
        expr: 1 - avg by (instance) (rate(node_cpu_seconds_total{mode="idle"}[10m])) > 0.9
        for: 30m
        labels: { severity: ticket, team: platform }
        annotations:
          summary: "{{ $labels.instance }} above 90% CPU for 30 minutes"
          runbook_url: https://runbooks.internal/platform/high-cpu

      - alert: Watchdog                            # always firing; its silence is the alarm
        expr: vector(1)
        labels: { severity: heartbeat, team: platform }
        annotations:
          summary: "Always firing. If the heartbeat service stops hearing this, alerting is broken."
```

> **Aside — the second condition on the disk rule matters.** `predict_linear` alone fires on any disk that's briefly filling fast, including a nearly empty one during a large import. Requiring less than 25% free as well keeps the projection for disks where running out is actually plausible. Two conditions joined with `and` also need matching labels on both sides, which holds here because both series come from the same exporter.

The Alertmanager configuration: routing, grouping, suppression, and the heartbeat.

```yaml
route:
  receiver: unrouted                  # missing or unexpected labels land here, and get fixed
  group_by: [alertname, cluster, service]
  group_wait: 30s
  group_interval: 5m
  repeat_interval: 4h
  routes:
    - matchers: [alertname = Watchdog]
      receiver: heartbeat
      group_wait: 0s
      group_interval: 1m
      repeat_interval: 1m             # the external service pages if it hears nothing for ~10 minutes
    - matchers: [severity = page]
      receiver: pager-default         # a page with an unknown team still wakes someone
      routes:
        - { matchers: [team = payroll],  receiver: pager-payroll }
        - { matchers: [team = platform], receiver: pager-platform }
    - matchers: [severity = ticket]
      receiver: tickets

inhibit_rules:
  - source_matchers: [alertname = PostgresPrimaryDown]
    target_matchers: [depends_on = postgres]   # symptom rules opt in with this label
    equal: [cluster]                           # only within the same cluster

receivers:
  - name: pager-payroll
    pagerduty_configs: [{ routing_key: "<payroll integration key>" }]
  - name: pager-platform
    pagerduty_configs: [{ routing_key: "<platform integration key>" }]
  - name: pager-default
    pagerduty_configs: [{ routing_key: "<primary on-call integration key>" }]
  - name: tickets
    webhook_configs: [{ url: "https://tickets.internal/hooks/alertmanager" }]
  - name: heartbeat
    webhook_configs: [{ url: "https://heartbeat.other-provider.example/ping/<id>" }]
  - name: unrouted
    webhook_configs: [{ url: "https://tickets.internal/hooks/unrouted-alerts" }]
```

> **Aside — suppression is opt-in, deliberately.** An inhibition rule that muted every `severity=page` alert in the cluster whenever the database was down would also mute an unrelated certificate expiry or a failing payment integration. Requiring symptom rules to declare `depends_on: postgres` keeps suppression exactly as wide as the dependency. The cost is one more label that someone must remember to add.

Finally, a unit test that would have caught Friday's gap before it shipped. The job succeeds once at time zero, then never again:

```yaml
# payroll.rules.test.yml   (run with: promtool test rules payroll.rules.test.yml)
rule_files: [payroll.rules.yml]
evaluation_interval: 1m

tests:
  - interval: 1h
    input_series:
      - series: payroll_export_last_success_timestamp_seconds{job="payroll-export"}
        values: "0x30"                  # the last success was at t=0, and it never updates
    alert_rule_test:
      - eval_time: 25h                  # a late run is still within tolerance
        alertname: PayrollExportOverdue
        exp_alerts: []
      - eval_time: 27h                  # overdue: this must page
        alertname: PayrollExportOverdue
        exp_alerts:
          - exp_labels: { severity: page, team: payroll, job: payroll-export }
            exp_annotations:
              summary: "Payroll export has not succeeded in over 26 hours"
              runbook_url: https://runbooks.internal/payroll/export-overdue
      - eval_time: 27h                  # the metric exists, so the absence rule stays quiet
        alertname: PayrollExportMetricMissing
        exp_alerts: []
```

> **Aside — test the rule you're afraid of, not the rule that's easy.** Tests for "error rate above threshold fires" rarely catch anything, because such rules fail loudly when wrong. The rules worth testing are the ones whose failure is silence: absence checks, last-success checks, and anything with `and` or `unless`, where a label mismatch quietly produces an empty result forever.

## Further Reading

- **_Site Reliability Engineering_ (Google),** chapter 6, "Monitoring Distributed Systems," and chapter 11, "Being On-Call." The page, ticket, and record distinction, symptom-based alerting, and the limits on on-call load.
- **_The Site Reliability Workbook_ (Google),** chapter 5, "Alerting on SLOs," and chapter 8, "On-Call." The burn-rate alerting that 16.3 introduced, worked through in full, and practical on-call design.
- **Rob Ewaschuk, "My Philosophy on Alerting."** A short essay that shaped most modern thinking on pages: urgent, actionable, and symptom-based.
- **Alex Hidalgo, _Implementing Service Level Objectives_** (O'Reilly). How SLO-based alerting fits into an organization, beyond the arithmetic.
- **Prometheus documentation:** alerting rules (`for` and `keep_firing_for`), the `absent`, `absent_over_time`, and `predict_linear` functions, `promtool test rules`, and the alerting best-practices page.
- **Alertmanager documentation:** routing, grouping, inhibition, silences, and `amtool` for testing routes.
- **Prometheus Operator runbooks** (runbooks.prometheus-operator.dev). Real runbooks for the alerts kube-prometheus ships, including `Watchdog`. Good models for structure.
- **PagerDuty Incident Response documentation** (response.pagerduty.com). An openly published guide to on-call and response roles, useful whichever tool you use.
- **Healthchecks.io documentation.** A clear explanation of heartbeat-style monitoring for scheduled jobs.
- **Atlassian's Opsgenie end-of-sale and end-of-support notice,** if you're choosing or migrating an on-call platform.

---

The habit to carry out of this: for any automated signal meant to reach a person, ask three questions. What should that person do in the next ten minutes? What will they receive if twenty related things break at once? And how would anyone know if this signal stopped arriving? An alert that can't answer all three isn't finished, however correct its expression is.
