# Taking the Network Out of Your Code: The Service Mesh

_Module 36.1a — Advanced Microservices Patterns_

## The Problem

This continues the property-management platform from 36.1b, 36.2 and 36.3, a year later. It now runs 14 services on about 240 pods, in three languages: Go (`payments`, `leases` and four others), TypeScript (the two BFFs, `messaging`, `documents` and two more), and Java (`accounts` and a reporting service, which came with an acquisition).

The team has absorbed this module's lessons, and every one of them was a library decision:

- **36.3a:** explicit timeouts, deadlines and bulkheads on every outbound call.
- **36.3b:** circuit breakers with explicit failure classification.
- **36.1b:** workload identity, mTLS and per-endpoint caller allowlists.
- **Module 19:** consistent metrics and trace propagation on every hop.

Each has now been implemented three times, once per language, by different people, in different months. Three problems follow.

**Three languages, three retry policies.** When `accounts` slowed down one afternoon, its traffic tripled instead of falling. The Go services retried once, as agreed in 18.3. The Java services used a Resilience4j default of three attempts. One TypeScript service used an HTTP library whose retry plugin also retried three times by default, including on `POST`, and the BFF above it retried too. Nobody had chosen any of those numbers except the Go team's.

**Security that stopped at the language boundary.** mTLS with SPIFFE identities was straightforward in Go (`go-spiffe`) and workable in Java. There was no maintained SPIFFE library the TypeScript team trusted, so `messaging` and `documents` stayed on per-service API keys "for now." Eight months later they still were. `documents` is the service with the SSRF history from 36.1b. The platform's weakest link was wherever the libraries were thinnest.

**Metrics that couldn't be compared.** The dashboard's "success rate per service" was built from three client libraries' metrics. Go counted a `404` as a success, Java counted it as an error, and one TypeScript library didn't record timeouts at all, because the request never completed. During incidents, engineers argued over whose numbers to believe before they started working on the problem.

None of these are design failures. The designs were right. The trouble was that **policy about the network was compiled into every application**, so changing a retry count, adding mTLS or fixing a metric meant a code change and a deploy in 14 services, three languages and several teams.

So the team introduces a service mesh. The first month brings three new surprises of its own:

- **Retries doubled again.** The first mesh configuration copied a retry block from an example, `retryOn: 5xx,reset`, and applied it to every route of every service. Nobody turned off the applications' own retries. Worse, the mesh now retried after connection resets, which can happen *after* the upstream processed a request, including `POST /autopay`.
- **Traces still didn't join.** The mesh's proxies emitted a span for every hop, and the spans didn't connect into traces, because the applications weren't forwarding `traceparent` from their inbound request to their outbound calls. That was 19.2b's warning, now visible at scale.
- **Jobs that never finished, and pods that failed at startup.** The nightly reconciliation job finished its work, but its pod never completed, because the proxy container kept running. Some services failed their first requests after starting, because they started faster than their proxy.

A mesh moves real work out of applications. It can't move all of it. Knowing which part is which is what this article is about.

## What It Is

A **service mesh** is infrastructure that handles communication between services. It's made of a proxy next to (or near) each workload, which carries all of its network traffic, and a control plane that configures those proxies. Applications make plain calls to other services. The proxies add encryption, identity, retries, timeouts, load balancing and telemetry, all driven by configuration the platform team can change without redeploying applications.

The two halves:

- **The data plane:** the proxies that carry traffic. Most meshes use Envoy. Linkerd uses its own lightweight Rust proxy.
- **The control plane:** the component that tells proxies where services are (service discovery), what policy to apply, and which certificates to present. Istio's is `istiod`.

The main products are **Istio**, **Linkerd** and **Cilium Service Mesh**, plus managed versions from cloud providers. They differ in where the proxy runs, which the next section covers, and in how much they try to do.

Where it sits relative to the rest of this module:

- The **gateway** (36.2a) handles traffic entering the system (north-south). The mesh handles traffic between services (east-west). Many meshes also provide an ingress gateway, which blurs the line.
- A mesh implements the **first half of 36.1b**, workload identity, mTLS and caller authorization, without application code. The second half, acting for a user, stays in the application.
- It implements **parts of 36.3a and 36.3b**: timeouts, retries, per-host ejection and connection limits. It can't decide what a dependency's failure means for a particular endpoint.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Timeout, retry and breaker settings differ between services for reasons nobody can explain.
- A policy change such as "retry once, not three times" means changing and redeploying many services.
- mTLS, or any cross-cutting security control, is done in some languages and "planned" in others.
- Metrics from different services can't be compared, because each library defines success differently.
- Canary releases between internal services need code, or are impossible.
- There's no way to inject latency or errors between services for a drill (36.3a Level 4).
- The opposite, after adopting a mesh: nobody can say whether retries happen in the app, the mesh or both; traces are fragmented; jobs don't complete; startup is flaky.

## How It Works

### How traffic reaches the proxy

The application calls `http://payments/…` as usual. The mesh captures that traffic transparently:

```
sidecar mode                                   ambient / node-proxy mode

┌─ pod: leases ──────────────┐                 ┌─ node ──────────────────────────────┐
│ app ──► iptables ──► Envoy │══ mTLS ══►      │ pod: leases   pod: messaging        │
└────────────────────────────┘                 │   app            app                │
                  ┌─ pod: payments ────────┐   │    └──► ztunnel (per node) ◄──┘      │══ mTLS ══►
                  │ Envoy ──► app          │   │         L4: identity, mTLS, L4 policy│
                  └────────────────────────┘   │    L7 (retries, routes) → waypoint   │
                                               └──────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The deployment models:

- **Sidecar.** A proxy container in every pod, with traffic redirected to it by iptables rules or eBPF. It's the most mature and gives full L7 features per pod. It costs a proxy's memory and CPU on every pod, which adds up across 240 pods, and a hop through two proxies on every call, typically a millisecond or two.
- **Ambient (sidecarless).** Istio's ambient mode runs one small L4 proxy per node (`ztunnel`), which handles mTLS, identity and L4 policy, and deploys full L7 proxies (waypoints) only for services that need L7 features such as retries or path-based policy. Cilium does similar work with eBPF in the kernel plus a per-node Envoy. It's cheaper and doesn't touch application pods, and it's newer, with a different set of edge cases.
- **Proxyless.** gRPC libraries can speak the mesh's configuration protocol (xDS) directly, so the application gets mesh policy with no proxy in the path. It's fast, but it only works for gRPC and puts the library back inside the application.
- **No mesh.** Shared libraries per language, which is where the platform started. It's a reasonable choice with one or two languages and a handful of services.

### Identity and mTLS, without application code

The mesh's control plane is a certificate authority. Each proxy receives a SPIFFE identity for its workload, derived from the Kubernetes service account, and rotates it automatically. Proxies use mTLS with each other. The application talks plaintext to its local proxy and never handles a certificate. This is 36.1b's first half, in every language at once, including the TypeScript services with no library.

Roll it out in two steps, as 36.1b described: **permissive** mode accepts both mTLS and plaintext while workloads join the mesh, and **strict** mode rejects plaintext once the telemetry shows every caller has an identity. Caller authorization then becomes policy keyed by identity, default deny, reviewed like code.

The application still needs the caller's identity for audit and for 36.1b's actor binding. The sidecar passes the verified peer identity to the application in a header, `X-Forwarded-Client-Cert` for Envoy. The proxy overwrites any value the client sent, which makes the header trustworthy in this specific case, the opposite of 36.2a's headers: **only the local proxy can set it**, and nothing reaches the application except through that proxy.

### Traffic policy: what the mesh can do, and what it can't

The mesh sees every request and response between services. That's enough for a lot of 36.3a and 36.3b:

| Mesh can do | Needs the application |
|---|---|
| per-route timeouts | propagating the request's **deadline** to the next hop (36.3a) |
| retries on connection-level failures, per route | knowing which requests are **idempotent**, and idempotency keys for the rest |
| retry budgets (Linkerd) | deciding a dependency is **optional** for this endpoint, and what to show without it (36.3b) |
| connection and request limits per destination (a bulkhead) | per-endpoint **fallbacks** |
| outlier detection: ejecting a bad instance (19.2a) | a breaker for the **whole dependency**, with failure classification (36.3b) |
| load balancing: least-request, locality-aware | **forwarding trace context** from inbound to outbound (19.2b) |
| traffic splitting for canaries (30.2), mirroring | acting **for a user**: token exchange, subject checks (36.1b) |
| fault injection: delays and errors, for drills | |

The right column has one thing in common: **it depends on what a request means, and the proxy only sees what a request looks like.** The proxy can see `POST /autopay` failed with a reset. It can't know whether `payments` already set up the autopay, or whether this page can render without it.

Envoy's vocabulary causes confusion here too. Its "circuit breakers" are connection and request limits, which is to say bulkheads. The open/closed breaker of 36.3b corresponds most closely to **outlier detection**, which ejects individual hosts, not whole dependencies.

### Retries: decide where they live

The first surprise above is the most common mesh incident. Two rules:

1. **One layer retries.** This is 18.3's rule, and the mesh is a new layer. Either the mesh retries and applications don't, or the reverse, per route. The mesh is usually the better place for **connection-level retries on idempotent reads**: it's consistent across languages, visible in one configuration, and Linkerd can enforce a budget. Applications keep retries that need meaning, such as writes with idempotency keys.
2. **Know the mesh's defaults.** Istio, for example, retries twice by default on a set of connection-level failures unless a route says otherwise. Set retry policy on every route explicitly, including `attempts: 0` for routes that shouldn't retry, so the configuration says what happens instead of inheriting it.

A connection reset is the dangerous case. It can happen before the upstream received the request, where a retry is safe, or after it processed it, where a retry is a duplicate. The proxy can't tell which. Only an idempotency key makes a retried write safe (10.2a, Module 17).

### Telemetry: uniform, but not complete

Every proxy reports the same metrics for every call: request rate, errors and latency by source, destination, route and response code. Those are the "golden signals," defined identically everywhere. That ends the three-definitions-of-success problem, and it produces a service graph from real traffic (36.4) with no application changes.

Two limits:

- **Traces need the application's help.** The proxy can start and end spans, but it can't connect an inbound request to the outbound calls the application makes while handling it. Only the application knows that. Every service has to forward `traceparent`, usually through OpenTelemetry's instrumentation (19.2b). Without it, the mesh produces thousands of one-hop traces.
- **The proxy can't see inside the application.** Queue waits, database time and business steps are still the application's instrumentation. The mesh gives the edges of the graph; the application still has to explain the nodes.

### Lifecycle: the proxy is part of your pod

With sidecars, a second container shares each pod's lifecycle, with predictable problems:

- **Startup order.** If the application starts first, its first outbound calls fail until the proxy is ready. Kubernetes' **native sidecars** (sidecar containers declared as init containers with `restartPolicy: Always`, on by default since Kubernetes 1.29 and generally available since 1.33) start the proxy first. Older setups use mesh settings such as Istio's `holdApplicationUntilProxyStarts`.
- **Shutdown order.** The proxy must outlive the application's last request, which is the 19.2a draining sequence with one more participant.
- **Jobs.** A job's pod completes only when all containers exit, and the proxy doesn't. Native sidecars fix this too, because they're terminated when the main containers finish. Ambient mode avoids the problem entirely.

### Operating the mesh

- **The control plane is critical.** If it's down, existing proxies keep working with their last configuration and certificates, until certificates expire (36.1b). New pods can't get configuration. Run it redundantly, and monitor certificate expiry.
- **Upgrades are major events.** The mesh sits in every request path, so an upgrade touches everything. Istio supports running two control-plane revisions side by side and moving namespaces over gradually. Plan for this from the start.
- **Configuration is global and powerful.** A wrong policy can deny all traffic to a service, and a wrong route can send it to the wrong version. Mesh configuration goes through review, validation (`istioctl analyze`, Linkerd's checks) and staged rollout, like 36.2a's gateway config.
- **Debugging moves.** "Connection reset" may now come from a proxy, a policy or a certificate. Teams need to be able to read proxy logs and configuration dumps, or ask someone who can.
- **Someone owns it.** A mesh without a platform team is a system nobody understands until the incident that requires understanding it.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Several languages**, where cross-cutting network policy would otherwise be implemented and maintained separately in each.
- **Dozens of services and several teams**, where a policy change like "retry once" or "require mTLS" has to reach everything without coordinated deploys.
- **Zero-trust requirements** (36.1b): mTLS and identity-based policy everywhere, including services whose languages lack good libraries.
- **Traffic management between internal services:** canaries, mirroring, locality-aware routing, fault injection for drills.
- **A platform team** that can operate it, upgrade it and debug it.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A handful of services in one or two languages.** A shared library per language, or 36.1b's `go-spiffe` in code, does the job with far less infrastructure.
- **A monolith, or a monolith plus a few workers.** There's hardly any east-west traffic to manage.
- **No one to operate it.** A mesh adopted by a team without capacity to own it becomes the most complex, least understood component in the system.
- **Only for metrics.** If uniform telemetry is the only goal, OpenTelemetry instrumentation and a shared metrics convention (16.3) are much cheaper.
- **Only for mTLS.** If identity and encryption are the only goals, an ambient or node-level L4 mode, or SPIFFE libraries, may be enough without full L7 proxies everywhere.

## Trade-offs

- **Latency on every call.** Each hop passes through two proxies with sidecars. That's typically a millisecond or two, which adds up across deep call chains.
- **Resources on every pod.** Sidecar memory and CPU across hundreds of pods is a real bill. Ambient modes reduce it at the cost of maturity.
- **A new critical dependency.** The control plane and the proxies are in the path of everything. Their bugs, misconfigurations and upgrades are everyone's outages.
- **Hidden behavior.** Retries, timeouts and routing now happen outside the code, so engineers reading the code see only part of what happens to a request. Defaults nobody set become behavior everybody has.
- **Operational expertise.** Reading Envoy configuration, debugging mTLS and policy failures, and managing upgrades are specialized skills.
- **It can make incomplete work look complete.** "The mesh handles resilience" is true for connection-level problems and false for fallbacks, deadlines and idempotency. Teams can stop doing the application half.

## Alternatives

- **Libraries per language.** Resilience4j, `go-spiffe`, OpenTelemetry SDKs and shared configuration. It's the most direct control and the least infrastructure, and it's practical for one or two languages.
- **Proxyless gRPC** with xDS: mesh-managed policy inside gRPC libraries, with no proxy hop. Good for gRPC-heavy systems, and it doesn't cover HTTP clients.
- **Platform-level networking:** Kubernetes network policies, cloud-provider service networking (such as AWS VPC Lattice) and eBPF-based CNIs like Cilium provide identity-aware L4 policy and some L7 features without a full mesh.
- **SPIFFE without a mesh:** SPIRE plus libraries, as in 36.1b, for identity and mTLS where that's the main need.
- **A smaller number of services.** Much of what a mesh manages is the cost of the number of network hops. Sometimes the better answer is fewer boundaries (2.1, 23.1b).

## How to Achieve It

**Level 1: observe.** Install the mesh in permissive mode on a few namespaces. Use its uniform metrics and service graph, and change no traffic behavior. Set retry policy explicitly on every route so the mesh's defaults don't silently apply. *Signal for the next step:* the telemetry shows every caller of a service already has a mesh identity.

**Level 2: identity.** Switch to strict mTLS namespace by namespace. Add default-deny caller authorization policies per service, reviewed like code. Retire per-service API keys. *Signal:* retry, timeout and limit settings still differ by language and team.

**Level 3: traffic policy.** Move connection-level retries for idempotent routes, per-route timeouts, connection limits and outlier detection into mesh configuration. Remove the corresponding application retries so exactly one layer retries. Make sure every service forwards trace context and deadlines. *Signal:* releases and drills still need custom code.

**Level 4: the mesh as a platform.** Canary routing between services, fault injection in game days (36.3a), locality-aware load balancing, revision-based mesh upgrades, and consider ambient mode to reduce per-pod cost.

## Implementation

Istio configuration for the parts the mesh takes over, and TypeScript for the parts it can't. The TypeScript is the more instructive half: `documents` and `messaging` are the services that couldn't get 36.1b's mTLS from a library, so they gain the most from the mesh. The code shows what remains their job.

**Identity and caller authorization, as configuration:**

```yaml
apiVersion: security.istio.io/v1
kind: PeerAuthentication
metadata: { name: default, namespace: istio-system }   # mesh-wide
spec:
  mtls: { mode: STRICT }                               # after PERMISSIVE showed every caller has an identity
---
apiVersion: security.istio.io/v1
kind: AuthorizationPolicy
metadata: { name: payments, namespace: payments }
spec:
  selector: { matchLabels: { app: payments } }
  action: ALLOW                                        # with an ALLOW policy, anything unmatched is denied
  rules:
    - from: [{ source: { principals: ["homes.example/ns/leases/sa/leases"] } }]
      to:   [{ operation: { methods: ["POST"], paths: ["/autopay"] } }]
    - from: [{ source: { principals: ["homes.example/ns/support/sa/support-tools"] } }]
      to:   [{ operation: { methods: ["POST"], paths: ["/refunds"] } }]
    - from: [{ source: { principals: ["homes.example/ns/bff/sa/resident-bff",
                                      "homes.example/ns/bff/sa/staff-bff"] } }]
      to:   [{ operation: { methods: ["GET"], paths: ["/balance"] } }]
```

This is 36.1b's Go allowlist as configuration, and it applies to all 14 services the same way regardless of language.

**Traffic policy, explicit per route:**

```yaml
apiVersion: networking.istio.io/v1
kind: VirtualService
metadata: { name: payments, namespace: payments }
spec:
  hosts: [payments.payments.svc.cluster.local]
  http:
    - match: [{ method: { exact: GET } }]
      timeout: 400ms
      retries: { attempts: 1, perTryTimeout: 200ms, retryOn: "connect-failure,refused-stream,reset" }
    - timeout: 2s
      retries: { attempts: 0 }                      # writes: never retried by the mesh
---
apiVersion: networking.istio.io/v1
kind: DestinationRule
metadata: { name: payments, namespace: payments }
spec:
  host: payments.payments.svc.cluster.local
  trafficPolicy:
    loadBalancer: { simple: LEAST_REQUEST }
    connectionPool:
      http: { http2MaxRequests: 200 }               # in-flight cap per caller proxy: a bulkhead (36.3a)
    outlierDetection:                               # eject one bad pod (19.2a), not the whole service
      consecutive5xxErrors: 5
      interval: 10s
      baseEjectionTime: 30s
      maxEjectionPercent: 34
```

> **Aside — `attempts: 0` is a decision, written down.** The first month's duplicate came from one retry block copied from an example and applied to every route. The opposite mistake is quieter: a route with no retry block isn't retry-free, because it inherits the mesh's default policy. Writes that need retrying get an idempotency key and a retry in the application, which knows what the operation means. And once the mesh retries idempotent `GET`s, the applications' own retries for those calls have to be removed, or the layers multiply again, as in 18.3.

**What the application still does: forward context, read the verified caller:**

```ts
import { AsyncLocalStorage } from "node:async_hooks";

type Inbound = { forward: [string, string][]; deadline?: number; caller?: string };
const inbound = new AsyncLocalStorage<Inbound>();

const FORWARD = ["traceparent", "tracestate", "baggage"];

// Express middleware, first in the chain.
export function captureInbound(req: Request, _res: Response, next: NextFunction) {
  const ms = Number(req.header("x-deadline-ms"));
  inbound.run({
    forward: FORWARD.flatMap((h) => (req.header(h) ? [[h, req.header(h)!]] : [])),
    deadline: ms > 0 ? Date.now() + ms : undefined,
    caller: callerFromXfcc(req.header("x-forwarded-client-cert")),
  }, next);
}

// Every outbound call goes through this. Plain HTTP: the sidecar adds mTLS, identity and policy.
export async function call(url: string, init: RequestInit = {}): Promise<Response> {
  const ctx = inbound.getStore();
  const headers = new Headers(init.headers);
  for (const [name, value] of ctx?.forward ?? []) headers.set(name, value);  // or spans never join

  const signals = init.signal ? [init.signal] : [];
  if (ctx?.deadline) {
    const left = ctx.deadline - Date.now() - 10;
    if (left <= 0) throw new DeadlineExceeded();         // don't start work nobody will wait for
    headers.set("x-deadline-ms", String(left));          // the mesh can't compute this for you
    signals.push(AbortSignal.timeout(left));
  }
  return fetch(url, { ...init, headers, signal: signals.length ? AbortSignal.any(signals) : undefined });
}

// Set by the local sidecar, which replaces any value the client sent.
function callerFromXfcc(xfcc?: string): string | undefined {
  return xfcc?.match(/URI=(spiffe:\/\/[^;,]+)/)?.[1];     // e.g. spiffe://homes.example/ns/bff/sa/resident-bff
}
```

> **Aside — which half OpenTelemetry already does.** OpenTelemetry's Node HTTP instrumentation propagates `traceparent` automatically, using the same `AsyncLocalStorage` mechanism shown here, so in practice the trace half of `call()` comes from the SDK. The deadline half doesn't: no library knows your deadline header, and the mesh only knows the per-route timeout, which isn't the same thing. A deadline is how much of the *user's* time is left. A route timeout is how long *this hop* may take, whatever happened before it. And `caller` from the XFCC header is safe only because strict mTLS means nothing reaches this process except through its own sidecar. In permissive mode, a plaintext client could set the header itself.

With the mesh in place, the three original problems are gone. Retries are configured per route in one place, and applications don't retry what the mesh does. `documents` and `messaging` have the same mTLS and caller policy as the Go services, with no library. Metrics come from identical proxies. The first month's surprises are addressed by explicit route policy, context forwarding in every service, and native sidecars for jobs.

- **Istio documentation:** security (peer authentication, authorization policy), traffic management (virtual services, destination rules, retries), and "Ambient mode overview."
- **Linkerd documentation**, especially retries and retry budgets, and its comparison of per-request retries with budgeted ones.
- **Envoy documentation:** "Circuit breaking," "Outlier detection," and `x-forwarded-client-cert`.
- **Kubernetes documentation, "Sidecar Containers,"** for native sidecars and the startup and job-completion problems they solve.
- **Christian Posta and Rinor Maloku, _Istio in Action_** (Manning, 2022), for operating a mesh in depth.
- **gRPC documentation, "xDS Features in gRPC,"** for proxyless mesh.

Worth seeing in **Go** as a sibling, with proxyless gRPC: the same policies delivered over xDS directly into `grpc-go`, with no sidecar in the path. It shows the trade-off in reverse: lower latency and no extra containers, and the network policy is back inside your binary and its dependency upgrades. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: for every piece of network behavior, ask whether it depends on what a request *looks like* or on what it *means*. The first can move into infrastructure and be the same everywhere. The second stays in code, and a mesh that seems to handle it is usually just applying a default.
