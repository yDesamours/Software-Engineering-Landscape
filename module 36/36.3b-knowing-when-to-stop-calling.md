# Knowing When to Stop Calling: Circuit Breakers and Fallbacks

_Module 36.3b — Advanced Microservices Patterns_

## The Problem

Suppose you run an online grocery delivery service. Customers browse aisles, fill a cart and choose a delivery slot. A Java service called `shop` renders listing pages and runs checkout. For every listing page and every checkout, it calls `pricing`, which owns shelf prices and promotions. `pricing` is required: a grocery page without prices is useless, and a checkout without them can't charge.

`shop` runs eight pods and makes about 150 pricing calls a second per pod, or 1,200 in total. The team has already done everything in 36.3a. Pricing calls have a 400 ms timeout, a 40-slot bulkhead per pod and one retry at a single layer. When pricing misbehaves, `shop`'s pods stay healthy.

That turns out to be necessary but not sufficient. Three incidents, a few weeks apart:

**Tuesday, 18:40, the dinner rush: the dependency that can't get back up.** An automatic statistics update gives pricing's database a bad query plan. Pricing's latency goes from 20 ms to 1.5 seconds. `shop` behaves exactly as designed: every call times out at 400 ms, retries once, and fails. The bulkheads cap how many calls are in flight, so `shop` itself is fine. Listing pages just take 800 ms to show an error.

Pricing isn't fine. `shop` gives up after 400 ms, but pricing's queries don't know that. They keep running for the full 1.5 seconds on a database that's already saturated. And they keep arriving, about 800 a second, each one abandoned before it finishes. At 18:46 a DBA pins the correct plan. The root cause is gone, but pricing doesn't recover. Its connection pool is full of abandoned queries, so every new request queues behind them and takes longer than 400 ms, so `shop` abandons it too, and retries it. The system is stuck in a failure that no longer has a cause.

At 19:21 an engineer blocks `shop`'s traffic to pricing at the mesh for 90 seconds. Pricing's backlog drains in 40 seconds, and it comes back healthy. The root cause lasted six minutes. The outage lasted 41. What finally fixed it was **not calling pricing for a while**. The engineer had built a circuit breaker by hand, under pressure, during dinner.

**Sunday, 03:10: the breaker that opens for the wrong reason.** After Tuesday, the team adds a circuit breaker to pricing calls: open when half the recent calls fail. At 3 a.m. a price-comparison scraper starts walking 60,000 old product IDs, most of them discontinued. Pricing answers each one correctly with `404 Not Found`. The client turns 404s into `ItemNotPricedException`, and the breaker, configured to count every exception as a failure, counts them all. At 3 a.m. the scraper is most of the traffic. The failure rate crosses 50%, and the breaker opens on all eight pods.

Every real customer shopping at that hour, including a steady trickle of night-shift workers, now gets a page with no prices. After 30 seconds each breaker lets ten trial calls through. Most of them are scraper requests, they 404, and the breaker opens again. It flaps for 70 minutes. Pricing was perfectly healthy the whole time.

**Saturday, 10:00: the fallback that charged the wrong price.** After Tuesday, the team also adds a fallback inside the shared `PriceService.getPrice()`: when the breaker is open, return the last known price from a local cache. At 10:00 on Saturday, the busiest shopping hour of the week, the weekly promotions go live. Activating 9,000 promotions saturates pricing's CPU for about two minutes, which is a known cost. The breaker opens at 10:00:20, and `getPrice()` starts returning cached prices from before 10:00.

On listing pages that's merely wrong for two minutes. But checkout calls the same method. **340 orders are charged pre-promotion prices** while the flyer, the email and the app banner all advertise the promotion. Customer service refunds the difference on every order, and a consumer-protection complaint arrives about advertised prices. The fallback did exactly what it was written to do. The mistake was giving one dependency one fallback, when two endpoints needed opposite answers.

## What It Is

A **circuit breaker** wraps calls to a dependency and watches their outcomes. When enough of them fail, it **opens**. For a while it rejects calls immediately, without contacting the dependency, and then it lets a few trial calls through to see whether the dependency has recovered. The name comes from electrical breakers, which cut the circuit when current is dangerous instead of letting the wiring burn. Michael Nygard named the software pattern in _Release It!_ (2007), and Netflix's Hystrix made it standard equipment. Hystrix has been in maintenance mode since 2018, and Resilience4j is its usual successor on the JVM.

A breaker does two jobs at once:

- **For the caller:** calls fail in microseconds instead of after a timeout, so pages show a fallback or an error immediately.
- **For the dependency:** its traffic stops, so it can finish or abandon its backlog and recover. This is the job Tuesday needed, and the one no per-call mechanism can do.

A **fallback** is what the caller does instead of the call: an error, a missing section, a default, a stale value, another source. The breaker decides *whether* to call. The fallback decides *what the user gets* when you don't. They're separate decisions, and Saturday happened because they were made in the same place.

How it relates to its neighbors:

- **Timeouts and bulkheads (36.3a)** bound the cost of each call while you keep calling. A breaker stops calling.
- **Retry budgets (18.3)** cap retries as a fraction of traffic. A breaker caps all calls, first attempts included.
- **Outlier detection (19.2a)** removes one bad *instance* from a load-balancing pool. A breaker responds to the whole *dependency* failing.
- **Load shedding (18.1)** protects a service from its callers, on the service's side. A breaker is the caller choosing to back off.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A dependency's root cause is fixed, but it recovers only after its callers are stopped, scaled down or blocked.
- A failing dependency's incoming traffic doesn't fall during its outage, or rises.
- During a dependency outage, every page waits the full timeout before failing, while the dependency is known to be down.
- A breaker opens while the dependency's own dashboards are green.
- One client, crawler or tenant can open a breaker that affects everyone.
- A breaker flaps: open, closed, open, every few seconds or minutes.
- The fallback code path has never appeared in a log line or a test.
- Display paths and money paths share one `getX()` method with one fallback.
- A fallback queries a database, calls another service, or does anything more expensive than the call it replaces.

## How It Works

### Three states

```
                  failure rate or slow-call rate ≥ threshold
                  (over a window, after a minimum number of calls)
       ┌────────┐ ────────────────────────────────────────────► ┌────────┐
       │ CLOSED │                                                │  OPEN  │  every call rejected
       │        │ ◄──────────────┐                               │        │  instantly
       └────────┘                │                               └────────┘
            ▲          trial calls succeed                          │
            │                    │                                  │ wait duration elapses
            │               ┌───────────┐                           │
            └───────────────│ HALF-OPEN │ ◄─────────────────────────┘
                            │ N trial   │
                            │ calls let │ ── trial calls fail ──► back to OPEN
                            │ through   │                         (wait longer next time)
                            └───────────┘
```

- **Closed:** calls go through, and the breaker records their outcomes in a sliding window.
- **Open:** calls are rejected at once with a specific exception (`CallNotPermittedException` in Resilience4j), so the caller can apply its fallback.
- **Half-open:** a fixed number of trial calls go through. If they succeed well enough, the breaker closes. If not, it opens again.

The mechanism is simple. Nearly every production problem with breakers comes from four decisions around it: what counts as a failure, how much evidence is enough, how it reopens, and what each caller does when it's open.

### What counts as a failure

A breaker should open when **the dependency** is failing. Sunday happened because it opened when the dependency was answering correctly. Classify outcomes explicitly:

| Outcome | Counts as | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 5xx, connection refused, reset, timeout | **failure** | the dependency is broken or overloaded |
| 429 or 503 with `Retry-After` | **failure** | the dependency is shedding load and asking you to back off |
| Slower than a threshold, but succeeded | **slow call** (its own rate) | slowness is the early sign, per 36.3a |
| 4xx: not found, validation, forbidden | **success** | the dependency did its job; the request was wrong |
| Your own bulkhead was full | **ignored** | a limit on your side, not a signal about theirs |
| Your caller's deadline expired first | **ignored** | a slow upstream shouldn't open breakers downstream |
| A bug in your code (parsing, null) | **ignored or success** | opening the breaker would hide your bug behind their name |

Two of these are easy to miss. **Slow calls** get their own threshold, set below the timeout, so the breaker opens while calls are still slow, before they start timing out. On Tuesday that would have been within seconds. **Deadline cancellations** from 36.3a have to be excluded, or a slow service at the top of a chain opens breakers on every healthy service below it.

Library defaults usually count every exception as a failure. That default is how Sunday happened.

### How much evidence: windows and thresholds

The breaker judges a **sliding window** of recent calls:

- **Count-based:** the last *N* calls. Simple, but at low traffic the last 100 calls may span an hour, so the breaker reacts to stale history.
- **Time-based:** calls in the last *N* seconds. Better on a request path, where traffic varies by hour.

Then two guards:

- **A minimum number of calls** before the rate means anything. Three failures out of four calls is 75%, and is noise. At 150 calls a second per pod, a 10-second window with a 100-call minimum sees enough evidence within a second of an outage starting.
- **Thresholds** for the failure rate and the slow-call rate. Around 50% for failures is common. Much lower, and ordinary blips open it. Much higher, and it waits until the dependency is almost entirely down.

**Low-traffic dependencies** break this arithmetic. A call made twice a minute never reaches a meaningful minimum inside a useful window. Either skip the breaker (36.3a's timeout and an alert do the work), or accept a long window and a small minimum knowing it reacts slowly.

### Reopening: open duration and half-open

**How long to stay open.** Long enough for the dependency to drain its backlog. Tuesday's took 40 seconds. Short enough that users don't see errors long after recovery. A fixed 30 to 60 seconds is common. Better is a duration that **grows with each consecutive opening**, like exponential backoff for the whole dependency: 5 seconds, 10, 20, capped at a minute or two, with jitter so eight pods don't all probe at the same instant.

**How many trial calls, and which.** Half-open lets through a small number, often 5 to 20 per instance. Two things to know:

- **Your probes are real user requests.** You don't choose which ones. On Sunday they were mostly scraper requests, which is why the breaker kept reopening. Correct failure classification fixes that. Some teams send a synthetic health request as the probe instead, which is cleaner but tests a path real traffic may not take.
- **Probes multiply by instances.** Ten trial calls on each of 200 caller pods is 2,000 calls to a dependency that's just recovering. Size half-open for the whole fleet, not one pod.

**Closing is a surge.** Every pod closes its breaker within seconds of the others, and full traffic returns at once, to a dependency with cold caches. A dependency that fails again the moment breakers close is a sign the open duration is too short, or that callers need to ramp back up gradually. Envoy's slow-start and gradual weight increases in load balancers do the ramping. A breaker alone doesn't.

### Scope: what one breaker covers

One breaker per *what*?

- **Per dependency** is the usual default: one breaker for all calls to pricing.
- **Per operation**, when a dependency's endpoints fail independently. Pricing's bulk-quote endpoint timing out on huge carts shouldn't cut off single-item prices, which run on a different code path. Split breakers along the lines where failures actually differ.
- **Per instance** is outlier detection's job, not the breaker's. If one of pricing's twelve pods is bad, the load balancer or mesh should eject that pod (19.2a). A per-dependency breaker sees roughly 8% failures and either does nothing, which is right, or opens for all twelve, which is wrong.
- **Per tenant** or per upstream caller, when one caller's traffic can fail for its own reasons. That's Sunday's scraper as a design input: its requests shouldn't be able to decide for everyone.

**Breaker state is per process**, and that's usually fine. Each pod forms its own opinion from its own traffic, and they agree within a second or two. Shared breaker state in Redis adds a dependency to your protection against dependencies.

### Order: breaker, retry, bulkhead and timeout together

Each mechanism sees what's inside it and is seen by what's outside it. The order that works:

```
 fallback           outermost: decides what the user gets, whatever went wrong below
  └─ retry          one layer only (18.3); never retries a rejection from below
      └─ breaker    records each attempt, including retries; rejects instantly when open
          └─ bulkhead  caps in-flight calls (36.3a); full → rejected, ignored by the breaker
              └─ call  with its own timeouts
```

- **Retry outside the breaker:** each attempt is recorded, so retries count as evidence, and once the breaker opens, the retry gets `CallNotPermittedException` and must not retry it.
- **Breaker outside the bulkhead:** when open, calls are rejected before taking a slot.
- **Fallback outermost**, so it applies whether the breaker is open, the bulkhead is full or the retry gave up.

With the breaker *outside* the retry, the breaker sees one outcome per logical call, after all its retries. It opens later, and while it's closed, every call still hits the dependency twice.

### Fallbacks: decided per endpoint, not per dependency

Saturday's lesson: **the same dependency needs different fallbacks on different paths.** Write the decision down as a table, endpoint by dependency:

| Endpoint | Pricing unavailable → | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Listing page | last known price, if still valid, labeled with its time; otherwise "price unavailable" and no add-to-cart | browsing survives; nobody is charged from here |
| Cart | same as listing, with "prices will be confirmed at checkout" | the cart isn't a commitment |
| Checkout | **no fallback.** "We couldn't confirm prices. Your cart is saved." | a stale value here is a wrong charge |
| Order history | the stored order price (never called pricing anyway) | no dependency, no fallback needed |

Fallbacks come in a few kinds, roughly from safest to riskiest:

1. **Fail fast with a clear error.** The default. Instant, honest, and often the right answer on paths that move money or change state.
2. **Omit the section.** The carousel from 36.3a. Only possible for optional dependencies.
3. **A static default.** A hard-coded "popular items" list, a feature turned off. Cheap and predictable.
4. **Stale data**, from a local cache or a replicated copy. Serve-stale caching (Module 13) and synced third-party data (Module 17) are this kind. Useful and dangerous: it needs a validity limit and a visible label.
5. **Accept now, finish later.** Take the order and confirm payment asynchronously, "Order received, confirming payment." Only where the business can tolerate the later failure, and it needs a follow-up path when it happens.
6. **An alternative source**, such as a second payment provider or a secondary region. Real redundancy, if the alternative is sized for the full load and is regularly exercised. Often neither.

### Fallbacks that do harm

Amazon's builders' library has a whole essay titled "Avoiding fallback in distributed systems," and its caution is earned. Fallbacks go wrong in predictable ways:

- **Stale values in the wrong place.** Saturday. Stale data needs a **validity limit that knows about scheduled changes**, not just a maximum age. A price with `validUntil = 10:00` stops being usable at 10:00, however recently it was cached.
- **Fallbacks that shift load onto something shared.** The team's first draft of a recommendations fallback queried the orders database for "frequently bought together." It would have moved 1,200 queries a second onto the database checkout depends on, exactly when things were already failing. **A fallback must be cheaper than the call it replaces, and independent of it and of anything critical.** In-memory, precomputed, or nothing.
- **Alternatives sized for normal times.** A secondary provider that handles 10% of traffic will fail immediately at 100%, and now both are down.
- **Fallbacks nobody has run.** Fallback code runs only during incidents, so its bugs are discovered during incidents. A `NullPointerException` in the fallback turns a degraded page into an error page. The fix is to exercise it deliberately (below).
- **Fallbacks that hide the outage.** A page that renders from stale data returns `200`. Count every fallback served, by endpoint and kind, and alert on it, the way 36.3a counted degraded responses.

### Operating breakers

- **Alert on state transitions**, not only on the dependency's error rate. "Pricing breaker opened on 8 of 8 pods" is a precise, early page.
- **Dashboards show state per instance**, not-permitted counts and fallbacks served. A breaker open on one pod out of eight usually means that pod's network, not the dependency.
- **Forcing a breaker open is a kill switch you already have.** Tuesday's engineer blocked traffic at the mesh. With breakers you can force pricing's breaker open from an admin endpoint or a feature flag, and every fallback path activates in a controlled way.
- **Drill it.** Force each breaker open in staging, and periodically in production for low-risk dependencies, and watch what users get. That's how you find the fallback's `NullPointerException` on a Wednesday afternoon instead of during the next incident.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Dependencies on a request path that can fail as a whole**, such as another service, a database cluster or a third-party API, called often enough for the window to hold real evidence. Tens of calls a second per instance is plenty.
- **Dependencies that can get stuck in overload**, where continued traffic keeps them down after the cause is gone. Anything with queues, connection pools or caches that go cold.
- **Paid or rate-limited third parties** (Module 17), where calls made during their outage cost money or quota.
- **Fallbacks:** wherever a degraded answer is genuinely better than an error for that endpoint, and the degraded answer is cheap, independent and labeled.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Dependencies called a few times a minute.** The breaker never gathers enough evidence. A timeout and an alert on failures do the job (as Module 17 noted for hourly calls).
- **Per-instance failures.** One bad replica behind a load balancer is outlier detection's job. A breaker at the dependency level is the wrong granularity.
- **Your own database, when every request needs it.** An open breaker and a failing database produce the same error page. The breaker adds a state machine and nothing else. Bulkheads between workloads (36.3a) help more.
- **Fallbacks on money or state-changing paths.** Charging, reserving stock, sending messages: fail clearly, or accept-and-confirm with a real follow-up. A stale value or a skipped step on these paths is a wrong outcome, not a degraded one.
- **Elaborate alternative sources before simple ones.** A second payment provider is a large, permanent investment. Fail fast and a good error message first.

## Trade-offs

- **An open breaker fails requests that would have succeeded.** During a partial outage some calls would have worked. The breaker rejects them anyway, on purpose, to buy recovery for everyone.
- **Tuning is a guess.** Thresholds, windows, minimums and open durations interact, and good values depend on traffic that changes by hour and by season. A wrong setting either never opens or flaps.
- **Classification is ongoing work.** Every new exception type or status code is a new decision about whether it's the dependency's fault. The default is usually wrong.
- **Fallbacks are a second product.** Each one is a code path, a UX state, a test and a metric. Stale-data fallbacks also need validity rules that understand the domain, such as scheduled price changes.
- **Fallbacks hide failures.** Degraded responses are successes to the error-rate dashboard. Without their own metrics, a dependency can be broken for a week.
- **Recovery is lumpy.** Probes and simultaneous closing across pods produce surges at the worst moment. Smoothing them needs ramping somewhere else.
- **Per-process state means disagreement.** For a few seconds some pods have opened and others haven't, and users on different pods see different pages.

## Alternatives

- **Load shedding at the dependency (18.1).** If pricing sheds excess load quickly with `503`s, it protects itself no matter what callers do, which helps with callers you don't control. It doesn't make callers fail fast, and it still pays for accepting and rejecting every request.
- **Retry budgets and client-side throttling (18.3).** They reduce extra load from retries, and adaptive throttling rejects locally in proportion to the server's failure rate, a soft, probabilistic breaker. It handles overload smoothly but never cuts traffic entirely, which Tuesday's recovery needed.
- **Outlier detection (19.2a)** for instance-level failure, as above. Usually you want both.
- **Deadline propagation (36.3a).** Much of Tuesday was pricing working on abandoned requests. Had pricing honored `shop`'s deadline and cancelled its queries, the backlog would never have formed. A breaker is still valuable, but a smaller one.
- **The mesh.** Watch the vocabulary: **Envoy's "circuit breakers" are concurrency limits**, maximum connections, pending requests and retries, which is to say bulkheads. The open/closed behavior in this article corresponds to Envoy's outlier detection, which ejects hosts, not whole dependencies. A mesh gives you uniform limits across languages, but it can't choose a fallback for your endpoint (36.1a).
- **Take the call off the path.** A listing page that reads prices from a replicated local copy, updated by events from pricing, never needs a breaker for display. Checkout still calls pricing live. The pattern is 23.4's and Module 38's: move display reads away from the live dependency, and keep live calls for commitments.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 1: a breaker with explicit failure classification.** Put a breaker on each request-path dependency that can fail as a whole. Define what counts as a failure, a slow call, a success and an ignored outcome. Use a time-based window with a minimum, and alert on state transitions. *Signal for the next step:* the breaker opens and fails fast, but every page on that path shows an error.

**Level 2: fallbacks decided per endpoint.** Write the endpoint-by-dependency table. Separate display paths from money paths in code, not just in intent. Give stale data a validity rule and a visible label, and count every fallback served. *Signal:* a fallback surprised someone during an incident, or has never run.

**Level 3: exercised and controllable.** Integration tests that force each breaker open and assert what the user gets. Forced-open as a kill switch. Drills in staging, then carefully in production. Review each fallback's cost and independence. *Signal:* breakers flap, or recovery surges knock dependencies over again.

**Level 4: tuned from data, with gradual recovery.** Open durations that grow with consecutive openings, with jitter. Breakers per operation where failures differ. Half-open sized for the fleet, and a ramp back to full traffic (slow-start at the load balancer or mesh). Thresholds tuned from observed incident data, not copied defaults.

## Implementation

Java with Resilience4j. The library has a breaker, retry, bulkhead and fallbacks as separate decorators, so the order in which they compose is a choice you make explicitly. That makes it a clear place to see the ordering from *How It Works*, and one of its traps. The class below is the pricing client for `shop`: one guarded call, and two public methods with deliberately different fallbacks.

**Failure classification and configuration:**

```java
public final class PricingGateway {

  /** True only when pricing itself is failing. Everything else is success or ignored. */
  static boolean isPricingFailure(Throwable e) {
    return switch (e) {
      case PricingHttpException h -> h.status() >= 500 || h.status() == 429;
      case PricingIoException io -> true;   // connect refused, reset, read timeout
      default -> false;                     // 404s, validation errors, our own bugs
    };
  }

  private static final CircuitBreakerConfig BREAKER = CircuitBreakerConfig.custom()
      .slidingWindowType(SlidingWindowType.TIME_BASED)
      .slidingWindowSize(10)                                  // seconds
      .minimumNumberOfCalls(100)                              // ~0.7 s of this pod's traffic
      .failureRateThreshold(50)
      .slowCallDurationThreshold(Duration.ofMillis(250))      // below the 400 ms timeout
      .slowCallRateThreshold(60)
      .recordException(PricingGateway::isPricingFailure)      // not "every exception"
      .ignoreExceptions(BulkheadFullException.class,          // our limit, not their failure
                        DeadlineExceededException.class)      // our caller gave up (36.3a)
      .permittedNumberOfCallsInHalfOpenState(5)               // × 8 pods = 40 probes
      .waitIntervalFunctionInOpenState(attempt ->             // 5 s, 10 s, 20 s … capped, jittered
          (long) (Math.min(60_000, 5_000 * Math.pow(2, attempt - 1))
                  * ThreadLocalRandom.current().nextDouble(0.8, 1.2)))
      .build();

  private static final RetryConfig RETRY = RetryConfig.custom()
      .maxAttempts(2)                                         // one retry, at this layer only
      .intervalFunction(IntervalFunction.ofExponentialRandomBackoff(Duration.ofMillis(25), 2.0))
      .retryOnException(PricingGateway::isPricingFailure)     // so never CallNotPermitted
      .build();                                               //    or BulkheadFull
```

> **Aside — "not recorded" means success.** In Resilience4j, an exception that `recordException` rejects counts as a *success*, which is right for a 404 (pricing worked) and wrong for your own bulkhead rejecting the call or your caller's deadline expiring. Those tell you nothing about pricing, so they go in `ignoreExceptions`, which excludes them from the window entirely. Sunday's scraper would have produced 60,000 successes and no open breaker.

**Composition, where the order is the trap:**

```java
  private final Function<String, Price> guarded;
  private final CircuitBreaker breaker;
  private final LastKnownPrices lastKnown;   // in-memory; written on every successful call
  // plus: clock, fallbacks and notPermitted counters, log

  PricingGateway(PricingClient client, CircuitBreakerRegistry breakers,
                 BulkheadRegistry bulkheads, RetryRegistry retries, LastKnownPrices lastKnown) {
    this.lastKnown = lastKnown;
    this.breaker = breakers.circuitBreaker("pricing", BREAKER);
    this.guarded = Decorators.ofFunction(client::price)
        .withBulkhead(bulkheads.bulkhead("pricing"))   // listed first  = innermost
        .withCircuitBreaker(breaker)
        .withRetry(retries.retry("pricing", RETRY))    // listed last   = outermost
        .decorate();
  }
```

> **Aside — the list reads top to bottom and runs inside out.** Each `with…` wraps everything above it, so the first decorator listed is the innermost. It reads like "bulkhead, then breaker, then retry," and executes as retry around breaker around bulkhead, which is the order from *How It Works*. Swap the last two lines and the breaker sees one outcome per pair of attempts, opens later, and still lets every call hit pricing twice. With Spring Boot annotations instead, the order is fixed by aspect priorities; the default is retry outermost, then breaker, then rate limiter, time limiter and bulkhead, and it changes only through the `*AspectOrder` properties. Either way, write a test that asserts pricing receives zero calls once the breaker is open.

**Two endpoints, two fallbacks:**

```java
  /** Listing and cart: a labeled, still-valid last-known price beats an empty aisle. */
  public DisplayPrice forDisplay(String sku) {
    try {
      Price p = guarded.apply(sku);
      lastKnown.put(sku, p);
      return DisplayPrice.live(p);
    } catch (RuntimeException e) {
      if (!pricingUnavailable(e)) throw e;          // a 404 is an answer, not an outage
      fallbacks.increment("pricing", "display", fallbackReason(e));
      return lastKnown.get(sku)
          .filter(p -> p.validAt(clock.instant())) // before validUntil, and under 15 min old
          .map(DisplayPrice::stale)                 // "Price as of 09:58 · confirmed at checkout"
          .orElseGet(DisplayPrice::unavailable);    // no price shown, no add-to-cart
    }
  }

  /** Checkout: an authoritative price or no charge. There is deliberately no fallback. */
  public Price forCharge(String sku) {
    return guarded.apply(sku);  // failure → "We couldn't confirm prices. Your cart is saved."
  }

  private static boolean pricingUnavailable(Throwable e) {
    return e instanceof CallNotPermittedException
        || e instanceof BulkheadFullException
        || isPricingFailure(e);
  }
```

> **Aside — `validUntil` is what would have saved Saturday.** Pricing returns each price with the time of its next scheduled change, so a cached price knows it expires at 10:00 on promotion day, however fresh the cache entry is. A maximum age alone can't know that: an entry cached at 09:59 is one minute old and entirely wrong at 10:00:20. And even with `validUntil`, `forCharge` still has no fallback. Keeping the two methods separate is what makes that a structural fact rather than a convention someone can forget.

**Observability and the kill switch:**

```java
    // in the constructor
    breaker.getEventPublisher()
        .onStateTransition(e -> log.warn("pricing breaker {}", e.getStateTransition()))
        .onCallNotPermitted(e -> notPermitted.increment());

  /** Admin endpoint or feature flag: every fallback path activates, on purpose. */
  public void forceOpen()  { breaker.transitionToForcedOpenState(); }
  public void release()    { breaker.transitionToClosedState(); }
```

With these pieces, the three incidents replay differently. On Tuesday the slow-call rate opens the breaker within a second or two, pricing's traffic drops to 40 probes every few seconds, and its backlog drains while the DBA works. Listing pages show labeled last-known prices, and checkout says to try again in a moment. On Sunday the scraper's 404s are successes, and nothing opens. On Saturday listing pages show "price unavailable" for items whose promotion started at 10:00, and checkout waits for pricing.
