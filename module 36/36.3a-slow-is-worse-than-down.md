# Slow Is Worse Than Down: Timeouts, Deadlines and Bulkheads

_Module 36.3a — Advanced Microservices Patterns_

## The Problem

Suppose you run an online store for outdoor gear. A Go service called `storefront` serves product pages and checkout, and it calls four other services to do it:

```
storefront ──► catalog          (product data, ~20 ms)      product page, checkout
           ──► pricing          (~15 ms)                    product page, checkout
           ──► recommendations  (the "you might also like" carousel, ~40 ms)   product page only
           ──► payments         (~300 ms)                   checkout only
```

`storefront` runs six pods and takes about 1,200 requests a second, or 200 per pod. Product pages are 70% of that (140 a second per pod). Checkout is about 3%, but it's all the revenue. Each pod also has the in-flight cap from 18.1: past 500 concurrent requests, it sheds new ones with a `503`.

**Monday: recommendations goes down, and nobody notices.** A bad deploy crashes it. Every call from `storefront` gets "connection refused" in under a millisecond. The product page code already treated a recommendations error as "no carousel," so pages render without it for twenty minutes. Checkout is untouched. The only trace of the outage is a dip in the carousel's click-through graph.

**Thursday: recommendations gets slow, and the whole store goes down.** A schema migration takes a lock on the table recommendations reads from. The service stays up and keeps accepting connections, but every query waits on the lock, so every request waits too. Nothing errors. Requests just don't finish.

The HTTP client `storefront` uses for recommendations is `&http.Client{}`, and the zero value of Go's `http.Client` has no timeout at all. Each product-page request now parks its goroutine on the recommendations call and stays there.

```
healthy:   140 req/s × 0.04 s   ≈    6 requests waiting on recommendations
Thursday:  140 req/s × (never)   →  +140 more every second, none leaving

  t = 0.0 s    [                                                  ]   0 / 500
  t = 1.0 s    [██████████████                                    ] 140 / 500
  t = 2.0 s    [████████████████████████████                      ] 280 / 500
  t = 3.6 s    [██████████████████████████████████████████████████] 500 / 500  → shedding
```

After 3.6 seconds every pod is full of product-page requests waiting on a carousel. The shedder does exactly what it was built to do: it rejects new requests, and it can't tell a product page from a checkout. **Checkout, which never calls recommendations, fails 100% for 23 minutes**, until the migration finishes and the lock is released. (The probes were on their own port, per 19.2a, so at least Kubernetes didn't also pull the pods.)

On Monday recommendations was down, and it cost nothing. On Thursday it was slow, and it cost the whole store. A dead dependency returns its error in a millisecond and hands your resources straight back. A slow one keeps them.

**The first fix is a timeout, and it's not enough.** The team sets a 3-second timeout on every outbound call and replays Thursday in staging. Product pages now hold a slot for 3 seconds instead of forever: `140 × 3 = 420` in flight per pod. That fits under 500, barely. Add ordinary traffic and a lunchtime peak, and checkout is shedding again. **A timeout bounds how long one call can hold a resource. It doesn't bound how many calls do.**

**The timeouts also don't line up.** A month later the tax service, which `pricing` calls, slows to about 4 seconds per response. Every hop has its own 3-second timeout, each starting its own clock. The edge gives up on the user at 3 seconds and returns a `504`. `pricing` is still waiting on `tax`, and `tax` is still computing. Traces from the incident show that about 40% of `tax`'s CPU went to answers that arrived after everyone upstream had stopped listening, on the service that was already struggling.

## What It Is

This is a **cascading failure**: a fault in one component spreading to others, through resources they share and through how callers react. Thursday's fault was a lock in one database. The failure was the entire store.

Three tools, each bounding a different quantity, stop most cascades at the service that first sees them:

- **Timeouts** bound how long one call can hold your resources.
- **Deadlines**, propagated across hops, bound how long the whole request can take and stop work that nobody is waiting for anymore.
- **Bulkheads** bound how much of your capacity one dependency can occupy at once. The name comes from ships: watertight compartments, so one breach floods one compartment instead of sinking the hull.

The obvious neighbor is the **circuit breaker** (36.3b). A timeout and a bulkhead limit how much damage a failing dependency can do while you keep calling it. A breaker decides to stop calling it for a while. They're complementary, and a breaker without the other two still lets the first few thousand calls through at full cost.

## Recognizing the Problem

- An outage in a feature nobody considers critical took down one that everybody does.
- During incidents, in-flight requests, goroutines or threads climb while throughput falls.
- Latency graphs flatten at a round number like 30 or 60 seconds. That's someone's default timeout, not a decision anyone made.
- Somewhere there's an HTTP, database or SDK client constructed with default settings.
- Every hop has the same timeout, copied from the same config template.
- A slow dependency correlates with errors on endpoints that don't call it.
- A downstream service logs requests completing after the caller already returned an error. Traces show child spans ending after their parent.
- Traffic to a failing service goes *up* during its outage.
- Recovery takes much longer than the root cause lasted.

## How It Works

### Why slow is worse than down

The quantity that matters is **occupancy**: how many of your resources are held at once. Little's law gives it directly:

```
in flight  =  arrival rate  ×  time each request is held
```

A dependency failing fast keeps the second term near zero, so occupancy stays tiny. A dependency failing slowly multiplies it by however long you're willing to wait. Every resource a waiting request holds fills up in proportion: request slots, goroutines or threads, memory for buffered bodies, file descriptors, and, the one most often forgotten, **a database connection checked out earlier in the handler and still held while waiting on something else**. That last one spreads the failure to every endpoint that needs the database, which is all of them.

So the goal isn't "make dependencies never fail." It's "make every dependency failure look like Monday": fast, bounded, and contained.

### Timeouts: every remote call has one

Library defaults are rarely safe. Go's `http.Client{}` waits forever. Its `DefaultTransport` allows 30 seconds to connect and has no limit on waiting for response headers. Many database drivers, SDKs and message clients default to no timeout or to minutes. **Every outbound call needs an explicit timeout**, and each of the following phases can hang on its own:

- **Connect**: establishing TCP (and TLS). Should be short, since a healthy host connects in milliseconds. A long wait here usually means an unreachable host.
- **Waiting for the response**: time to first byte. This is where a stuck dependency parks you.
- **The whole call**: including reading the body, which a dependency can trickle.

Choose the overall value from two directions, and it has to satisfy both:

1. **From the dependency's behavior**: a bit above its p99.9 latency when healthy, so the timeout fires on failure, not on normal variation.
2. **From your caller's budget**: whatever this call is allowed to spend out of the request's total.

If the dependency's p99.9 is larger than the budget allows, no timeout value works. That call doesn't belong on this path synchronously. Precompute or cache it, or move it off the request path (23.4).

Timeouts should also differ by call. The carousel gets 150 ms because the page is fine without it. A card authorization gets 8 seconds, because giving up early on a payment is its own kind of failure. And a timeout on a write is ambiguous: the write may have happened. Retrying it needs an idempotency key (10.2a, Module 17).

### Deadlines: one budget for the whole request

Independent per-hop timeouts can't express "the user stops waiting at 3 seconds." Each hop starts its own clock, so downstream work outlives the request:

```
independent timeouts, 3 s at every hop

  edge     ├────────── 3.0 s ──────────┤ 504 to the user
  pricing     ├─────────────── still waiting on tax ───────────────┤
  tax            ├──────────────── still computing ─────────────────────┤ 4.1 s: answer for nobody

one deadline, set at the edge and propagated

  edge     ├────────── 3.0 s ──────────┤ 504 to the user
  pricing     ├──── 2.9 s left ────────┤ cancelled
  tax            ├── 2.8 s left ───────┤ cancelled, and its database query aborted
```

A **deadline** is set once, where the request enters your system, and travels with it. Each hop does four things:

1. **Receives the remaining budget** from the caller, and takes the smaller of that and its own ceiling.
2. **Refuses to start** when too little is left to do anything useful, returning immediately instead of beginning work that can't finish.
3. **Forwards what remains** to every call it makes, minus a small margin for the network and for composing its own reply.
4. **Cancels in-flight work** when the deadline passes or the caller disconnects: outbound calls, database queries (with drivers that support cancellation), and computation that checks for it.

Send the budget as a **relative duration** ("1,840 ms left"), not an absolute timestamp. Machines' clocks disagree, and a deadline computed on one and checked on another would inherit the skew. gRPC does exactly this: its `grpc-timeout` header carries remaining time, and the Go implementation turns it into the server's context deadline automatically. Over HTTP you define a header yourself.

The same rule applies to queues. A job whose caller's deadline passed while it sat in the queue should be dropped, not run (23.4's stale backlogs).

Two exceptions to plan for:

- **Work that must outlive the request**, such as writing an audit record, an outbox row or a compensation step, must be detached from the request's cancellation on purpose, with its own timeout.
- **The public edge must not accept a deadline from the internet.** A client that sends "you have 1 ms" or "you have a week" is either broken or probing. Set the deadline at the edge from your own ceiling, the way 19.2b treats an incoming `traceparent`.

### Bulkheads: cap what each dependency can hold

A timeout bounds duration, so occupancy for a failing dependency is `rate × timeout`, which is still large. A **bulkhead** bounds occupancy directly: at most *N* calls to this dependency are in flight at once. When all *N* slots are taken, new calls fail immediately instead of waiting.

Together they bound the worst case: **one dependency can hold at most N slots, for at most the timeout each.** On Thursday, a 20-slot bulkhead on recommendations means 20 goroutines stuck on the carousel per pod, and 480 slots left for everything else. Checkout never notices.

Bulkheads come in several strengths:

- **A concurrency limit per dependency** (a semaphore). Cheap, in-process, and the right default. Fail fast when it's full. A bulkhead that queues callers is just a slower way to fill the same pod.
- **Separate connection pools per dependency or per workload.** A reporting job and the checkout path sharing one database pool is a bulkhead with no walls. Give them separate pools with separate limits.
- **Separate deployments for critical paths.** Run checkout on its own pods, even from the same code. Then it doesn't share memory, CPU, garbage-collection pauses or the shedder with product pages at all. It's the strongest bulkhead, and it costs real capacity.
- **Per-tenant partitions**, so one customer's traffic can't occupy everyone's slots. That's Module 5's noisy-neighbor problem, and it uses the same mechanism keyed differently.

Size a concurrency limit from the healthy case with headroom. Recommendations normally has `140 × 0.04 ≈ 6` calls in flight per pod, so 20 allows a 3× burst before anything is rejected. Too small, and the bulkhead rejects ordinary peaks. Too large, and it no longer protects anything. Watch its rejection count as closely as the dependency's error rate.

### Decide which failures are optional

Bulkheads and tight timeouts only help if the caller knows what to do when they fire. For each endpoint, classify each dependency:

- **Required**: its failure fails the request. Catalog data for a product page, payments for checkout.
- **Optional**: its failure removes a section. The carousel, a "customers also bought" strip, a loyalty-points badge.

An optional dependency gets a tight timeout, a bulkhead, and code that renders without it. A required one gets a timeout that fits the request's budget and a clear error. What the degraded response *contains*, including stale data, defaults and the risks of fallbacks, is 36.3b's subject.

### Retries multiply down the chain

Retries turn a slow dependency into an overloaded one. If each of four layers retries 3 times (4 attempts each), one user request can become up to `4⁴ = 256` calls at the bottom, arriving exactly when the bottom is slowest. 18.3 covers the fix: retry at exactly one layer, with a budget. 10.2a covers backoff and jitter.

Deadlines add one rule for microservices: **retry only if the remaining budget covers another full attempt.** A retry that will be cut off by the deadline halfway through adds load and can't succeed.

A related technique is **hedging** (Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale"). For an idempotent read, if the first attempt hasn't answered by the dependency's p95, send a second one to a different replica and take whichever answers first. It cuts tail latency sharply for a few percent more calls. Cap hedges at a small fraction of traffic, and never hedge writes. An uncapped hedge is a retry storm triggered by slowness, which is precisely when the dependency can least absorb it.

### Other ways failures cascade

Resource exhaustion inside one service is the common path, but not the only one:

- **Load redistribution.** Two of six pods run out of memory from hung requests and restart. The remaining four take 50% more traffic each, and they tip over too. Capacity needs headroom for losing instances *during* an incident, not just on a quiet day.
- **Recovery surges.** When the lock releases, every held request completes at once, retries arrive together, and caches are cold. The system can fail again on the way back up. 18.1 covers the herd, and 23.4 covers draining a backlog.
- **Shared infrastructure.** Services with perfect bulkheads between them still share a database, a cache cluster, DNS or a message broker. A bulkhead only isolates what it actually partitions.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Timeouts:** on every remote call, always. Database, HTTP, gRPC, cache, message broker, third-party SDK. No exceptions and no defaults.
- **Deadline propagation:** once a request crosses three or more hops, fans out, or triggers expensive downstream work such as heavy queries, report rendering or model inference, where abandoned work costs real capacity.
- **Per-dependency bulkheads:** when one process calls several dependencies and some of its traffic doesn't need all of them, which is nearly every service that serves more than one endpoint.
- **Optional dependencies with tight budgets:** whenever a page or response has parts it can render without.
- **Separate deployments:** when a small slice of traffic carries most of the value, like checkout, login or a paid API tier, and a full outage of it is much more expensive than the idle capacity.
- **Hedging:** idempotent reads with a long latency tail, from a dependency with enough replicas and spare capacity to absorb a few percent more calls.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Deadline propagation for two hops.** A timeout at each hop, with the inner one clearly smaller than the outer, is the whole solution.
- **Bulkheads between dependencies that every request needs.** If every endpoint calls catalog, partitioning catalog's slots from everything else protects nothing, because there's no traffic that doesn't need it. The timeout does the work.
- **Separate deployments before you've seen shared fate.** Per-dependency bulkheads handle most cases. Split pods when an incident shows the process itself (memory, garbage collection, the shedder) is what's shared.
- **Hedging a dependency that's already at capacity**, or any write. You'd be paying extra load to make an overload worse.
- **Batch jobs with no one waiting**, which share no pools with interactive traffic. A generous timeout and a retry policy are enough.

## Trade-offs

- **Timeouts create failures.** A timeout at p99.9 fails one healthy call in a thousand by design. Set it too tight and a dependency that's slow but working becomes an outage, and with retries, an overload.
- **Timeout values rot.** A dependency's p99 drifts as data and code change, and a value that fit last year cuts off normal traffic this year. Timeouts need to live in configuration, with a metric per call showing how often each one fires.
- **Writes become ambiguous.** A timed-out write may have succeeded. Every timeout on a write creates a reconciliation or idempotency problem.
- **Deadlines are a protocol every hop must honor.** One component that ignores the header, such as a queue, a third-party SDK, or a service in another team's language, breaks the chain silently. Everything past it goes back to independent clocks.
- **Bulkheads waste capacity.** Slots reserved for one dependency sit idle while another partition rejects requests. And sizing is a guess that needs revisiting as traffic changes.
- **Degraded responses hide failures.** A product page without its carousel returns `200`, so error-rate dashboards show nothing wrong. Unless degraded responses are counted as their own metric, optional dependencies can be broken for weeks.
- **More knobs, more paths.** Per-call timeouts, per-dependency limits and degraded rendering are all code paths that run mostly during incidents, which is when you'll discover they don't work. They need tests that inject slowness.

## Alternatives

- **Take the dependency off the request path.** The strongest bulkhead is not calling it. Precompute recommendations into a cache or the product document, and the page never waits on the recommendations service at all (Module 10, 23.4). It costs freshness and a pipeline.
- **Load shedding** (18.1) protects the process as a whole but can't choose which traffic survives unless requests carry a priority. With priorities, it can drop product pages before checkout. That's a coarser version of the same partitioning, decided at the door rather than per dependency.
- **Adaptive concurrency limits** derive the limit from observed latency instead of a fixed number, borrowing TCP congestion control: when latency rises, the limit shrinks. Netflix's `concurrency-limits` library and Envoy's adaptive concurrency filter work this way. They adjust to change without retuning, and they're harder to predict and explain.
- **A service mesh** (36.1a) can enforce timeouts, connection limits and outlier ejection in the proxy, uniformly across languages. It can't know which of your dependencies is optional for this endpoint, and it can't carry a deadline your code doesn't propagate.
- **Cell-based architecture** partitions whole stacks (services, databases, queues) so a failure is confined to the customers in one cell. It's a bulkhead at the scale of the system, with the operational cost to match.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 1: explicit timeouts everywhere.** Audit every client constructor: HTTP, database, cache, broker, SDKs. Give each a connect timeout and an overall timeout, and record a metric per call for timeouts fired. *Signal for the next step:* timeouts fire correctly during an incident, yet endpoints that don't use the slow dependency still fail.

**Level 2: classify dependencies and add bulkheads.** For each endpoint, mark each dependency required or optional. Give optional ones tight timeouts, a concurrency limit and a degraded rendering path, and count degraded responses. Split shared connection pools between interactive and batch work. *Signal:* traces show work continuing downstream after the caller returned, or timeouts that don't nest across a chain.

**Level 3: propagate deadlines.** Set a deadline at the edge, pass the remaining budget on every internal call, refuse work with too little budget, and plumb cancellation into database queries. Retry only within the remaining budget, at one layer. *Signal:* a critical endpoint still shares fate with bulk traffic through something process-wide, such as memory, garbage-collection pauses or the shedder.

**Level 4: physical isolation and proof.** Run critical paths on their own deployments. Then test it: inject **latency**, not errors, into each dependency in turn in a staging or game-day environment, and watch what else degrades. Error injection tests Monday. Latency injection tests Thursday.

## Implementation

Go, because its `context.Context` carries a deadline and a cancellation signal through every call in the standard library, including outbound HTTP and `database/sql`. Propagation is visible in the code, not a convention you hope each library follows. Three pieces: deadlines at the boundary, a bulkhead, and a product page that uses both.

**Deadlines in and out:**

```go
const deadlineHeader = "X-Deadline-Ms" // remaining budget, relative: clocks disagree

// Incoming: every request gets a deadline — the caller's, capped by our own ceiling.
// Install only on internal listeners; the public edge ignores the header.
func WithDeadline(ceiling time.Duration, next http.Handler) http.Handler {
	return http.HandlerFunc(func(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
		budget := ceiling
		if ms, err := strconv.Atoi(r.Header.Get(deadlineHeader)); err == nil && ms > 0 {
			budget = min(budget, time.Duration(ms)*time.Millisecond)
		}
		if budget < 20*time.Millisecond { // too little left to do anything useful
			http.Error(w, "deadline exceeded", http.StatusGatewayTimeout)
			return
		}
		ctx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(r.Context(), budget)
		defer cancel()
		next.ServeHTTP(w, r.WithContext(ctx))
	})
}

// Outgoing: forward what's left, minus a margin for the network and our own reply.
func Do(ctx context.Context, c *http.Client, req *http.Request) (*http.Response, error) {
	if dl, ok := ctx.Deadline(); ok {
		left := time.Until(dl) - 10*time.Millisecond
		if left <= 0 {
			return nil, context.DeadlineExceeded // don't start work nobody will wait for
		}
		req.Header.Set(deadlineHeader, strconv.FormatInt(left.Milliseconds(), 10))
	}
	return c.Do(req.WithContext(ctx))
}
```

> **Aside — cancellation comes for free, until you detach it.** `r.Context()` is cancelled when the caller's connection closes, so a caller that gives up cancels this request, which cancels every `Do` and every `db.QueryContext` beneath it. That's what you want for the request's own work, and exactly wrong for an audit write or an outbox insert that must happen anyway. Detach those deliberately, keeping the context's values (trace ID, tenant) but not its cancellation:
>
> ```go
> ctx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(context.WithoutCancel(r.Context()), 2*time.Second)
> ```
>
> The reverse mistake is more common: a goroutine started with `context.Background()` to "not block the request" silently escapes both the deadline and the cancellation, and becomes the orphan work from the tax incident.

**A bulkhead:**

```go
var ErrBulkheadFull = errors.New("bulkhead full")

type Bulkhead struct{ slots chan struct{} }

func NewBulkhead(n int) *Bulkhead { return &Bulkhead{slots: make(chan struct{}, n)} }

// Run executes f only if a slot is free right now. It never waits: a full
// bulkhead means this dependency already holds everything we agreed to give it.
func (b *Bulkhead) Run(ctx context.Context, f func(context.Context) error) error {
	select {
	case b.slots <- struct{}{}:
		defer func() { <-b.slots }()
		return f(ctx)
	default:
		return ErrBulkheadFull
	}
}
```

**The product page, with one required and one optional dependency:**

```go
var (
	recsBulkhead = NewBulkhead(20) // healthy: ~6 in flight per pod (140/s × 40 ms)
	recsClient   = &http.Client{   // never the zero value: it has no timeout at all
		Transport: &http.Transport{
			DialContext:           (&net.Dialer{Timeout: 100 * time.Millisecond}).DialContext,
			ResponseHeaderTimeout: 500 * time.Millisecond,
		},
		Timeout: 1 * time.Second, // backstop; the per-call context below is tighter
	}
)

func productPage(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
	ctx := r.Context() // carries the request's deadline from WithDeadline

	product, err := catalog.Get(ctx, productID(r)) // required: its failure fails the page
	if err != nil {
		writeError(w, err)
		return
	}

	// Optional: a tight budget of its own, inside whatever the request has left.
	var recs []Item
	rctx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(ctx, 150*time.Millisecond)
	defer cancel()
	err = recsBulkhead.Run(rctx, func(ctx context.Context) error {
		var e error
		recs, e = fetchRecs(ctx, recsClient, product.ID) // uses Do(), so the deadline travels
		return e
	})
	if err != nil {
		degraded.WithLabelValues("recs", reason(err)).Inc() // bulkhead_full | timeout | error
		recs = nil                                          // render without the carousel
	}

	render(w, product, recs)
}
```

> **Aside — the `200` that hides an outage.** This page returns `200` whether or not the carousel loaded, so request error rates will never show recommendations failing. The `degraded` counter is the only signal, and it needs its own alert. The `reason` label matters too. `timeout` and `bulkhead_full` rising together means recommendations is slow: calls pile up to the 150 ms limit, which at 140 a second is about 21 in flight, just over the 20 slots. `bulkhead_full` rising alone means it's answering at normal speed but traffic outgrew the limit, so resize it. `timeout` rising alone means it's slow at low traffic.

With these three pieces, Thursday replays like this: product pages hold at most 20 goroutines per pod on recommendations, each for at most 150 ms, render without the carousel, and increment a counter. Checkout never sees a `503`.

- **Michael T. Nygard, _Release It!_, 2nd ed.** (Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2018). The stability antipatterns (integration points, chain reactions, cascading failures, blocked threads) and the patterns that answer them, including Timeouts and Bulkheads by name.

- **gRPC documentation, "Deadlines"** (grpc.io/docs/guides/deadlines). Relative deadlines on the wire and automatic propagation, as a reference design for your own HTTP header.
- **Sameer Ajmani, "Go Concurrency Patterns: Context,"** The Go Blog, 2014, and the `net/http` documentation for `Client.Timeout` and `Transport`'s individual timeouts.
- **Netflix `concurrency-limits`** (GitHub) and **Envoy's adaptive concurrency filter**, for limits that adjust themselves.
