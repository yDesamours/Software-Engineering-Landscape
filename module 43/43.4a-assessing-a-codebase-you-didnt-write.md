# Assessing a Codebase You Didn't Write: Health Reviews and Technical Due Diligence

_Module 43 · Finishing Software · 43.4a_

## The Problem

Suppose your company makes booking software for veterinary clinics and agrees to buy a smaller competitor: a practice-management product used by about 600 clinics. Most use the hosted version; 90 larger clinics run an on-premises edition on their own servers. The competitor has eight engineers. Your engineering team gets two weeks and read access to the repositories to say whether the technology is sound.

The team does what seems thorough:

- Runs a static analysis scan: maintainability grade B, 68% test coverage, a moderate number of issues.
- Reads the architecture document: six services, each with its own data store, a clean diagram.
- Spends two hours on a call with the CTO, who answers every question confidently.
- Watches a demo, which goes smoothly.

The report says "well built, moderate technical debt, integration in about four months." The deal closes. Then:

**Day 12.** The engineer behind the lab-results integration resigns. It imports test results from diagnostic labs, and clinics treat it as essential. Git history shows he wrote 81% of it and 40% of the scheduling core. Nobody had looked.

**Day 30.** Legal finds that the on-premises installer bundles a reporting library under the GPL. Distributing it to 90 clinics inside a proprietary product means replacing the library or taking on obligations nobody wants.

**Day 45.** Nobody at your company can build the on-premises edition. Its installer is produced on one Windows machine in the competitor's office, with a licensed packaging tool tied to that machine. Reproducing the build takes three weeks.

**Day 60.** The six "services" share one database and are always deployed together. The diagram showed how the system was meant to work.

**Day 75.** The first full hosting bill arrives at 2.6 times what the financial statements showed. Part of the platform had been running on startup credits that ended with the acquisition.

**Day 90.** Twenty-two of the 90 on-premises clinics turn out to run their own customer-specific branches, each with small changes someone agreed to years ago. Every fix has to be applied up to 23 times.

The integration estimate grows from four months to fourteen.

None of this was hidden. It was in the git history, the dependency tree, the build process, the cloud account and the branch list. The review measured the **code**, when the decision depended on the **system around the code**: who knows it, whether it can be built and run, what it's legally allowed to contain, and what it really costs. It also took the CTO's answers as findings, not as claims to check.

## What It Is

A **codebase assessment** is a time-boxed, evidence-based review of a system you didn't build, done to inform a specific decision. When the decision is a purchase or an investment, it's called **technical due diligence**. The same method applies when you inherit a system from a departing team, take over from an agency, choose whether to adopt a source-available product, or decide between rewriting and keeping something.

What separates it from a code review or a quality scan is its question. It doesn't ask "is this code good?" It asks **"what will owning this cost us, and what could hurt us?"** Code quality is one input to that, and often not the largest.

It's also different from the continuous health tracking your own team does on its own code (31.1). An assessment starts with no trust, no context and a deadline, and it has to separate what's claimed from what's shown.

## Recognizing the Problem

- You're about to make a decision (buy, adopt, inherit, rewrite) about a system you didn't build.
- The evidence offered is a demo, a diagram, a quality dashboard and a conversation.
- Nobody on your side has built, deployed or restored the system yet.
- "It's all documented" or "only Sam knows that part" comes up.
- After taking over a system, you keep being surprised, and your estimates for changing it keep being wrong.
- You can't say what the system costs to run, per customer.
- Nobody can list who has production access.
- You don't know which licenses its dependencies use, or whether any customer has a special version.

## How It Works

### 1. Start from the decision

Write down what the decision needs to know before opening any code. For an acquisition, typically:

- **Can we run it?** Build, deploy, operate, recover.
- **Can we change it?** Who understands it, how safely it can be modified.
- **Is it ours to ship?** Licenses, ownership of code written by contractors, copied code.
- **What does it really cost?** Hosting, third-party services, people, customer-specific work.
- **What could hurt us?** Security, data handling, compliance, single points of failure.
- **How far is it from what we were told?** Architecture, scale, customers.

Each question gets an answer with evidence, a confidence level and, where possible, a cost range. Anything you couldn't check is listed as such, not left out.

### 2. Four sources of evidence

Each source catches problems the others miss:

| Source | What it shows | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| **Code** (static analysis, reading) | Structure, complexity, obvious security flaws, dependency list | Who knows it, how it runs, what it costs |
| **History** (version control) | Hotspots, who knows what, change coupling, how work actually flows | Knowledge that never became commits |
| **Runtime** (production telemetry, bills, incidents, access lists) | Real dependencies, load, cost, reliability | Why things are the way they are |
| **People** (interviews, watching work being done) | Intent, history, workarounds, what worries them | Anything they don't know or won't say |

The vet review used only the first and the fourth. Git history would have shown the knowledge concentration; the cloud account and the deploy process would have shown the shared database and the real cost.

**Triangulate.** Treat each interview answer as a claim to confirm from another source. "Services are independent" is checked against deploy logs and database connections. "We deploy daily" is checked against release tags. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

### 3. Who knows what

Git records who changed what. From it you can estimate knowledge concentration. The best-known method, from Avelino and colleagues, works like this:

- Give each developer a **degree-of-authorship** score for each file, based on whether they created it, how often they changed it, and how often others did.
- Treat the top scorers for each file as its authors.
- Repeatedly remove the developer who's an author of the most files, until more than half the files have no authors left.

The number removed is the **truck factor** (or bus factor). Across open-source studies, a truck factor of one or two is common. More useful than the headline number is **which** files belong to one person, and whether that person is staying. For the vet product, the lab integration had one author, which made his retention a question for the deal itself.

Commit history undercounts people who contribute by reviewing, designing or operating rather than committing. A comparison of truck-factor algorithms that asked developers directly found this in about a third of the projects studied. Interviews fill that gap.

The same history gives you **hotspots** (31.1): files that change most often and are most complex, which is where the cost of change concentrates. Also look at **change coupling**: files in different services that always change in the same commit, a sign the boundary on the diagram isn't real.

### 4. Can it be built and run?

The most revealing single test is to **build and deploy the system from a clean checkout, without help from its authors**. It checks the documentation, tooling, secrets management and any hidden machines in one go. Anything that needs "ask Maria for the file" is a finding.

Close behind it:

- **Watch a real deploy**, and a rollback, rather than hearing about one.
- **Restore a backup** into an empty environment (24.1a). A backup that's never been restored is a hope.
- **Read the last year of incidents** and on-call pages: what breaks, how often, and who fixes it.

### 5. Is it legally yours to ship?

This question is invisible to code-quality tools and can be expensive. Check:

- **Licenses of every dependency**, including transitive ones, against how the product is distributed. Copyleft licenses (GPL) matter mostly when you ship software to customers, as with the on-premises edition. Network-copyleft licenses (AGPL) can also apply to hosted services. This is common: Black Duck's 2026 audit report found license conflicts in 68% of the 947 commercial codebases it examined, and in 94% of acquisition deals in its M&A analysis.
- **Code that isn't from a package**: copied snippets, vendored libraries with their license files removed, and increasingly code generated by AI assistants that may reproduce licensed code.
- **Who owns the code**: whether contractors and early contributors assigned their rights to the company.

A dependency inventory and software bill of materials (31.2b) make the first part mechanical. The others need legal review. The engineer's job is to produce the facts for it.

### 6. What does it really cost?

Operating cost is often where the biggest surprises are. Look at:

- **The actual bills**, all accounts, including credits, discounts and anything paid by personal cards.
- **Cost per customer**, and how it grows. A product that looks profitable at 600 clinics may not be at 6,000.
- **Third-party contracts** that end or reprice with a change of ownership.
- **Customer-specific versions**: branches, forks, configuration flags only one customer uses, and contractual promises about them. Each one multiplies the cost of every change.
- **Toil**: manual steps someone does weekly that aren't in any system.

### 7. Claimed architecture versus real architecture

Compare the documented design with runtime evidence: which services talk to which (from traces or network data, 36.4), which databases each one connects to, and what gets deployed together. Differences aren't necessarily bad; systems drift (31.4b). They matter because every plan built on the diagram inherits its errors.

### 8. Security and access

A quick pass rather than a full audit:

- Secrets in the repository or its history.
- Who has production access, including former staff and contractors.
- Authentication and tenant isolation on the main paths.
- Where personal data goes, compared with what customers were told (34.1a).
- Known vulnerabilities in dependencies, prioritized by whether they're reachable (31.2b).

### 9. Report findings as decisions, not grades

A letter grade or a score hides what the decision-maker needs. Each finding should say:

- **What**, in one sentence, and the **evidence** for it.
- **Severity**, in terms of the decision. For an acquisition: _deal-breaker_, _affects price or terms_ (such as retention for a key engineer, or an escrow for a license fix), _goes into the integration plan_, or _noted_.
- **Confidence**: verified, likely, or claimed but not checked.
- **Cost**: a range, and what it depends on.

Separate **messy** from **expensive**. Ugly code in a module that never changes costs almost nothing (31.3a). A clean module with one author who's leaving can cost a lot.

A two-week assessment usually runs like this:

```
days 1–2   questions written, access requested, build from clean started
days 3–7   history, dependencies, runtime, bills, incidents   → hypotheses
days 8–9   interviews that test the hypotheses, not open-ended ones
day 10     findings with evidence, severity, confidence, cost; list of what wasn't checked
```

Interviewing last means you arrive with specific questions ("git shows one person wrote the lab integration; who else could change it?") instead of general ones that invite general answers.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Acquisitions and investments** where software is a large part of the value.
- **Taking over a system**: from an agency, a departing team, or a reorganization.
- **Before deciding to rewrite**, so the decision is about measured costs, not frustration.
- **Choosing to depend on a product** whose source you can see: an open-source platform, a vendor's source-available product, an internal platform from another division.
- **Joining as a new technical lead**, as a structured first month rather than impressions.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Small or throwaway code**: a script, a prototype you'll replace, a tool with one user.
- **When the decision is already made** and the cost doesn't change it. Then do onboarding, not assessment.
- **Your own team's system**, repeatedly. Continuous health signals (31.1, 31.3a) do this better than periodic reviews.
- **A full-depth review everywhere.** Most systems deserve depth in a few areas the questions point at, and a quick pass elsewhere.

## Trade-offs

- **It's a snapshot.** It describes the system during two weeks. People leave, contracts change, and the findings age quickly.
- **Access is usually limited.** Sellers restrict what can be seen before a deal, often for good reasons. Many findings end up "likely" rather than "verified," and the report has to say so.
- **The people you assess may become your colleagues.** A review that feels like an interrogation damages the relationship before it starts, and makes people less candid.
- **Metrics give false comfort.** A scan produces numbers quickly, and numbers feel like evidence. Most costly findings come from history, runtime and legal checks that take longer.
- **Tools are noisy.** License and vulnerability scanners over-report, and every finding needs a human to check it.
- **Someone has to act on it.** An assessment with no one responsible for its findings changes nothing.
- **It costs senior time.** A good two-week review takes several experienced engineers away from other work, or a specialist firm's fee.

## Alternatives

- **Contractual protection.** In acquisitions, warranties and indemnities make the seller liable for certain problems. That's financial protection, not knowledge: it pays for damage, and it doesn't tell you how to integrate.
- **A trial period.** Run the system, or a pilot of the deal, before committing fully. The best evidence there is, and often not available.
- **Keep the original team.** Retention agreements reduce knowledge risk more than any analysis, at a cost, and only for as long as people stay.
- **A quick checklist.** For low-stakes decisions, a dozen yes/no questions about the build, tests, deploys and bug tracking (in the spirit of the Joel Test) catches the worst problems in an hour.
- **A specialist firm.** Independent, experienced and fast, and far from how your team will actually work with the system. Often paired with an internal review.

## How to Achieve It

**Step 1: write the decision questions** and the severity levels before touching code. _Signal to move on:_ everyone agrees what the review is for.

**Step 2: try to build and deploy from clean**, and pull the dependency inventory. These two catch the most per hour. _Signal:_ you know whether the system can be operated by someone new.

**Step 3: analyze the history**: knowledge concentration, hotspots, change coupling. _Signal:_ you have a list of people and areas that carry risk.

**Step 4: look at runtime and money**: bills, incidents, access lists, traces, customer-specific versions. _Signal:_ you have hypotheses where claims and evidence disagree.

**Step 5: interview to test those hypotheses**, then write the findings with evidence, confidence and cost.

**Step 6: turn findings into the first 90 days**: retention conversations, license replacements, reproducible builds. That's where the assessment pays off.

## Implementation

TypeScript, in two pieces: knowledge concentration from git history, and findings kept as data so the report can't lose its evidence.

The first estimates who knows each file and the truck factor, following Avelino and colleagues. Input comes from `git log --no-merges --format='--%an' --name-status`, parsed into one record per change.

```ts
type Change = { author: string; file: string; created: boolean };

// Degree of authorship (Fritz et al.), as used by Avelino et al.
const doa = (fa: number, dl: number, ac: number) =>
  3.293 + 1.098 * fa + 0.164 * dl - 0.321 * Math.log(1 + ac);

export function authorsByFile(changes: Change[]): Map<string, Set<string>> {
  const stats = new Map<string, Map<string, { fa: number; dl: number }>>();
  for (const c of changes) {
    const perFile = stats.get(c.file) ?? new Map();
    const s = perFile.get(c.author) ?? { fa: 0, dl: 0 };
    s.dl += 1;
    if (c.created) s.fa = 1;
    perFile.set(c.author, s);
    stats.set(c.file, perFile);
  }

  const result = new Map<string, Set<string>>();
  for (const [file, perFile] of stats) {
    const total = [...perFile.values()].reduce((n, s) => n + s.dl, 0);
    const scores = [...perFile].map(([a, s]) => [a, doa(s.fa, s.dl, total - s.dl)] as const);
    const max = Math.max(...scores.map(([, d]) => d));
    // An author has a high absolute score and is close to the top scorer.
    const authors = scores.filter(([, d]) => d > 3.293 && d / max > 0.75).map(([a]) => a);
    if (authors.length) result.set(file, new Set(authors));
  }
  return result;
}

export function truckFactor(authors: Map<string, Set<string>>) {
  const removed: string[] = [];
  const orphaned = () =>
    [...authors.values()].filter(set => [...set].every(a => removed.includes(a))).length;

  while (orphaned() <= authors.size / 2) {
    const counts = new Map<string, number>();
    for (const set of authors.values())
      for (const a of set) if (!removed.includes(a)) counts.set(a, (counts.get(a) ?? 0) + 1);
    const top = [...counts].sort((x, y) => y[1] - x[1])[0];
    if (!top) break;
    removed.push(top[0]);
  }
  return { truckFactor: removed.length, keyPeople: removed };
}
```

> **Aside: history lies in known ways.** Squash merges credit one person with a team's work. Bots, mass reformatting commits and file moves create false authorship. People commit under several names. Before trusting the numbers, map aliases with a `.mailmap`, exclude bot accounts and known reformat commits, and run `git log` with `--follow` or rename detection for important files. Then treat the output as a list of hypotheses to take into interviews, not a verdict.

> **Aside: the whole-repo number matters less than the files.** A truck factor of three for the repository can hide a critical integration with one author. For an assessment, the more useful output is the files whose only authors have left or are at risk of leaving, sorted by how often they change and how important they are.

The second piece keeps each finding together with its evidence, so the report can always answer "how do we know?"

```ts
type Severity = "deal-breaker" | "price-or-terms" | "integration-plan" | "noted";
type Confidence = "verified" | "likely" | "claimed";

type Finding = {
  area: "run" | "change" | "legal" | "cost" | "security" | "architecture";
  summary: string;
  evidence: string[];          // commands, links, documents, interview notes
  severity: Severity;
  confidence: Confidence;
  costRange?: [number, number]; // engineer-weeks, or currency
};

const RANK: Record<Severity, number> =
  { "deal-breaker": 0, "price-or-terms": 1, "integration-plan": 2, "noted": 3 };

export function report(findings: Finding[]) {
  const unsupported = findings.filter(f => f.evidence.length === 0);
  if (unsupported.length) throw new Error(`Findings without evidence: ${unsupported.map(f => f.summary)}`);

  return {
    ordered: [...findings].sort((a, b) => RANK[a.severity] - RANK[b.severity]),
    // What the decision rests on but nobody has checked.
    unverifiedSerious: findings.filter(f => f.confidence === "claimed" && RANK[f.severity] <= 1),
  };
}
```

> **Aside: "claimed" is a legitimate confidence.** Many facts can't be checked before a deal closes. Recording them as claimed, not dropping them or quietly promoting them to verified, is what lets someone decide whether to ask for a warranty or a retention agreement instead. In the vet acquisition, "services have separate databases" should have appeared here as a claimed finding with no evidence behind it.

## Further Reading

- **Adam Tornhill, _Your Code as a Crime Scene_ (2nd edition) and _Software Design X-Rays_.** Using version-control history to find hotspots, change coupling and knowledge concentration. The best practical source for section 3.
- **Guilherme Avelino, Leonardo Passos, Andre Hora and Marco Tulio Valente, "A Novel Approach for Estimating Truck Factors"** (ICPC 2016), and **Mívian Ferreira et al., "Algorithms for Estimating Truck Factors: A Comparative Study"** (Software Quality Journal, 2019). The algorithm used here, and its limits.
- **Black Duck, _Open Source Security and Risk Analysis_ (2026) and _Open Source Risk in M&A by the Numbers_.** Annual data on licenses, vulnerabilities and outdated components in audited commercial codebases.
- **OpenChain (ISO/IEC 5230)** and **SPDX**. The standard for an open-source license compliance program, and the format for recording what a product contains.
- **Michael Feathers, _Working Effectively with Legacy Code_.** For what comes after the assessment: making safe changes to code you didn't write.
- **DORA** (dora.dev). The four delivery measures are a useful, quick check on how a team actually works.
- **In this collection:** 24.1a (backups and restores), 31.1 (hotspots), 31.2b (dependency vulnerabilities and SBOMs), 31.3a (debt as interest), 31.3b (refactoring safely), 31.4b (architecture drift), 34.1a (data maps), 36.4 (dependency maps from traces).

A sibling in **Go** could be worth writing, though it isn't yet: running the history analysis over dozens of repositories at once, with concurrent `git` processes, is the realistic case for a larger acquisition, and it's where Go's concurrency would change the shape of the tool.

---

The habit to take from this: whenever someone tells you something about a system — "it's well tested," "the services are independent," "deploys are routine" — ask what would show it. The gap between the claim and the evidence is usually where the cost is.
