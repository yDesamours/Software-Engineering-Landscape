# Domain-Driven Design Essentials

_Module 2.3 — Software Architecture Patterns_

## The Problem

An e-commerce platform, six years old. Open `Order.java`. It has 84 fields.

That didn't happen through carelessness. It happened one reasonable request at a time. The warehouse needed to know pick-location and package dimensions, so those went on the order. Finance needed tax jurisdiction, deferred-revenue date, and a purchase-order reference. Support needed a free-text note and a flag for "do not auto-cancel." Marketing needed the attribution campaign. Each of those was three fields and a migration, and each was obviously the right place to put them, because they're facts about an order.

Now look at what the class is like to work with.

At any moment, most of the fields are null. Which ones depends on where the object came from: an order created by the checkout flow has no `pickLocation` and no `invoiceNumber`; one loaded by the finance report has both but has had its lines lazily loaded and then detached. There is no such thing as a valid `Order`. There are six different partially-populated shapes, and the only way to know which one you have is to know who handed it to you.

Then there's `isShipped`. Warehouse sets it when the pick list closes. Billing reads it to decide whether an invoice can be issued. Those are not the same moment — a split shipment closes the first pick list while half the order is still in the building — and the day someone "fixed" the flag to match the warehouse's meaning, invoices started going out for goods that hadn't left.

And there's a corruption bug nobody can reproduce. Some orders have a `total` that doesn't equal the sum of their lines. The write paths are: `addLine()`, which recalculates; a bulk discount job that updates `total` directly for speed; a support tool that deletes a line row without touching the order; and a 2019 data-repair script that still runs nightly. Four writers, one invariant, nothing enforcing it.

Two distinct problems are tangled together here, and they have different answers.

The first is that **one word is being made to mean several things at once.** "Order" means a customer's intent to Sales, a set of physical items to Fulfilment, and a claim on revenue to Billing. Forcing those into one class means every change has to satisfy three audiences who were never in the same room.

The second is that **nothing owns the rules.** "Total equals the sum of lines" is a fact about the business that lives nowhere in particular, so it's everybody's job and therefore nobody's.

## What It Is

Domain-Driven Design is a set of practices for shaping software around the structure of the business rather than around the structure of the database or the framework. It has two halves, and they answer the two problems above.

**Strategic DDD** answers *where do meanings end.* Its central idea is the **bounded context**: an explicit boundary inside which a model and the words describing it mean one consistent thing. Sales, Fulfilment, and Billing each get their own Order. They are different models with different fields and different rules, and that is not duplication to be eliminated — it is the accurate representation of a business where those three departments genuinely mean different things.

**Tactical DDD** answers *where do invariants end.* It's a small vocabulary of building blocks — entities, value objects, aggregates, repositories, domain events — whose job is to give each business rule exactly one place to live and make violating it difficult.

Underneath both is the **ubiquitous language**: the code uses the words the business uses, with the meanings the business gives them, inside each context. Not a naming convention — a claim that if the business says "a policy lapses" and the code says `setStatus(3)`, you have a translation step happening in somebody's head, and translation steps in heads are where misunderstandings live.

Two things this is not:

**DDD is not the tactical patterns.** This is the most consequential misunderstanding in the field. Teams import aggregates and repositories, apply them to the single 84-field canonical model, and end up with the same tangle plus a lot more ceremony. The patterns are downstream of the boundaries; without the boundaries they mostly add cost. If you're going to do half of DDD, do the strategic half.

**DDD is not microservices.** Bounded contexts are logical boundaries; they run fine inside one deployable. They do happen to be the best available candidates when you *do* split (Module 2.1), which is why the two get mentioned in the same breath — but "we're doing DDD" and "we're doing microservices" are answers to different questions.

It also isn't a competitor to layered architecture (Module 2.2). Layering says where code lives; DDD says what the code in the domain layer should be shaped like. The `canBook` function in that article was already doing tactical DDD without the vocabulary.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A central class with dozens of fields, most of them null most of the time, and no way to say what "valid" means.
- The same word means different things to two teams, and meetings begin with five minutes of disambiguating it.
- Data reaches states that "shouldn't be possible," and nobody can say which write path produced them.
- A status enum with fourteen values and an unwritten rule about which transitions are legal.
- Business people describe a process using words that appear nowhere in the codebase.
- Objects with a full set of setters, constructible in states the business would reject.
- You can't answer "what is a single transaction allowed to change?" without a discussion.
- Any change to the shared model needs sign-off from three teams, so nobody changes it and workarounds accumulate beside it.
- Long `if (type == …)` ladders in the middle of business logic, each branch belonging to a different department.

The first and second signals point at the strategic half. The third through sixth point at the tactical half. Most systems have both.

## How It Works

### Bounded contexts

A bounded context is a boundary inside which one model applies and one vocabulary holds. Cross it and both change.

```
   SALES CONTEXT           FULFILMENT CONTEXT        BILLING CONTEXT
   ─────────────           ──────────────────        ───────────────
   Order                   Shipment                  Invoice
    · customer              · pick location           · bill-to party
    · lines, prices         · weight, dimensions      · line totals, tax
    · promo, discount       · carrier, tracking       · payment terms
    · placed / cancelled    · picked / dispatched     · issued / settled

        └──── OrderPlaced ─────▶ │
                                 └──── Dispatched ─────▶
        (each boundary translates; no model is shared across them)
```

One real-world order exists here as three objects with three lifecycles. Fulfilment doesn't know about promo codes. Billing doesn't know about pick locations. Sales doesn't know an order can be split across two vans.

The instinct to unify these is strong and should be resisted, because the unification is what produced the 84-field class. The duplication is real — a customer's name may be stored in two contexts — and it is cheaper than the coupling it replaces.

Contexts relate to each other in a handful of recognized ways, collectively a **context map**. The one that earns its name in daily work is the **anti-corruption layer**: a translation shell around a context you don't control — a legacy system, a vendor API, a team that won't change — whose job is to stop their concepts leaking into your model. If your shipping code contains a field called `custRefCd2` because that's what the carrier's API calls it, you needed an ACL and didn't build one.

Finding contexts is mostly listening. Where does the same word change meaning? Where does a handoff happen between teams? Where does one department's process end and another's begin? EventStorming is the structured version of this and it works well; the mechanism is a wall, sticky notes for events in past tense, and domain experts in the room.

### Value objects

A **value object** is defined entirely by its attributes, has no identity, and is immutable. Money. A date range. An email address. A postal address. A quantity.

Two things make them worth the trouble.

First, **validation has a single place to happen**: the constructor. If `EmailAddress` cannot be constructed from a malformed string, then no function anywhere needs to check whether an email is well-formed. The check moved from every use site to one definition site.

Second, **behaviour follows the data**. `Money.plus(Money)` can refuse to add euros to dollars. `DateRange.overlaps(DateRange)` is written once instead of being reimplemented, slightly differently, in six places. The scattered helper functions that operate on primitives collapse onto the type they belong to.

The failure they cure is primitive obsession — a signature like `void transfer(String from, String to, long amount)` where every argument is swappable with every other and the compiler is indifferent.

### Entities

An **entity** has identity that survives change. Two customers with identical names are different customers; a customer who changes their name is the same customer. So equality is by identifier, not by fields.

That's the whole distinction, and the practical consequence is: if you find yourself asking "are these two objects the same?" and the honest answer is "if their fields match," you have a value object and should treat it as one — immutable, no ID, freely copied.

### Aggregates

The most useful idea in tactical DDD, and the one worth understanding precisely.

An **aggregate** is a cluster of entities and value objects treated as one unit for the purposes of consistency, with one entity designated the **root**. Four rules follow:

1. **Outside code holds a reference only to the root.** Nothing else in the cluster is reachable from outside.
2. **The root enforces the invariants.** Every change goes through a method on the root, so there is no path to an inconsistent state.
3. **The aggregate is the transaction boundary.** One transaction modifies one aggregate. Everything inside it is immediately consistent; everything outside is eventually consistent.
4. **Other aggregates are referenced by identifier, not by object.** An `Order` holds a `CustomerId`, not a `Customer`.

Rule 3 is the one that turns this from a code-organization idea into an architectural one. "What must be true the instant the transaction commits?" is a business question, and its answer is your aggregate boundary. Order total equalling the sum of its lines must be true instantly — one aggregate. An order and the customer's loyalty points do not have to agree instantly — two aggregates, reconciled by an event.

Rule 4 is what keeps aggregates small, and small is the thing to aim for. A large aggregate means loading a large object graph to change one field, and it means every concurrent write to any part of it contends with every other. The classic mistake is sizing aggregates by what a screen displays rather than by what must be consistent — which produces a `Customer` aggregate containing every order that customer ever placed, and a lock on it every time anything happens.

### Repositories

A **repository** is a collection-like interface for loading and storing aggregates — one repository per aggregate root, never one per table. `Orders.byId(orderId)` returns a fully-formed `Order` with its lines. There is no `OrderLines.byId()`, because an order line has no independent existence.

Structurally this is the port from Module 2.2: the interface is declared in the domain, implemented in infrastructure. What DDD adds is the constraint on its granularity. A repository per table reintroduces exactly the problem aggregates exist to solve, because it gives outside code a way to load and modify a child without going through the root.

### Domain events

A **domain event** records that something meaningful happened, named in the business's language, in the past tense: `OrderPlaced`, `PaymentDeclined`, `PolicyLapsed`. Not `OrderTableUpdated`.

Events are how consistency is achieved *across* aggregates without one enormous transaction. The order aggregate commits and emits `OrderPlaced`; a handler in the loyalty context awards points; another in fulfilment creates a shipment. Each of those is its own transaction on its own aggregate. The system is consistent within moments rather than within a single commit, and in exchange nothing is locking anything else.

They're also the natural translation currency between bounded contexts — the arrows in the diagram above — which is why this is the same rung as L4 in Module 2.1's ladder, and why Module 37 picks the thread up at scale.

## When It's Appropriate

**When the domain is the hard part.** Insurance underwriting, freight routing, payroll across jurisdictions, clinical scheduling, lending, trading settlement. The test: could a competent engineer read the requirements and understand them, or would they need a domain expert explaining for several weeks? If it's the latter, the complexity is real and needs somewhere to live.

**When multiple teams collide over vocabulary.** Three departments, one shared noun, constant misunderstanding. Contexts are worth drawing here even if you adopt nothing else.

**When invariants matter more than throughput.** A booking system that occasionally double-books, or a ledger that occasionally doesn't balance, has a correctness problem that no amount of performance work compensates for.

**When the system will outlive several of its technologies.** Rules modelled explicitly migrate. Rules smeared across controllers and stored procedures get rewritten.

**When you have access to domain experts.** This is a prerequisite, not a nice-to-have. A ubiquitous language is discovered by talking to people who know the domain; you cannot derive one from a database schema. A team doing DDD with no expert access is building a model of its own guesses.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

**Technically hard, domain simple.** A CDN, a log pipeline, a video transcoder, a metrics store. The difficulty is in latency, throughput, and failure handling. There is very little domain to model, and aggregates would be ceremony around a byte stream.

**CRUD.** If the "business rule" is "save what the user typed," a value object for every field and a repository per aggregate produce cost with no return. Module 2.2's point applies here too: the depth should match the feature, and most applications are lumpy.

**No access to domain experts.** Covered above, but worth restating as a disqualifier rather than a caveat.

**Tactical patterns on a canonical model.** The most common way DDD fails in practice. Aggregates, value objects and repositories get bolted onto the same 84-field `Order` that everyone shares, and the result is worse than before: the tangle survives, the ceremony is new, and the team concludes DDD doesn't work. If contexts haven't been drawn, the tactical patterns don't have boundaries to operate within.

**Pre-product-market-fit.** The rules change weekly because nobody knows them yet. A carefully modelled domain has to be re-modelled every time, and the modelling is the expensive part.

**Aggregates sized by screens.** If the boundary was chosen because that's what the detail page shows, it will be too big, it will be contended, and it will make every write slow. Size by invariant.

## Trade-offs

**The main cost is organizational, not technical.** Sustained access to domain experts, workshops, and a team willing to change its vocabulary. Most failed DDD adoptions fail here, not in the code.

**Deliberate duplication feels wrong and gets attacked.** Three `Customer` classes in three contexts will look like an obvious refactoring opportunity to every new engineer and to every architect doing a review. Somebody will unify them, and the 84-field class will start growing again. This needs to be an explicitly documented decision or it will not survive.

**Translation layers are real code.** Every context boundary needs mapping, and mappings drift from the models they connect. An ACL around a vendor API is a thing you own and maintain forever.

**Aggregate boundaries are expensive to change.** They're transaction boundaries, so moving one changes what is atomic — which changes your consistency guarantees, which changes what the UI can promise. Getting this wrong early and discovering it late is the most painful mistake in tactical DDD.

**Eventual consistency leaks into the product.** Once loyalty points update a second after the order is placed, someone has to decide what the screen says in that second. That's a product conversation, not just an engineering one, and it recurs for every cross-aggregate rule.

**Reference-by-ID costs queries.** You can't join across aggregates through object graphs, so you make more round trips, or you build read models specifically for display (Module 2.4).

**ORMs fight aggregate design.** JPA and Hibernate want no-arg constructors, field mutability, and lazily-loaded collections. A root with a private collection and no setters is exactly what they're least comfortable with, and the compromises — a protected constructor here, a `@Access(FIELD)` there — are small individually and add up to a model that's partly shaped by the persistence framework after all.

**Vocabulary overhead.** Teams new to this spend real time arguing whether something is an entity or a value object. The arguments are usually not the valuable part; the boundary conversations are.

## Alternatives

**Transaction scripts over a relational schema.** For thin domains, the shape from Module 2.2: a procedure per use case, straightforward data access, no domain model. Cheaper, and for most CRUD it's the correct answer.

**Let the database enforce the invariants.** Underrated and frequently the right call. A `CHECK` constraint, a foreign key, a unique index, or a generated column enforces "total equals the sum of lines" more reliably than any object graph, because it holds against the repair script and the ad-hoc `UPDATE` too. Aggregates protect invariants from *your application's* code paths; constraints protect them from everything. Using both is normal, and if you can only have one, the constraint is the stronger guarantee.

**The strategic half alone.** Draw the contexts, agree the language, translate at the boundaries — and model each context simply inside. This has an excellent cost-to-benefit ratio and is what a large share of teams should actually do. Most of the 84-field pain came from the missing boundary, not the missing aggregate.

**Functional domain modelling.** Make illegal states unrepresentable with types instead of with encapsulation: sum types for status, so that a `DraftOrder` and a `PlacedOrder` are different types with different available operations, and "place an already-placed order" is a compile error rather than a runtime exception. Same goal, fewer objects, and a better fit in languages with good algebraic data types.

**Event sourcing** is often mentioned here but isn't an alternative — it's a persistence choice that composes with aggregates, and Module 2.4 covers it.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — One canonical model, anemic, shared by everyone.** The starting point.

**L1 — Name the contexts.** No code changes. A workshop, a whiteboard, a list of terms that mean different things in different departments, and a diagram of the handoffs. A day or two of work, and it immediately explains several long-running disagreements.

*Next when:* you can name the contexts but the code doesn't reflect them anywhere.

**L2 — Introduce value objects for the primitives that keep causing bugs.** `Money`, `EmailAddress`, `DateRange`, `Quantity`. Entirely local, no architectural commitment, and the payoff is immediate because these are usually where the recurring bugs are.

*Next when:* the bugs that remain are about state changing inconsistently rather than about bad inputs.

**L3 — Give one violated invariant an owner.** Pick the rule that keeps breaking. Make one class the root: private collections, no setters, all mutation through named methods. One aggregate, done properly, teaches the team more than a repo-wide conversion.

*Next when:* code outside the root can still load and modify its children.

**L4 — Repositories per aggregate.** One per root; delete the per-table ones; make the child repositories inaccessible. This is what actually closes the loopholes L3 opened up.

*Next when:* changes for one department keep breaking another department's code.

**L5 — Split the model along context lines.** Separate packages or modules, each with its own classes, with explicit translation between them. This is Module 2.1's L2/L3 rungs and the same work: the contexts you named in L1 become the modules.

*Next when:* contexts need to react to each other rather than call each other.

**L6 — Domain events between aggregates and contexts.** In-process first. Separate deployables only if Module 2.1's criteria are met, which is a separate decision.

## Implementation

Java, because the DDD literature's idioms are Java and C# ones, and because records and sealed types make the value-object half unusually direct.

### A value object

```java
public record Money(long minorUnits, Currency currency) {

    public Money {                                   // compact constructor: validation
        Objects.requireNonNull(currency, "currency");
    }

    public static Money zero(Currency currency) {
        return new Money(0, currency);
    }

    public Money plus(Money other) {
        if (!currency.equals(other.currency)) {
            throw new IllegalArgumentException(
                "cannot add %s to %s".formatted(other.currency, currency));
        }
        return new Money(minorUnits + other.minorUnits, currency);
    }

    public Money times(int factor) {
        return new Money(minorUnits * factor, currency);
    }
}
```

`minorUnits` is a long, not a double — currency arithmetic in floating point is its own category of bug. Equality, `hashCode`, and immutability come from `record`. And the mixed-currency addition that used to be possible anywhere is now impossible everywhere, because there is one place where addition is defined.

### An aggregate root

```java
public class Order {                                 // the root; nothing else here is public

    private final OrderId id;
    private final CustomerId customerId;             // another aggregate — by ID, never by object
    private final Currency currency;
    private final List<OrderLine> lines = new ArrayList<>();
    private final List<DomainEvent> events = new ArrayList<>();
    private OrderStatus status = OrderStatus.DRAFT;

    public void addLine(ProductId product, int quantity, Money unitPrice) {
        if (status != OrderStatus.DRAFT)      throw new OrderAlreadyPlaced(id);
        if (quantity < 1)                     throw new IllegalArgumentException("quantity must be positive");
        if (!unitPrice.currency().equals(currency)) throw new CurrencyMismatch(currency, unitPrice.currency());

        lines.add(new OrderLine(product, quantity, unitPrice));
    }

    public Money total() {                           // derived, never stored
        return lines.stream()
                    .map(OrderLine::subtotal)
                    .reduce(Money.zero(currency), Money::plus);
    }

    public void place(Instant now) {
        if (lines.isEmpty()) throw new CannotPlaceEmptyOrder(id);
        status = OrderStatus.PLACED;
        events.add(new OrderPlaced(id, customerId, total(), now));
    }

    public List<OrderLine> lines() { return List.copyOf(lines); }   // a copy: callers cannot mutate

    public List<DomainEvent> pullEvents() {
        var pending = List.copyOf(events);
        events.clear();
        return pending;
    }
}
```

The opening bug is gone, and it's worth being precise about *how*. `total()` is computed from the lines rather than stored, so "total equals the sum of lines" isn't an invariant being enforced — it's an invariant that can no longer be expressed falsely. Wherever you can convert "enforce a rule" into "make the violation unrepresentable," take it; enforcement requires every future author to cooperate, and representation doesn't.

The discount job and the support tool from the Problem section have no way in. There's no `setTotal`, no mutable line list, and no repository that hands out `OrderLine` objects. That's rules 1 and 2 of the aggregate doing their work — not by policy, by absence.

Three things to notice at this point in the code:

**Concurrency is not handled yet, and the aggregate is where it will be.** Two requests both load the order, both call `addLine`, both save — one line is lost. The fix is optimistic locking with a version column on the root, checked on write, and the reason it belongs on the root specifically is rule 3: the aggregate is the unit of atomicity, so it's also the unit of conflict detection. Notice this only has a clean answer *because* the boundary exists.

**`pullEvents` sets up a dual write.** Events are collected during the transaction and published after it commits. Publish before the commit and you announce something that may roll back; publish after and a crash in between loses the event silently. The transactional outbox is the standard resolution (Modules 10 and 37), and this is the second article in a row where the same problem has appeared at the same seam — it's not a coincidence, it's what "the transaction boundary" means.

**JPA will push back on this class.** It wants a no-arg constructor, it wants to manage the `lines` collection itself, and `List.copyOf` in the getter defeats its dirty-checking. The usual accommodations are a `protected Order() {}`, field-level access, and returning the managed collection wrapped as unmodifiable rather than copied. They're each small; the thing to watch is that once you've made four of them, the persistence framework has become one of the authors of your domain model.

### The repository

```java
public interface Orders {                 // declared in the domain package
    Optional<Order> byId(OrderId id);
    void save(Order order);
}
```

Two methods, one aggregate. No `findLineById`, no `updateTotal`, no query DSL leaking in. The implementation lives in infrastructure and is the only code in the system that knows how an order becomes rows — which is the same arrangement as Module 2.2's ports, with the granularity now decided by the aggregate rather than by the table layout.

## Further Reading

- **Eric Evans, _Domain-Driven Design_ (Addison-Wesley, 2003).** The source. Part I and Part IV are the parts people skip and the parts that matter; the tactical patterns in between are the parts people read.
- **Eric Evans, _Domain-Driven Design Reference_ (2015).** Free PDF, about 60 pages, every pattern as a short definition. The right thing to keep open while working.
- **Vaughn Vernon, "Effective Aggregate Design" (three-part paper, 2011).** Free, and the single best treatment of aggregate sizing — why small, what breaks when large, and how to reconcile across them.
- **Vaughn Vernon, _Domain-Driven Design Distilled_ (2016).** The 150-page version of the 560-page book. Start here if you're deciding whether this applies to you.
- **Vaughn Vernon, _Implementing Domain-Driven Design_ (2013).** The long, practical one, with real Java.
- **Alberto Brandolini, _Introducing EventStorming_.** The workshop technique for discovering contexts, events, and aggregate boundaries with domain experts in the room. The most directly actionable item on this list.
- **Millett & Tune, _Patterns, Principles, and Practices of Domain-Driven Design_ (Wrox, 2015).** Strongest on the strategic half and on context mapping, which most other treatments rush.
- **Scott Wlaschin, _Domain Modeling Made Functional_ (Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2018).** The types-instead-of-encapsulation alternative from the Alternatives section, worked end to end.
- **Martin Fowler, "BoundedContext" and "UbiquitousLanguage" (bliki).** Two short entries, useful for handing to a colleague who doesn't want a book.

A TypeScript sibling would reveal something this article's Java hides. Java's guarantees here lean on nominal typing and real immutability: `OrderId` and `CustomerId` are genuinely different types, and a `record` is genuinely frozen. TypeScript is structurally typed, so `type OrderId = string` and `type CustomerId = string` are the same type and interchangeable everywhere — the protection is entirely imaginary unless you reach for branded types, and even then it exists only at compile time and evaporates at any boundary where data arrives as JSON. That changes where validation has to sit and makes the parse-at-the-edge discipline mandatory rather than stylistic. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying out of this article doesn't require adopting any of it: **when a single noun in your system is doing several jobs, notice it.** The 84-field class was not the result of bad code, it was the result of three departments quietly negotiating over one word for six years. Every time you're about to add a field to a central class, the useful question is not "does this belong on an order" — it almost always does — but "which meaning of order does it belong to, and is that meaning the same one this class already has."
