# Architecture Decision Records

_Module 2.5 — Software Architecture Patterns_

## The Problem

An engineer joins a team working on a billing platform and, in their second week, finds something that looks wrong.

Outbound webhooks are delivered by a worker that processes one endpoint's queue at a time, sequentially, with no parallelism. Throughput is about 40 deliveries a minute per endpoint. The fix is obvious — a worker pool, twenty at a time — and it's a fifteen-line change. They ship it on Thursday.

The following Friday, a customer reports that their integration is creating duplicate invoices. Their consumer received `invoice.updated` before `invoice.created`, treated the update as a new record, and wrote both.

The single-threaded worker was not an oversight. In 2023 the team looked at parallel delivery, discovered that a meaningful share of customer consumers assume ordered delivery, weighed "guarantee ordering per endpoint" against "guarantee throughput," and chose ordering. The decision was correct. It was made in a Slack thread by two engineers, both of whom have since left the company.

Now watch the second failure, which is quieter and more expensive.

Nine months later, someone else proposes parallelizing webhook delivery. The team spends forty minutes in a meeting reconstructing the argument from fragments — somebody half-remembers "there was a reason," somebody else says "I think it was about a specific customer." They reach the opposite conclusion this time. Not because anything changed; because the person who remembered the ordering constraint wasn't in the room.

And the third thing, which is the part that makes this worth an article: **there was documentation.** A Confluence page called "Webhook Architecture," last edited fourteen months ago, roughly 60% accurate. It describes the queue, the retry policy, and the payload format. It describes the system as it is. It contains not one word about what was considered and rejected, or why.

That's the gap. Code and current-state documentation both record *what was decided*. Neither records **why, what else was on the table, and what would have to change for the answer to be different.** And that missing part is the part with all the value in it, because it's the only part that tells you whether a decision still applies.

A decision is made in a high-context moment — three people who've just spent two days on the problem, holding the constraints in their heads. It's consumed in low-context moments, for years, by people who weren't there. The artifact that survives is the code, which is the residue of a decision with all the reasoning evaporated off it.

## What It Is

An **architecture decision record** is a short document capturing one significant decision: the situation that forced it, what was decided, what else was considered, and what it costs. Numbered, stored in version control next to the code it governs, and never edited after acceptance — superseded instead.

The properties that make it work, each of which is doing something:

**One decision per document.** Not "our architecture." A document covering several decisions can't be superseded, because half of it is still true.

**Immutable once accepted.** When thinking changes, you write ADR-0042 saying it supersedes ADR-0007. You don't edit 0007. The record of what you believed in 2023, and why, is the asset — including the parts later shown to be wrong.

If that sounds familiar, it's the same instinct as Module 2.4: an `UPDATE` keeps the conclusion and destroys the reason, and an append-only log with compensating entries keeps both. The domain here happens to be your own reasoning.

**Short.** One to two pages. A twelve-page document is a design doc, and it won't be read in the moment when it matters — which is somebody on a Tuesday wondering whether they can change a thing.

**In the repository.** Reviewed as a pull request, versioned with the code, greppable, and impossible to lose in a migration from one wiki to another.

### What counts as "architecturally significant"

This is the entire scoping question, and the usual answers — "affects structure," "affects non-functional requirements" — are too vague to act on.

The formulation that works in practice: **would a competent engineer who wasn't there plausibly undo this without understanding why?**

That's the webhook worker exactly. It looks like an oversight. It isn't. Anything with that property — a deliberate choice that reads as an accident — needs a record, regardless of how architectural it sounds.

Two supporting tests, both useful:

- **Is it a one-way door?** Decisions that are cheap to reverse rarely need recording. Decisions that are expensive to reverse always do. Module 2.1's service boundaries, Module 2.3's aggregate boundaries, and Module 2.4's event schemas are all one-way doors, which is why each of those articles flagged the cost of getting them wrong.
- **Did it take an argument?** If two reasonable people disagreed, the reasoning that resolved it is worth keeping, because the same two reasonable positions will recur.

### Neighbors

**ADRs are not design docs or RFCs.** A design doc is forward-looking, proposes a body of work, runs to many pages, and is largely dead once the work ships. An ADR is a durable record of one decision, written to be read years later by someone who doesn't care about the project it came from. Teams often want both; they're not substitutes.

**ADRs are not current-state documentation.** The Confluence page describing how webhooks work today is useful and should exist. It answers "what"; ADRs answer "why, and what else."

**ADRs are not commit messages.** The instinct behind a good commit message is identical, and the granularity and discoverability are both wrong: nobody finds a decision by reading two years of `git log`.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The same decision gets re-argued every few months, with no new information, sometimes reaching different answers.
- "Why is it like this?" is answered with a name, and the name belongs to someone who left.
- Someone "fixed" something deliberate and broke it.
- There are code comments saying "do not change this" with no reason attached.
- Nobody can say whether a constraint still applies, because nobody knows what caused it.
- Onboarding takes months, and most of the time is absorbing reasoning that exists only in conversation.
- A vendor or library choice is questioned and no one can list what else was evaluated.
- Incident reviews keep finding that a protective design existed at some point and was removed by someone who didn't know what it protected against.
- An auditor or a customer's security review asks why you made a choice, and the answer has to be reconstructed under time pressure.

## How It Works

### The sections, and what each is for

**Context.** The forces in play at the time, stated concretely enough to be falsifiable — volumes, latencies, deadlines, team size, contractual obligations. Non-technical constraints belong here and are usually the most valuable part, because they're the ones that leave no trace anywhere else. "We had six weeks before a contractual launch date" explains more decisions in real systems than any technical argument does.

**Decision.** Active voice, present tense: "We will deliver webhooks sequentially per endpoint." One paragraph.

**Alternatives considered.** The section that carries most of the article's value. Each alternative, and the specific reason it lost. A decision without its rejected alternatives is an assertion; with them, it's an argument someone can evaluate.

**Consequences.** Both directions, including what you've given up and what you now can't easily do. A consequences section listing only benefits is a sales document.

**Revisit when.** Not in the original template, and worth adding to yours: the condition under which this should be reconsidered. "If per-endpoint volume exceeds 500/min sustained, or if fewer than 5% of active consumers are ordering-sensitive." This converts an ADR from an archive entry into something with a trigger, and it pre-answers the "does this still apply?" question that otherwise requires reconstructing the whole argument.

### Lifecycle

```
                   ┌── Rejected ──┐   (kept, not deleted — the
                   │              │    reasoning is the point)
   Proposed ───────┤              ▼
                   └── Accepted ──┬──▶ Superseded by ADR-0042
                                  └──▶ Deprecated (no longer relevant)
```

Rejected ADRs get kept. "We considered Kafka in 2024 and decided against it, for these reasons" is exactly as useful as a record of what you did adopt, and it's the thing that stops the proposal recurring annually.

### The mechanism that makes it stick

Two practices do almost all the work, and teams that skip them end up with a folder nobody reads.

**The pull request is the decision forum.** The ADR is drafted as `Proposed`, opened as a PR, and the review comments *are* the discussion. Merging it is the act of deciding. This matters because it makes writing the record the same activity as making the decision, rather than homework afterwards — and homework afterwards never happens.

**Code links back.** At the place where the decision is visible in the code, a one-line comment: `// Sequential by design — see docs/adr/0007-webhook-ordering.md`. This is what would have prevented the incident at the top of this article. An ADR nobody can find from the code is a wiki page with extra ceremony; the link is the difference between a record and a working safeguard.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any team expecting turnover**, which is every team over a long enough horizon. The value of an ADR is realized entirely by people who weren't in the room.
- **Decisions that are expensive to reverse.** Service boundaries, data models, event schemas, storage engines, auth models, tenancy strategy. Everything Module 1 and Module 2 have flagged as a one-way door.
- **Distributed or asynchronous teams**, where the decision conversation has no shared physical moment to happen in. This is the documentation-first culture Module 32 covers, applied to one artifact.
- **Regulated environments.** Auditors ask why. Having the answer written, dated, and version-controlled converts a week of archaeology into a link.
- **Open source and multi-team platforms**, where contributors have no access to tribal knowledge at all.
- **Genuinely contested decisions.** Writing the alternatives down forces the disagreement into a form where it can be examined, and reasonably often resolves it — because one side discovers their objection doesn't survive being written in a sentence.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

**Two-way doors.** If a decision can be reversed in an afternoon, recording it is bureaucracy. Choosing a date-formatting library is not an ADR.

**Consistency choices.** "We used the same logging library as the other six services" doesn't need a record. The original decision does; every instance of following it doesn't.

**Solo or short-lived projects.** No future stranger, no problem.

**When it becomes an approval gate.** The most common way this fails: a process requiring an approved ADR before any architectural change. People route around it, decisions get made in code and back-filled into documents nobody reads, and the record becomes fiction — worse than no record, because it's misleading rather than merely absent. An ADR is a record of a decision, not permission to make one.

**Compliance theatre.** ADRs written after the fact, in bulk, to satisfy an audit, with the Context section reverse-engineered from the code. These cost real time and carry no information.

**At volume, without navigation.** Three hundred undifferentiated ADRs is its own failure. An index, tags by area, and honest use of `Superseded` are what keep the collection usable past year two.

## Trade-offs

**Writing cost is small; review cost is real.** Fifteen to thirty minutes to draft, and then a PR discussion that can run for days. Most of that isn't new cost — it's the decision cost becoming visible instead of happening invisibly in a hallway — but it does slow the calendar, and on a team that was moving fast by deciding informally, it will feel like a tax before it feels like a benefit.

**Silent drift is the main failure mode.** An accepted ADR describes what was true when it was written. If the system changes and nobody supersedes it, the record and reality diverge quietly, and a reader has no way to tell. Nothing in the practice detects this; only supersession discipline does, and supersession discipline is the thing teams stop doing first.

**They record intent, not outcome.** An ADR tells you what you expected. It never tells you whether it worked. That's a real limitation, and the partial fix is a follow-up ADR revisiting the decision with what actually happened — which is also the only honest way to keep immutability intact.

**Political honesty is uncomfortable.** The true Context is sometimes "we chose Postgres because nobody here has run Cassandra" or "we had four weeks." Those are excellent reasons and terrible things to write down in some organizations. An ADR that launders the real reason into a technical-sounding one is actively harmful, because it will be read later as though it were true.

**Discoverability degrades as the collection grows**, and nothing about the practice fixes that automatically.

## Alternatives

**RFC / design doc processes.** Longer documents, one per project, reviewed before work starts — the Google and Squarespace model. Better than ADRs for large greenfield decisions where the work itself needs designing. Worse as a durable record, because the decisions are buried inside project narratives that go stale as a whole.

**Rich commit messages.** Free, tied precisely to the change, and genuinely valuable. The problem is retrieval: nobody finds a decision by searching commit history, and the decision often spans many commits or none.

**Code comments.** The right tool for local "why." They don't survive the file being refactored away, and they can't hold an argument about alternatives.

**Wiki architecture pages.** Complementary rather than competing — they answer "what does this look like now," which ADRs deliberately don't. The failure is using them as a substitute, which is exactly the Confluence page from the Problem section.

**Issue-tracker discussion.** Where most decisions actually get made. Poorly searchable, closed tickets become invisible, and the whole corpus is hostage to whichever tracker you migrate off next.

**arc42 and similar documentation frameworks** include a decisions section, so they're a container for ADRs rather than an alternative to them.

**Nothing, relying on tenure.** Worth naming honestly, because it works — at small scale, with stable staff, for a while. It fails at the first departure, and it fails silently, which is why teams usually adopt ADRs one incident after they needed them.

## How to Achieve It

**L1 — Write one, retroactively, for the decision people argue about most.** Don't announce a process. Half an hour, one file, and it settles an argument that has been recurring for a year. This is also the cheapest possible way to find out whether your team will use these.

*Next when:* somebody asks where to put the second one.

**L2 — `docs/adr/`, a template, sequential numbering.** Back-fill three to five of the most contested existing decisions — this both delivers immediate value and teaches the format by example, which a template alone doesn't.

*Next when:* an ADR exists for something and someone changes that thing anyway, because they never saw it.

**L3 — Link from code to ADR.** One-line comments at the places where a decision is visible and looks arbitrary. This is the step that converts the collection from documentation into a safeguard, and it's the one most commonly skipped.

*Next when:* ADRs are being written after decisions rather than as them.

**L4 — Make the ADR PR the decision forum.** Draft as `Proposed`, discuss in review, merge as `Accepted`.

*Next when:* you have enough of them that finding the relevant one is work, or you notice one that's been wrong for a year.

**L5 — Index, supersession discipline, and periodic review.** A README listing them by area and status. A pass during planning to check `Revisit when` conditions against current reality.

Start retroactive, not prospective. A team that adopts the process before it has any examples produces a template and no documents.

## The Artifact

There's no code in this one — the deliverable is the document itself, and the difference between a useful ADR and a useless one is entirely in the writing.

### Template

```markdown
# NNNN. <Short decision, in the imperative: "Deliver webhooks sequentially per endpoint">

- Date: YYYY-MM-DD
- Status: Proposed | Accepted | Rejected | Superseded by ADR-NNNN | Deprecated
- Deciders: <names>

## Context
What situation forced this decision. Concrete: volumes, deadlines, constraints,
team realities. Include the non-technical forces — they're the ones nothing else records.

## Decision
"We will ..." One paragraph, active voice.

## Alternatives considered
Each option, and the specific reason it lost.

## Consequences
What gets better. What gets worse. What this now makes difficult.

## Revisit when
The condition that should trigger reopening this.
```

### A worked example

```markdown
# 0007. Deliver webhooks sequentially per endpoint

- Date: 2024-03-11
- Status: Accepted
- Deciders: R. Okafor, M. Vasilyev

## Context

We deliver ~3,400 webhooks/day, peaking around 40/min for our largest endpoint.
Delivery is currently one worker per endpoint, processing its queue in order.

The proposal under discussion was a worker pool (20 concurrent), which would take
peak drain time for a backlog from ~25 min to under 2 min.

Surveying our 140 active webhook consumers: 17 documented endpoints are idempotent
and order-tolerant. For the rest we have no information. Two customers have
explicitly told support that their integrations assume `*.created` arrives before
`*.updated` — one of them builds records on first sight of an ID.

Our published webhook documentation has never stated an ordering guarantee either way,
so consumers have reasonably assumed the behaviour they observed, which has been ordered
since launch.

## Decision

We will deliver webhooks sequentially per endpoint, preserving per-endpoint ordering.
Concurrency across different endpoints is unconstrained.

## Alternatives considered

**Parallel delivery with a worker pool.** Fastest to implement and solves the backlog
drain. Rejected: silently breaks consumers who have depended on ordering since launch,
with no way for us to identify which ones in advance, and the failure is data corruption
on their side rather than an error on ours.

**Parallel delivery plus a sequence number in the payload.** Correct long-term shape, and
it moves the ordering burden to consumers who can handle it. Rejected for now: requires
every consumer to change, and we can't force that. Revisit as part of API v3.

**Opt-in parallelism per endpoint.** Consumers declare order-tolerance; we parallelise
for those. Rejected as premature: 17 known-tolerant endpoints, none of which are near
our throughput limits, so it's configuration surface with no current beneficiary.

## Consequences

- Per-endpoint throughput is capped around 40/min. A large backlog for one endpoint
  takes ~25 min to drain and that endpoint's customer sees delayed events throughout.
- One slow consumer delays only its own queue — an unintended benefit; we get isolation
  between customers for free.
- Ordering is now a de facto guarantee. It should be documented as one, because we are
  relied upon for it whether or not we say so. (Tracked: PLAT-2291.)
- The single-worker design will look like an oversight to anyone reading it. Linked from
  `delivery/worker.go`.

## Revisit when

- Sustained per-endpoint volume exceeds 500/min, or
- A sequence-number scheme ships in API v3 and the majority of traffic has migrated, or
- We add per-endpoint ordering opt-out and at least ~20% of volume opts out.
```

That document is fifteen minutes of writing. It would have prevented a customer-facing data-corruption incident and a forty-minute meeting that reached the wrong answer.

### And a useless one, for contrast

```markdown
# 0012. Use Kafka for messaging

- Date: 2024-06-02
- Status: Accepted

## Context
We need a scalable, modern messaging solution for our microservices.

## Decision
We will use Kafka.

## Consequences
Improved scalability and decoupling.
```

Everything wrong with this is instructive:

- **The Context contains no facts.** No volume, no latency requirement, no retention need, no ordering need. Nothing a future reader could check against their own situation to decide whether it still holds. Module 1.2's rule applies to prose as much as to design reviews: a claim that can't be falsified can't be used.
- **"Scalable" and "modern" are doing the work of an argument.** Precisely the cargo-cult framing to avoid — a property asserted without the pressure that justified it.
- **No alternatives.** So the reader cannot tell whether RabbitMQ, SQS, NATS, or a Postgres table were considered and beaten, or never mentioned. Next year, someone will propose all four.
- **Consequences list only upside.** No word about operational burden, partition-count decisions, consumer-group semantics, or the fact that someone now has to run a Kafka cluster.
- **No revisit condition**, so it will be treated as permanent and correct forever, or ignored entirely.

The distinguishing question when reviewing an ADR is simple: *could a competent stranger, two years from now, use this to decide whether the decision still applies?* The first document passes. The second is a sentence wearing a template.

## Further Reading

- **Michael Nygard, "Documenting Architecture Decisions" (2011).** The original post that named the practice. Short, and still the best statement of it.
- **adr.github.io** — a maintained collection of templates, tooling, and examples, including MADR (Markdown Any Decision Records) for a slightly richer structure than Nygard's.
- **Nat Pryce, `adr-tools`.** Shell tooling for creating, numbering, and superseding ADRs. Not necessary, but it removes the small frictions that otherwise stop the practice.
- **ThoughtWorks Technology Radar — "Lightweight Architecture Decision Records."** In the Adopt ring since 2016; the entry is two paragraphs and useful for convincing a skeptical team.
- **arc42, section 9 ("Architecture Decisions").** How ADRs slot into a fuller documentation framework, if you need one.
- **Malte Ubl, "Design Docs at Google" (industrialempathy.com).** The RFC-style alternative, described well. Read alongside this to see which parts of your decisions want which format.
- **Jeff Bezos, 1997 and 2015 Amazon shareholder letters.** The origin of the one-way/two-way door framing used above as a scoping test. Two pages, and it's the most useful filter in this article.
- **Michael Nygard, _Release It!_ (2nd ed., Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2018).** Not about ADRs, but it's the best catalogue of the kinds of decisions that turn out to be one-way doors.

This is the one article in the collection with no language dimension, so there's no sibling to forward-reference. The artifact is Markdown in a repository, and that's the same in every ecosystem.

---

The habit worth carrying out of this article, and out of Module 2 generally: **notice which of your decisions are one-way doors.** Service boundaries, aggregate boundaries, event schemas, tenancy models — every article in this module has ended up at the same place, which is that a small number of early choices are cheap to make and expensive to unmake, and that they don't announce themselves at the time. Recording those is the practice here. But the recording is downstream of the noticing, and the noticing is the part that makes you better at this.

A corollary for the other side of the transaction, since most of us are the new engineer more often than the departed one: **when something in an unfamiliar codebase looks obviously wrong, assume for ten minutes that it was deliberate and go looking for the reason.** If you find one, you've learned something about the system. If you find nothing, that absence is itself the finding — and writing the ADR you wished existed is a better first contribution than the fifteen-line fix.
