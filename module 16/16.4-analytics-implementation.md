# Analytics Implementation

_Module 16.4 — Analytics & Metrics_

## The Problem

The tracking plan from 16.1a exists and the metrics from 16.2 are defined. What remains is the part that looks like plumbing and turns out to be an ownership decision.

**Month 1 — the tool.** Someone adds a product analytics SDK to the front end and instruments twenty events. It takes an afternoon. For three months it is the best thing that has happened to the team.

**Month 4 — the second and third tools.** Marketing needs conversions in the ad platform, so a pixel goes on the page. Sales needs signups in the CRM, so a third snippet joins them. Each was added by a different person for a different reason, and each collects its own copy. Signups last week:

```
ad platform     1,412     fires on page load; counts bots; double-fires on refresh
product tool    1,290     blocked for a share of users; dedupes by device
your database   1,187     the actual rows; only counts completed signups
```

Nobody is wrong. There are three measurement systems with three definitions, three populations, and three sets of bugs, and no way to reconcile them because none of them can see the others' raw data.

**Month 6 — the question that spans systems.** "How much revenue came from the paid channel?" Behaviour is in the product tool. Subscriptions are in Postgres. Spend is in the ad platform. None of the three can join to the other two, so an analyst exports CSVs and assembles the answer in a spreadsheet over four days. The number goes into a board deck and has never been reproduced since, because the spreadsheet encoded twenty judgement calls that live nowhere.

This is the structural one: **the questions that actually matter always span systems, and a product analytics tool is a silo by construction.**

**Month 9 — the client-side bill comes due.** Three SDKs is a couple of hundred kilobytes of JavaScript. A consent banner means a fraction of visitors are never measured at all. Tracking prevention caps cookie lifetime, so returning visitors are counted as new. Top-of-funnel numbers are wrong by an amount nobody can estimate, and the funnel built on them is quoted to two decimal places.

**Month 12 — the silent break.** A frontend rewrite renames one event. Nothing errors. Three dashboards go to zero and it is five weeks before anyone notices, because a chart at zero looks like a chart.

**Month 14 — the deletion request.** An erasure request arrives. Engineering deletes the user row. The person's events remain in the product tool, in the warehouse, in an ad platform audience, in a CS tool, and in a CSV on a laptop. Nobody has a list of where the data went, and assembling one takes longer than the statutory deadline.

**Month 16 — the invoice.** The tool prices by monthly tracked user. A campaign brought 200,000 visitors who bounced, and the bill tripled for data no one will ever query.

Every one of those follows from a single decision nobody remembers making: **each consumer of the data collects its own copy.** The pipeline question is really one question — how many times is a fact measured, and which copy is canonical?

## What It Is

**Analytics pipeline architecture**: the arrangement of five layers, and the choice of who owns each.

**Collection** produces events (client SDKs, server libraries). **Transport** receives, validates, enriches and buffers them. **Storage** keeps them, ideally raw and append-only. **Modelling** turns raw events into the tables people query — sessions, funnels, account rollups, metric definitions. **Activation** pushes the modelled results back out to the systems that act on them: the CRM, the ad platforms, the CS tooling, and the product itself.

Three things get presented as competitors and are actually different layers, which is why "should we use a CDP or a warehouse" is a malformed question. A **product analytics tool** is storage plus a query interface specialised for funnels and cohorts. A **CDP** is collection plus routing, and performs no analysis. A **warehouse** is storage plus arbitrary SQL, and knows nothing about collection. You can hold all three, any two, or none, and the interesting decision is not which brand but where the canonical copy lives.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Three systems report three numbers for the same thing.
- Answering a real question requires exporting CSVs and joining them by hand.
- There are more than two analytics SDKs on the page.
- Nobody can enumerate every place a given user's events end up.
- A dashboard went to zero after a release and stayed there.
- Your analytics bill is priced on a quantity you don't control.
- A vendor holds the only copy of two years of history.
- A bug in enrichment means that period's data is wrong permanently, because you never kept the raw version.
- Marketing has its own tracking and engineering has its own, and the two have never been compared.
- Renaming an event requires knowing who consumes it, and nobody does.
- Deletion requests are handled by deleting a row in the production database.
- "Does this account's usage justify its price?" can't be answered, because usage and price live in different systems.
- The same metric is defined in the analytics tool, in the warehouse, and in the app, by three different people.

## How It Works

### Three shapes, and the real difference between them

**A — fan-out at the edge.** Every consumer is instrumented separately.

```
app ──► product analytics tool
    ──► ad platform
    ──► CRM
    ──► warehouse (maybe)
```

Fastest to start and where almost everyone begins. N integrations, N schemas, N sets of numbers, N privacy surfaces, and every new destination is app code and a deploy.

**B — a collector hub.** Collect once, route many.

```
app ──► collector ──┬──► product analytics tool
                    ├──► ad platform
                    ├──► CRM
                    └──► warehouse
```

One schema, one consent gate, one place to redact, one place to add a destination. This is a **routing** decision: the tools are still doing the analysis, you have just stopped collecting four times.

**C — warehouse-first.** The canonical copy is yours, and the tools become consumers.

```
app ──► collector ──► warehouse (raw, append-only)
                            │
                      modelling layer
                            │
                  ┌─────────┴──────────┐
              BI / SQL          reverse ETL ──► ad platform, CRM,
                                                 CS tooling, the product
```

This is an **ownership** decision, and it is what makes the month-6 question answerable: behaviour, billing, support and spend become tables in one place and the join is ordinary SQL. It costs latency — minutes to hours rather than seconds — and it hands you the modelling layer, which is software with tests and an on-call rotation.

B without C is common and reasonable. C essentially always implies B.

### Build versus buy is five decisions, not one

Treating it as one produces either a team building browser SDKs or a team with no data of its own. Decomposed, most organisations should buy some layers and build others, and the split is not arbitrary:

- **Client SDKs — buy, always.** The work is not the first version. It is the browser matrix, mobile offline queues, retry and batching semantics, consent framework integrations, and a decade of accumulated bug fixes for behaviours you will otherwise discover one at a time in production.
- **Collector — buy, or self-host something open source.** Build only for a genuine constraint: data residency, volume economics, an unusual protocol.
- **Storage — buy.** A managed warehouse or column store.
- **Modelling — build.** This is your business logic. What counts as active, how accounts roll up, which events constitute the funnel: no vendor can supply these, and a vendor's built-in version is a guess at your business.
- **Analysis interface — buy, mostly.** Funnel and cohort UIs are good products and rebuilding them is a two-year detour from whatever you actually do.

The rule underneath: **own the parts that encode your business, rent the parts that are undifferentiated plumbing.** The tracking plan, the models, and the metric definitions are yours regardless of whose software runs. Batching and browser quirks never should be.

And the reconciliation that dissolves most of the lock-in argument: **buying does not require giving up ownership**, as long as the raw stream lands in your storage first and the vendors are fed from it. Lock-in is not the SDK. Lock-in is two years of history that exists only inside someone else's product.

### What the pricing model does to your behaviour

Worth checking before signing, because each common model pushes a team toward a specific mistake.

**Per tracked user** punishes traffic you didn't want and can't control — the bounced campaign visitors from month 16 cost the same as customers. The team's response is to stop tracking anonymous users, which deletes the top of the funnel.

**Per event** makes instrumentation cost money, so people instrument less, which is exactly backwards: the expensive event is the one you didn't emit.

**Per query or per unit of compute** is the least harmful of the three, and still discourages exploration by the people you most want exploring.

None is disqualifying. The discipline is to price the model against your growth curve rather than your current volume, and to ask what the bill does when a campaign doubles anonymous traffic for a month.

### Schema evolution, which is where governance actually bites

16.1a covered naming and the tracking plan. The part that fails later is change.

**Additive is safe; everything else is a break.** Adding a property is backward compatible. Removing, renaming, or retyping one is not. And the most damaging change of all — a property keeping its name and its type while its *meaning* shifts — is invisible to every validator ever written, which is why an event needs a human owner and a written description more than it needs a schema.

**Version the schema, not the name.** `checkout_completed` with a version field keeps one event that consumers can query. `checkout_completed_v2` forces every downstream query to union two names forever, and then three.

**Validate at ingest, and quarantine rather than reject or accept.** Rejecting loses data you cannot re-collect. Accepting silently poisons every model downstream. Quarantine writes the malformed event to a side table, alerts the owner, and lets you replay once the producer is fixed — which converts a permanent data loss into a delay.

**Keep raw immutable and reprocessable.** Store the event exactly as received and derive everything else. The justification is simple: every enrichment and every model has bugs, and if the enriched version is the only version, the bug is permanent. This one property is most of what warehouse-first buys you.

**Deprecate explicitly.** Mark it in the registry, alert while it is still being emitted, remove it from the producer, then stop accepting it. Historical data is never deleted — a deprecated event's past is still the only record of that past.

### Point-in-time versus current, and where enrichment belongs

An event can carry attributes, and there is a right answer about which ones.

Attach to the event what was true **at that instant and cannot be reconstructed later**: the app version, the referrer, the experiment variant, the price displayed. Join everything else from a dimension table at query time.

The reason is that "what did Pro customers do in March" and "what did the customers who were Pro in March do" are different questions with different answers, and copying the current plan onto every event collapses them into one. The warehouse world has a formal name for keeping both available — slowly changing dimensions, type 2 — and it is worth knowing, because the first time finance and product disagree about a cohort's revenue, this is usually why.

The related failure is a model that reads current state and is therefore not a function of its inputs. If `active_accounts_march` joins to `accounts.plan` as it is today, re-running it next quarter produces a different number, last quarter's report can never be reproduced, and nobody can tell whether the data changed or the code did. **Models must be deterministic functions of raw events plus dated dimensions**, or reprocessing — the entire point of keeping raw — silently rewrites history.

### Privacy is a property of the pipeline, not a checkbox

Module 34 covers the legal frame. What belongs here is where the controls physically sit.

**Consent gating belongs at the collector.** Enforced in each SDK it is N places to get wrong and N places to audit. Enforced once, at the single point everything passes through, it is one.

**PII arrives through properties, not through fields named `email`.** URLs with reset tokens in query strings, page titles containing a customer's name, search terms someone pasted an address into, form values captured by autocapture. The defence is an **allowlist** of permitted properties rather than a blocklist of forbidden ones — with a blocklist, every new property is collected by default, and the incident is always a property nobody reviewed.

**Deletion must reach every destination**, which means maintaining a data map: for each destination, which identifier it holds and what its deletion mechanism is. Append-only storage fights this by design, and there are two standard resolutions. Partition by user, so a delete is a partition drop rather than a full scan. Or **crypto-shredding**: encrypt each user's identifying data under a per-user key and delete the key, rendering the rows unreadable without rewriting a petabyte. The second is the technique most teams haven't heard of, and the one that makes append-only storage compatible with erasure rights.

**Retention is a control, not a shortfall.** Raw behavioural events kept forever are a liability that grows. Set a raw window, keep aggregates beyond it, and be able to say why the number is what it is.

## When It's Appropriate

- **The moment a second team needs the same events.** That is the point at which the hub pays for itself, and it arrives sooner than anyone expects.
- **When a question spans behaviour and money.** Usage against price, retention against plan, support load against feature adoption. This is the case for warehouse-first, and there is no other good way to get it.
- **When you already have a warehouse for other reasons.** Adding events to an existing warehouse is a fraction of the cost of standing one up for events alone.
- **B2B with a non-standard account model.** Parent companies, resellers, multi-workspace hierarchies — product analytics tools model one or two shapes and yours will be a third.
- **Regulated data or residency requirements**, which usually force a self-hosted collector regardless of everything else.
- **When data needs to flow back into the product** — in-app usage displays, CS health signals, lifecycle messaging. That is reverse ETL, and it needs a canonical modelled source to push from.
- **When the vendor bill crosses the cost of running storage yourself**, which tends to happen abruptly rather than gradually.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Pre-product-market-fit.** One tool, twenty events, no pipeline. The month-1 setup in the Problem was correct for month 1; the mistake was three months of nobody revisiting it.
- **A warehouse with only events in it is a slower analytics tool.** The value is the join. If there is nothing to join to yet, you have paid for infrastructure and given up the funnel UI.
- **A full modelling and orchestration stack for a company that asks four questions a month.** Someone will maintain it, and that someone could have been answering the four questions.
- **Streaming infrastructure when hourly batch answers everything.** Real-time is justified by a decision that must be made in seconds — fraud, operational dashboards, live in-product counters — not by a PM refreshing a chart. The difference between hourly and real-time is roughly an order of magnitude in both cost and operational surface.
- **A CDP with one destination.** That is a proxy.
- **Self-hosting to save money** when the saving is smaller than the engineer-weeks. Compute the second number before starting.
- **Building your own SDK**, under essentially any circumstances.

## Trade-offs

- **A hub is a single point of failure for everything measured**, and its failures are silent: you lose data you don't know you lost, and discover it as a gap in a chart weeks later.
- **Warehouse-first trades latency for joinability.** Anything the product must read in real time cannot come from there, so you keep a second path and now maintain two.
- **You own the modelling layer**, which means tests, review, an on-call for broken pipelines, and a backlog competing with product work.
- **Raw retention is simultaneously your most valuable asset and your largest liability**, and both grow at the same rate.
- **Lock-in is history, not integration.** Switching tools is easy; extracting two years of events from a vendor with no bulk export is not — and that is a question for the evaluation, not the migration.
- **Each destination multiplies the privacy surface and the number of places a definition can drift**, and destinations get added by people who will not be maintaining them.
- **Reprocessing rewrites the past.** Fixing a model changes last quarter's numbers, which is correct and also means a figure someone already presented is now wrong. Model versions and a changelog are the minimum defence.
- **A composable stack has more seams, and data goes missing at seams.** A single vendor is worse at nearly everything except the one thing that dominates daily life: nobody has to integrate it.

## Alternatives

**One tool and no pipeline.** Correct for longer than this article's structure implies. If nobody has yet asked a question spanning two systems, the pipeline solves a problem you don't have.

**The production database and a BI tool.** Metabase or equivalent pointed at a read replica, no events at all. Zero new systems, and for B2B with hundreds of accounts it answers a large fraction of real questions. Its ceiling is exactly the one 16.1a described — state, not behaviour — but the ceiling is higher than people assume.

**A CDC-based pipeline** (Module 38.4). Stream row changes into the warehouse and model them there. No instrumentation to forget, nothing to block, and it complements events rather than replacing them.

**Server-side collection only.** Skip client SDKs entirely. You lose intent — scrolls, abandons, failed searches — and gain reliability, ad-blocker immunity, a far simpler consent story, and a faster page. For B2B tools where nearly every meaningful action hits the API, this is underrated and frequently right.

**Structured request logs as the event source.** You already emit them; ship them to the warehouse and treat events as a byproduct. Cheap, coarse, and all of the identity work is yours.

**A single vendor's full suite** — collection, analysis, warehouse export and activation from one company. Fewer seams, one relationship, one bill, and a genuinely lower operational burden. Check the export path before signing, because it is the only part that is hard to change later.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — one product analytics tool, client SDK, twenty events.** Correct at the start.

**L1 — server-side emission for the facts that matter, and a raw copy in your own storage.** Even a Postgres table. *Signal to start:* the first disagreement between the tool's number and the database's.

**L2 — one collector everything passes through.** Consent gating and redaction live there; tools become destinations rather than collectors. *Signal:* the third SDK on the page, or the first deletion request.

**L3 — the warehouse as canonical raw store, plus a modelling layer with tests.** *Signal:* a question that spans behaviour and billing.

**L4 — validation with quarantine, a schema registry, named owners, a deprecation flow, and monitoring of instrumentation health.** *Signal:* a dashboard that went to zero and nobody noticed.

**L5 — activation.** Modelled data pushed back into the CRM, the CS tooling, the ad platforms and the product; metric definitions living once in the modelling layer and consumed everywhere. *Signal:* another team building its own copy of a number you already compute.

## Implementation

TypeScript, because the collector is where this article's decisions become code.

```ts
type Registered = {
  version: number;
  validate: (props: unknown) => { ok: true } | { ok: false; error: string };
  allowedProperties: string[];      // an allowlist, for the reason below
  owner: string;                    // a team, not a person who has left
};

const registry: Record<string, Registered> = loadTrackingPlan();
```

```ts
app.post("/e", async (req, res) => {
  // Acknowledge first. An analytics outage must never become a product
  // outage, and the client must never wait on our fan-out.
  res.status(202).end();

  for (const raw of req.body.batch) {
    const schema = registry[raw.name];
    if (!schema) {
      await quarantine(raw, "unknown_event");
      continue;
    }

    const result = schema.validate(raw.properties);
    if (!result.ok) {
      await quarantine(raw, result.error);
      continue;
    }
    // Quarantine, not reject and not accept. Rejecting loses an event that
    // cannot be re-collected; accepting silently poisons every model built
    // on it. A side table plus an alert to schema.owner turns a permanent
    // data loss into a fix-and-replay.

    await rawStore.append(enrich(redact(raw, schema), req));
    // Append-only, immutable, and the only copy that matters. Destinations
    // are fed by a worker reading from here — never from this handler, or a
    // destination's outage becomes your data loss.
  }
});
```

```ts
function redact(raw: RawEvent, schema: Registered): RawEvent {
  // An allowlist, because with a blocklist every new property is collected
  // by default and the incident is always a property nobody reviewed: a URL
  // carrying a reset token, a page title containing a customer name, a
  // search box someone pasted an email address into.
  const properties = Object.fromEntries(
    Object.entries(raw.properties)
      .filter(([key]) => schema.allowedProperties.includes(key)),
  );
  return { ...raw, properties };
}

function enrich(e: RawEvent, req: Request): StoredEvent {
  return {
    ...e,
    receivedAt: new Date(),
    ip: truncateIp(req.ip),          // last octet dropped before storage
    // Attach only what was true at this instant and cannot be reconstructed
    // later: app version, referrer, experiment variant, the price displayed.
    // Current plan, company size and industry are NOT copied here — they are
    // a dated join at query time, because "what did Pro customers do in
    // March" and "what did the customers who were Pro in March do" are
    // different questions, and copying collapses them into one.
    appVersion: e.context.appVersion,
    variant: e.context.variant,
  };
}
```

## Further Reading

- **Segment's documentation on the CDP model**, particularly the destination and consent architecture. The clearest public statement of shape B, even if you never buy it.
- **Snowplow's open-source documentation** — the schema registry, validation, and "bad rows." The Implementation above is closest to their design, and their failed-event handling is worth copying whatever you end up running.
- **RudderStack and Jitsu**, as open-source collectors with different trade-offs. Useful for costing the self-host option honestly rather than optimistically.
- **dbt's documentation**, for modelling as reviewed software — sources, tests, lineage — and for the semantic layer that L5 depends on.
- **Kimball & Ross on slowly changing dimensions, type 2.** The formal treatment of point-in-time versus current, and the reason dimension tables need validity dates.
- **Chad Sanderson's data contracts writing.** Cited in 16.1a for the tracking plan; here for schema ownership across team boundaries and the argument that the producer owns correctness.
- **Crypto-shredding / key-based erasure**, in any of the major cloud vendors' GDPR architecture guides. Short, and it is the technique that reconciles append-only storage with deletion rights.
- **BigQuery's GA4 export schema.** A concrete, public example of a raw event schema designed for warehouse consumption rather than for a UI.
- **The sceptical writing on the modern data stack.** Read at least one piece before adopting six tools; the failure it describes — a data team maintaining infrastructure instead of answering questions — is common.

A **Go sibling** for this article would be worth writing, and it would not be a translation. The TypeScript above quietly skips the part where a collector actually fails: ingest under load. `rawStore.append` per event inside a loop is fine at a thousand events a second and is the wrong shape at fifty thousand, and the runtime hides the queue, the worker pool, the batch boundary and the drop policy. In Go those are things you write down — a bounded channel, a fixed set of workers, an explicit `default:` branch deciding what happens when the buffer is full. That turns "we drop events sometimes" from something you discover as a gap in a chart into a policy chosen on a line of code, which is exactly the property this article asks for and the TypeScript cannot demonstrate. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying past analytics: **count the copies.** Any fact measured independently by two systems will eventually disagree, and reconciling them permanently costs more than collecting once and distributing. It is the same shape as a cache with no invalidation story, a denormalised column nobody recomputes, a config value pasted into three deploy manifests. Wherever something is measured or stored in more than one place, the useful question is not whether the copies currently agree — it is which one is canonical, and what is supposed to happen to the others when it changes.
