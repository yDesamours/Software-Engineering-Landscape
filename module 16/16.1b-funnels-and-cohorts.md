# Funnels & Cohorts

_Module 16.1b — Analytics & Metrics_

## The Problem

The event stream from 16.1a exists. Names are stable, the plan is enforced, the warehouse is populated. A PM opens the analytics tool, builds the onboarding funnel, and reports that it converts at **34%**.

An analyst rebuilds it the same afternoon and gets **19%**. A growth engineer gets **52%**. The head of sales, quoting a number from a board deck, says **61%**. Nobody has made an error, nobody is querying different data, and the four numbers come from the same week of the same table.

```
same week, same events, one flow: signup_started → project_created → invite_sent

strict order, no other events between, single session      19%
ordered with gaps, 24-hour conversion window               34%
ordered with gaps, 7-day conversion window                 41%
any order, 30-day window                                   52%
counted per account rather than per user, 7-day window     61%
```

A funnel looks like one number. It is the output of at least five decisions — which events, counted over whom, in what order, within what window, entered when — and most tools set four of them by default while showing you only the result. The number on the slide has no parameters attached to it.

Then the recency problem, which is worse because it looks like information. Every weekly conversion chart the team builds slopes downward at the right-hand end, and it has done so every week for a year:

```
report run 14 Sep, 7-day conversion window

user A   started 2 Sep ──────▶ converted 6 Sep       ✓ counted
user B   started 5 Sep ──────▶ converted 11 Sep      ✓ counted
user C   started 12 Sep ─────▶ will convert 19 Sep   ✗ counted as a failure,
                                                        because it is only the 14th
```

Users who entered in the last seven days have not had seven days. The most recent bucket is structurally pessimistic, always, and it is also the bucket everyone looks at first. Six of the last twelve "conversion is dropping" investigations found nothing, which taught the team to ignore the chart, which is the expensive outcome — the seventh drop was real.

And retention, where the same trick runs again. "Our day-7 retention is 40%" — except one tool asks whether the user was active *exactly* on day 7, another whether they were active *on day 7 or any day after*, and a third whether they were active *any day between 7 and 13*. Three numbers, one dataset, and for a product people use once a week the first of the three produces a sawtooth that looks like a weekly crisis.

Underneath all of it: **an aggregate number describes a population and a clock, and the chart shows neither.**

## What It Is

Two ways of imposing structure on the event stream so that a population and a clock become explicit.

A **funnel** holds a group of people constant and varies the step: of everyone who did the first thing, how many did the second, the third. A **cohort** holds the starting point constant and varies elapsed time: of everyone who started in March, how many were still here a week later, a month later, a quarter later. Funnels answer *where do people stop*; cohorts answer *do people stay*. They are the same operation rotated ninety degrees, and both exist for the same reason — an average over a mixed population, measured on a calendar rather than from each person's own starting line, is a number that can move for reasons that have nothing to do with the product.

One neighbour is worth separating immediately, because the confusion is common and consequential.

**A cohort is not a segment.** A segment is defined by an attribute — plan is Pro, country is Germany, company size above 200. A cohort is defined by a shared event in a shared time window — signed up in the first week of March. The difference that matters is mutability: **cohort membership is fixed forever, segment membership is not.** A user who upgrades today leaves the Free segment and joins the Pro one, which silently changes the historical retention of both — Pro retention can improve because an existing loyal user upgraded, not because Pro is working. Cohorts don't do this; the March cohort is the same people next year as it is today. You can segment a cohort, and that combination is usually the useful thing; you cannot substitute one for the other.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Two people quote different conversion rates for the same flow, and both are looking at the same tool.
- Your conversion rate always appears to be declining, and the decline is always in the most recent days.
- The retention curve is a sawtooth with spikes on days 7, 14 and 21.
- Nobody can state the conversion window of your headline funnel without opening the tool.
- The aggregate metric is flat or falling while every individual segment is improving.
- "Activation rate" is on a dashboard and no written definition of activation exists.
- Someone reads the short, incomplete bottom-right corner of a cohort table as a trend.
- You know how many customers cancelled, but not when they stopped using the product.
- A funnel step reports more events than the step above it.
- You improved a step's conversion rate and total completions didn't move.
- A cohort chart has a dip that appears in a different column for each row.
- The metric moved on the day of a release, and you can't tell whether behaviour changed or the funnel definition's events did.
- Average sessions per active user is rising at the same time retention is falling, and this is being reported as good news.

## How It Works

### A funnel is five parameters

**1. The steps.** Events, optionally filtered by properties. `report_exported` and `report_exported where format = 'pdf'` are different funnels with different denominators.

**2. The counting basis.** Users, sessions, or accounts. A user who attempts the flow three times and succeeds once is 100% converted on a per-user basis and 33% on a per-session basis. In B2B, per-account is usually the question being asked — but note that per-account conversion counts the account as converted if *any* member finishes, which is why the account number in the table above is the highest of the five.

**3. The ordering rule.** *Strict* requires the steps in exact sequence with nothing in between — almost always too strict for real products, where people open a help doc mid-flow. *Ordered with gaps* requires the sequence but tolerates other events between, and is the right default. *Any order* stops being a funnel and becomes a set-membership question, which is sometimes what you actually want and should be labelled as such.

**4. The conversion window**, measured from that user's first step, not from a calendar date. This is the parameter most often wrong in hand-written queries, and §Implementation shows why.

**5. The entry window and re-entry policy.** Who is in the denominator: everyone who ever did step one, or everyone who did it during the report period? And if someone starts, abandons, then starts again next month — one row or two? Tools differ, silently.

None of the five has a universally right answer. What matters is that the answer is written down next to the number, because a funnel screenshot in a deck carries no parameters at all.

### Right-censoring, and the rule that follows

Any cohort whose conversion window has not fully elapsed will under-report, by an amount that grows the more recent the cohort. This is not noise — it is a deterministic artifact with a known shape, and it points the same direction every time.

The rule: **never compare a bucket whose window has closed with one whose hasn't.** Either exclude incomplete buckets or render them visibly differently, and put the convention in the report rather than in the head of the person who built it. A trend line that includes the current partial week is not a trend line.

The same artifact is why "conversions this month vs last month" is a broken comparison at the start of a month, and why the only honest version compares complete windows: full weeks against full weeks, day-30 against day-30.

### Drop-off is not the same as friction

A step where 70% of people leave looks like a problem. Two very different things produce it.

**Friction** — the step is hard, slow, confusing, or asks for something people don't have yet. Fixable at the step.

**Filtering** — the step is where people discover the product isn't for them. This is the funnel working. The improvement is not to reduce the drop; it is to move the discovery *earlier*, so people spend less of their time and yours finding out.

The diagnostic is segmentation, and it usually points upstream:

```
drop at step 3, by acquisition source

organic search    ████░░░░░░  38% drop     ← consistent across sources
referral          ████░░░░░░  41% drop        → friction at the step itself
paid social       ████████░░  79% drop     ← concentrated in one source
                                              → a targeting or expectation
                                                problem before the funnel started
```

Which is the general lesson: **you usually cannot fix a funnel by looking only at the funnel.** A step that converts badly for one channel and fine for four others is not a UI bug, and a redesign of that screen will produce a week of work and no movement.

The other dimension the rate hides is **time to convert**. Two steps both converting at 60%, one with a median of 20 seconds and one with a median of three days, are different products with different problems — and the three-day one has a re-engagement opportunity the rate alone will never surface.

### Retention has three definitions and they disagree on purpose

Take one user active on days 0, 3 and 10, and ask whether they were retained at day 7:

```
                    definition                              answer
N-day (exact)       active on day 7 exactly                   no
unbounded           active on day 7 or any day after          yes
bucketed (range)    active any day within days 7–13           yes
```

Each is right for a different question. **N-day** is the strictest and is honest only for products with a genuine daily habit; applied to a weekly product it produces a sawtooth, because everyone shows up on day 7 and day 14 and nobody shows up on day 9. **Unbounded** asks "did they ever come back," which is the right question when judging whether the product has any staying power at all, and it can only be computed for cohorts old enough to have had the chance. **Bucketed** smooths over usage cadence and is the sane default for almost everything that isn't a daily-habit product.

The rule underneath: **match the bucket to the product's natural cadence.** Weekly buckets for a weekly product, monthly for a monthly one. Measuring a payroll tool on daily retention produces a chart that is 95% zeros and describes nothing.

And "retained" needs "active" defined, which is the same trap in miniature: logged in, or did the thing the product is for? Those diverge enormously, and the first one is the easier number to hit.

### What a retention curve is actually for

The day-1 number gets the attention. The shape is the information.

```
100 ┤●
    │ ●
 60 ┤  ●
    │   ●●
 30 ┤     ●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●●   plateau — a group found durable value.
    │                           the height of the plateau, times how
    │                           many you can acquire, is the business.
  0 ┤ ○○○○○○○○
    │         ○○○○○○○○○○○○○○○   decays to zero — nobody stuck. no
    │                           acquisition spend fixes this shape.
    └────────────────────────────
      0    7   14   21   28   35   days
```

A curve that flattens to a non-zero asymptote means some segment of users found something they keep coming back for; the work is then to find out who they are and acquire more of them. A curve that continues toward zero means the product is a leaking bucket, and every growth tactic applied to it is a more expensive way to lose the same people. This is the single most useful read in the module, and it requires no statistical sophistication — only that the curve be drawn from cohorts rather than from a calendar average, which would have mixed new and old users together and hidden the shape entirely.

### Reading a cohort table in three directions

```
% of cohort active, by weeks since signup   (w0 is 100% by construction)

                w1      w2      w3      w4
Jul wk4        62%     45%     41%     26% ●
Aug wk1        61%     44%     29% ●   38%
Aug wk2        60%     28% ●   36%      —
Aug wk3        27% ●   35%      —       —
Aug wk4        59%      —       —       —
```

**Across a row** is one cohort ageing — the retention curve for that group.

**Down a column** is the same age across cohorts — the comparison that tells you whether changes to the product are working, because it holds elapsed time constant.

**Along a diagonal** is calendar time. Every marked cell above happened in the same calendar week. The naive read of this table is "our cohorts are getting much worse and then recovering." The real read is that one week in August was bad for everyone — an outage, a broken release, a holiday, a pricing change — and it has nothing to do with the cohorts at all. **A dip that traces a diagonal is a calendar event. A dip that stays in a column is a cohort effect.** Learning to see that distinction is most of what cohort literacy consists of.

### Churn is an absence, and absences are observed late

Nobody emits `user_churned`. You observe that events stopped, and "stopped" is only distinguishable from "hasn't happened yet this week" after you decide how long to wait.

So behavioural churn requires a threshold — no core action in 28 days, say — and that threshold is a guess about your product's cadence that directly determines the number. The consequence is structural: **you can never know this month's churn.** You know churn as of one threshold-length ago, and the most recent period is censored in exactly the way the funnel's last bucket was.

This is a different quantity from the revenue churn defined in 15.5, and the gap between them is the interesting part. Behavioural churn happens first — often weeks or, on an annual contract, most of a year before the cancellation. Billing sees the cancellation; only the event stream sees the silence that preceded it. That window is the entire premise of customer success as a function, and a system that only measures revenue churn discovers every departure at the moment it has become irreversible.

### Two ways aggregates lie, both common

**Survivorship.** Average sessions per active user rises. Nothing improved — the least engaged users left, and the average is computed over whoever remains. Any metric averaged over currently-active users improves when churn increases, which means that metric cannot be used to judge the health of the product without the retention curve next to it.

**Mix shift.** Both channels improved. The total got worse:

```
                    August                    September
paid search    1,000 → 200    20%        6,000 → 1,320    22%
organic        2,000 → 800    40%        2,000 →   860    43%
                    ─────────────             ──────────────
total          3,000 → 1,000  33.3%      8,000 → 2,180    27.3%
```

The paid campaign worked, conversion improved in both segments, and the headline rate fell six points because the lower-converting channel became most of the volume. This is Simpson's paradox with a marketing budget attached, and the practical rule it produces is: **never read a change in an aggregate rate without also looking at what changed in the mix.** If the volumes moved, the rate comparison is not a comparison.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A flow of three or more steps where people genuinely leave**, and enough volume to see a change. At roughly 1,000 entries a week, a move from 40% to 45% is visible; at 50 a week it is not.
- **Judging a release.** Cohort by release exposure rather than reading a calendar average, which mixes users who never saw the change into the same line.
- **Onboarding and activation work**, which is the canonical funnel and usually the highest-leverage one, because everything downstream is multiplied by it.
- **Asking whether the product works at all.** The retention plateau answers this and almost nothing else does — and it answers it before you have spent a year on acquisition.
- **Pricing and packaging work.** Cohorting by plan-at-signup separates "Pro users retain better" from "users who would have retained anyway chose Pro."
- **Churn prevention in B2B.** A behavioural threshold at the account level gives customer success a list weeks before the renewal conversation, which is the only point at which the list is actionable.
- **Choosing what to experiment on.** The funnel locates the biggest drop; the experiment then tests one explanation for it.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Volumes where the interval swamps the effect.** Fifty entries a week at 40% conversion carries a 95% interval of roughly ±13 points. Every week will look different from the last, and none of those differences will be real. Aggregate to a longer period or accept that the answer is "we can't tell yet."
- **Flows with no genuine sequence.** If users legitimately do A, B and C in any order, a funnel imposes an ordering that exists only inside the analytics tool, and the drop-offs you find are artifacts of your definition.
- **Before event names are stable.** Building funnels on an unenforced tracking plan (16.1a, L2) measures instrumentation changes and reports them as user behaviour.
- **Retention charts for products with a one-shot job.** A visa application, a house sale, an annual tax filing. Here a returning user may be a *failure* signal, and importing the retention framework wholesale leads teams to optimise for people needing the product again.
- **Slicing until something is significant.** Twenty segments tested at the 5% level produce a discovery by construction. If the segment wasn't named before the query, treat what you find as a hypothesis, not a result.
- **Using a funnel to answer *why*.** It locates the drop and never explains it. Ten session replays or five user interviews at the drop step routinely beat another week of slicing, and cost less.

## Trade-offs

- **A funnel is a hypothesis you've frozen into a report.** It can only show the path you defined. A large group succeeding by a different route is invisible, and shows up as failure — which then gets optimised against.
- **Definitions become political the moment they become targets.** The cheapest way to improve activation rate is to redefine activation, and it will be proposed sincerely. The defence is a written, versioned definition with a named owner and a change process.
- **Redefinitions rewrite history.** Changing a metric's definition moves the whole trend line, and everyone's intuition about what "normal" looks like is invalidated at once. Treat it like a schema migration: announce it, compute both in parallel for a period, then cut over — never quietly swap.
- **The parameters are invisible in the artifact.** A chart in a deck carries no window, no ordering rule, no counting basis. The number will travel; the definition won't.
- **Incomplete buckets mislead every reader who doesn't know the convention**, and most readers don't. Greying them out is not decoration.
- **Cohort tables are expensive to compute and go stale**, and the temptation to materialise them at a fixed grain is also the decision that makes the next question ("can we see this by plan?") a rebuild.
- **Approximate distinct counts break arithmetic.** Analytics tools use sketch-based cardinality estimates for speed, and estimates do not subtract: the difference between two approximate step counts is not the number of people who dropped, and can come out negative. Exact counts on the warehouse and approximate ones in the tool will disagree, and both are behaving as designed.
- **You are optimising against the population you currently have.** A funnel tuned to today's traffic mix is tuned to today's acquisition strategy, and it will quietly resist changing it.

## Alternatives

**Path or flow analysis.** Rather than testing a defined sequence, show where people actually go from a given point. It finds the funnel you should have defined, which is genuinely valuable once; it is noisy, hard to act on, and does not give you a number to track.

**Session replay at the drop step.** Watch ten people fail. This answers *why*, which no amount of aggregation will, and it is frequently the fastest path from "we know where they leave" to "we know what to change." Small n, no statistical claim, and that's fine — it generates the hypothesis the funnel then measures.

**Survival analysis.** Kaplan–Meier curves, hazard rates, and Cox models are the statistically correct treatment of exactly this data: time-to-event observations where many subjects haven't had the event yet. It handles right-censoring properly instead of by greying out cells, and it answers questions the cohort grid can't — at what point is the hazard highest, which covariates actually predict departure. Underused in product analytics largely because the tools don't offer it, and worth knowing by name for when the cohort table stops being enough.

**Controlled experiments.** Not a substitute — a complement, and the division of labour is clean. Funnels and cohorts tell you what happened to the users you had; an experiment tells you what a change caused. Observational funnel comparisons are full of confounds (the users who saw the new flow are also the users who arrived in October), and the usual healthy pattern is that the funnel chooses the experiment.

**Predictive churn models.** Feed behavioural features to a classifier and rank accounts by risk (Module 39). Often worth less than it sounds relative to a simple threshold rule, because the CSM has to act on the output and a model that says "0.72" without saying why produces no action. A rule that says "no admin has logged in for three weeks and seat usage halved" is worse at prediction and better at causing something to happen.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — count outcomes.** Signups, purchases, exports. No sequence, no cohorts. Fine, and it answers the first questions honestly.

**L1 — one funnel, with its definition written down.** Pick the flow that matters most, fix all five parameters explicitly in a document, compute it in SQL, publish one number weekly. *Signal to start:* two people quoting different conversion rates for the same flow.

**L2 — handle censoring and segment by one dimension.** Exclude or mark incomplete windows; break the funnel down by acquisition source. *Signal:* the chart "always declines recently," or an aggregate that contradicts its own segments.

**L3 — cohort retention, bucketed to your product's cadence, read for the plateau.** *Signal:* someone asks whether the product is working, rather than whether a screen is.

**L4 — a behavioural churn threshold, fed back into the product.** Define inactivity, compute it per account, and route it somewhere that acts — a CS queue, a re-engagement sequence. *Signal:* churn is being discovered at renewal.

**L5 — definitions as versioned, owned artifacts.** A metrics or semantic layer where activation, active, and retained are defined once and consumed identically by the tool, the warehouse, and the in-app dashboard. *Signal:* a third system disagreeing about a headline number, or a redefinition that invalidated a board deck after it was presented.

Most teams overshoot to L3 and skip L1's written definition, which is the step that actually stops the four-numbers problem.

## Implementation

SQL, because funnels and cohorts are query shapes rather than application code, and the mistakes live in the query.

The funnel, with the conversion window done correctly:

```sql
with entries as (                              -- the entry cohort, fixed
  select user_id, min(occurred_at) as t0
  from events
  where name = 'signup_started'
    and occurred_at >= date '2026-09-01'
    and occurred_at <  date '2026-09-08'       -- entry window: a calendar range,
  group by user_id                             -- and the ONLY calendar range here
),
s2 as (
  select e.user_id, min(ev.occurred_at) as t2
  from entries e
  join events ev on ev.user_id = e.user_id
   and ev.name = 'project_created'
   and ev.occurred_at >= e.t0                            -- ordering
   and ev.occurred_at <  e.t0 + interval '7 days'        -- window, per user,
  group by e.user_id                                     -- relative to their t0
),
s3 as (
  select s2.user_id
  from s2
  join entries e using (user_id)
  join events ev on ev.user_id = s2.user_id
   and ev.name = 'invite_sent'
   and ev.occurred_at >= s2.t2                           -- from the PREVIOUS step,
   and ev.occurred_at <  e.t0 + interval '7 days'        -- window still from t0
  group by s2.user_id
)
select
  (select count(*) from entries) as step_1,
  (select count(*) from s2)      as step_2,
  (select count(*) from s3)      as step_3;
```

The one line that separates a correct funnel from the most common broken one is `ev.occurred_at < e.t0 + interval '7 days'`. The tempting version puts the same calendar `between` clause on every step, which counts an `invite_sent` from a user who entered six weeks ago, produces step counts that can exceed the step above them, and is exactly the bug behind every funnel anyone has ever seen report over 100%.

Note also that each step joins against the *previous* step's timestamp, not the first — that is what "ordered" means, and dropping it quietly converts the query into an any-order set intersection while still looking like a funnel.

Retention:

```sql
with cohort as (
  select user_id,
         date_trunc('week', min(occurred_at)) as cohort_week,
         min(occurred_at) as t0
  from events where name = 'signed_up'
  group by user_id
),
activity as (
  select c.cohort_week,
         c.user_id,
         floor(extract(epoch from a.occurred_at - c.t0) / 604800)::int as week_n
  from cohort c
  join events a on a.user_id = c.user_id
   and a.name = 'project_created'          -- "active" means the core action,
  where a.occurred_at >= c.t0              -- not "logged in". this line is the
)                                          -- definition everyone argues about
select cohort_week,
       week_n,
       count(distinct user_id) as retained
from activity
where cohort_week + (week_n + 1) * interval '7 days' <= now()   -- drop incomplete
group by cohort_week, week_n                                     -- buckets
order by cohort_week, week_n;
```

Three things the query makes explicit that a tool would have decided for you.

Bucketing by `floor(elapsed / one week)` is the bucketed definition. Exact N-day retention is `elapsed_days = 7` and unbounded retention is `elapsed_days >= 7` — one predicate apart in the SQL, and a different chart, a different headline number, and occasionally a different strategic conclusion.

The `where` clause dropping incomplete buckets is the censoring rule expressed as code rather than as a convention someone has to remember. If it isn't in the query, it has to be in the reader, and it won't be.

And `count(distinct user_id)` is exact here and will be an approximation in any tool operating at scale — worth knowing when the warehouse and the dashboard disagree by a percent, because neither is broken.

## Further Reading

- **Amplitude's and Mixpanel's retention documentation.** Both define N-day, unbounded and bracketed/bucketed retention explicitly — and they use different names for the same things, which is itself the lesson about why your own definition needs writing down.
- **Social Capital's 2015 investor letters on growth accounting.** The decomposition of net user growth into new, retained, resurrected and churned, plus the quick ratio. The clearest public treatment of why a flat user count can hide two large opposing flows.
- **Andrew Chen and Brian Balfour on retention curves and product/market fit**, and Casey Winters on the flattening curve. The argument that the asymptote, not the day-1 number, is the thing to read.
- **Lenny Rachitsky's retention and conversion benchmark posts** — useful for rough calibration, with the standing caveat that self-reported benchmarks are computed under definitions you can't see.
- **An introduction to survival analysis** — Kaplan–Meier estimators, hazard functions, and Cox proportional hazards. Any applied biostatistics text does this well. Churn is censored time-to-event data and there is a mature field for it that product analytics mostly ignores.
- **Kohavi, Tang & Xu, _Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments_.** For the boundary between what a funnel can tell you and what only an experiment can, for Twyman's law, and for the multiple-comparisons discipline that slicing violates by default.
- **Kimball & Ross, _The Data Warehouse Toolkit_ — periodic snapshot fact tables.** A cohort table is one, and the grain discussion tells you what you're locking in when you materialise it.
- **dbt's semantic layer / metrics documentation.** A concrete implementation of L5: a metric definition as a versioned artifact in source control rather than a saved report in a vendor's UI.

A **Python sibling** would be worth writing, and it would not be a translation. The SQL above approximates survival analysis with a grid of buckets because that is what SQL does comfortably; pandas with `lifelines` makes the actual machinery available — Kaplan–Meier curves with confidence bands that handle censoring correctly rather than by discarding rows, hazard curves that show *when* risk peaks rather than only how much is left, and Cox regression to ask which behaviours predict departure once the others are held constant. That is a different set of answers to the same question, and the shift from "what fraction remain" to "what is the instantaneous risk, and what drives it" is the point at which cohort grids stop being enough. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying past analytics: **every summary number has a population and a clock, and the chart displays neither.** Before believing one — your own included — ask who is in the denominator, when their clock started, and whether it has finished running. Most numbers that mislead a team are not wrong; they are correct answers to a question nobody stated, quoted by someone who assumed a different one.
