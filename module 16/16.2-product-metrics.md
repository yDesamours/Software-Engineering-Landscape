# Product Metrics

_Module 16.2 — Analytics & Metrics_

## The Problem

The events are clean and the funnels are defined. The remaining question is which numbers go on the wall — and this is where a team with excellent data can still spend two years steering by numbers that don't describe anything.

**Month 1 — the dashboard.** Twelve tiles: signups, DAU, MAU, sessions, average session length, feature adoption, NPS, tickets, MRR, churn, conversion, retention. Everyone looks at it for a fortnight. Then nobody does. Twelve numbers with no stated relationship between them cannot be read, because there is no way to be *surprised* by them — no tile implies anything about any other tile, so no combination of values is unexpected.

**Month 4 — DAU/MAU rises from 0.24 to 0.31.** Reported as engagement improving. What happened is that marketing switched off a channel that had been bringing in occasional, low-frequency users. Every remaining user behaves exactly as they did before. The ratio went up because the denominator got smaller.

**Month 7 — average session length is up 40%.** Two readings: people are getting more value, or people cannot find what they came for. The team has no way to distinguish them, and the number goes into the board deck under "engagement."

**Month 9 — activation becomes a quarterly target.** Q1 is honest work: onboarding improves, the rate goes from 34% to 41%. In Q2 someone notices that activation is defined as "created a project *and* invited a teammate," and that the invite step is where most of the loss is, and that the definition is a line in a dashboard config. Redefined to "created a project," the rate is 68% on Monday morning. Nobody lied; the argument for the change was sincere and even reasonable. In Q3 the product auto-creates a starter project during signup, and the rate hits 91%. Ninety-day retention has not moved once in three quarters.

**Month 11 — feature adoption.** "62% of accounts have used Dashboards." Someone finally looks at the second week: 4% came back. Adoption measured as *ever touched it* is mostly a measurement of where you put the button.

**Month 14 — churn climbs**, and the eventual post-mortem traces it to a decision made in month 8. Everything the team was watching resolved after the fact. They were driving by the rear-view mirror and the mirror had a six-month delay.

The thread through all six: **a metric is a proxy, and every proxy has a gap between it and the thing you actually care about.** DAU/MAU is a proxy for engagement, with a gap you can drive a mix-shift through. Activation is a proxy for "got value," with a gap the size of a config change. Session length is a proxy for two opposite things at once. Choosing metrics is not choosing what to measure — it's choosing which gaps you can live with, and saying out loud what you'd expect to see if the gap opened up.

## What It Is

**Product metrics** are a small, deliberately structured set of quantities that stand in for "is the product working," with the relationships between them made explicit rather than implied by being on the same screen.

The neighbours they get confused with are separated by lag and audience. **Business metrics** — MRR, ARR, CAC, LTV, logo churn (Modules 1.1 and 15.5) — describe the company, are what a board asks for, and are lagging by construction: on an annual contract, the business metric for a decision you make today resolves next year. **Technical metrics** — latency, error rate, saturation (16.3 and Module 19) — describe the system, resolve in seconds, and say nothing about whether anyone wanted what the system did. Product metrics sit between: they describe behaviour, they move in days to weeks, and their entire value is that they *lead* the business metrics. A team steering only on business metrics is correcting the last quarter's mistakes; a team steering only on technical metrics is making sure the wrong thing is fast.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The dashboard has a dozen tiles and nobody can say which one matters most.
- Two teams are moving metrics that trade off against each other, and neither knows.
- A metric on the wall cannot be connected to retention or revenue by any chain of reasoning anyone can state.
- "Engagement is up" — and nobody can finish the sentence.
- A metric improved because its definition changed.
- Every metric you review is lagging, so bad decisions are discovered a quarter later.
- Three people give three definitions of activation, or nobody gives one.
- Adoption numbers count one-time use.
- Prominent numbers are cumulative totals — total users, total projects created — which cannot go down and therefore cannot be informative.
- You can't tell whether a metric moved because behaviour changed or because the population did.
- The monthly review consists of movements that are within noise, so it has quietly become theatre.
- Someone hit their number and nothing about the business changed.
- A number that has been the company's headline metric for eight quarters is still the company's headline metric.

## How It Works

### Metrics have roles, and mixing them is most of the damage

**A primary (or north star) metric** — the one that, if it moves, everyone agrees the product is working. Three requirements, all of which get skipped: it describes *value delivered to a user*, not activity; the team can actually move it; and it correlates with revenue on a lag you can state.

**Input metrics** — the small set that composes into the primary. The test is arithmetic: if you can't write the inputs as a formula that produces the top number, you have a list, not a tree, and moving an input carries no obligation to move the top.

**Guardrails** — quantities that must not degrade while you move the primary. Latency, ticket volume per account, refund rate, unsubscribe rate. These are thresholds, not targets; the goal is never to improve them, only to notice.

**A counter-metric** — chosen deliberately as the thing the cheapest way of gaming the primary would damage. Named at the same moment the primary is named, by asking "if I wanted to move this without helping anyone, what would I do, and what would it break?"

```
                    weekly active teams that shipped a report
                                (primary)
                    ┌───────────────┼────────────────┐
         teams activated      ×  retention of   ×  reports per
      (signups × act. rate)      active teams      active team
              │                       │                 │
       traffic, signup CR      onboarding, support   templates, integrations

guardrails     p95 latency · tickets per 100 teams · refund rate
counter-metric reports deleted or never opened within 24h
```

Read the tree downward and it says what to work on. Read it upward and it says why the work matters. A dashboard that can't be read in either direction is a dozen tiles.

### DAU/MAU measures cadence, not engagement

The ratio is the fraction of a month's active users who are present on an average day — which is the same as saying that the average active user shows up on `30 × ratio` days per month. A ratio of 0.20 means six days a month. That is a statement about **cadence**, and it inherits three properties people forget.

It is only interpretable for products with a genuine daily rhythm. A payroll tool run twice a month has a ratio around 0.07 and is working perfectly. Comparing your ratio to a published benchmark from a messaging app compares two different products' natural frequencies.

It moves when the mix moves, with nobody's behaviour changing — which is the month-4 failure above, in one sentence.

And it averages a population that is usually not shaped like its average:

```
distinct days active in the last 28, among all "monthly active" users

  1–3   ████████████████████████████████   61%   ← the bulk of your MAU
  4–7   ███████████                        21%
  8–14  █████                              10%
 15–21  ██                                  5%
 22–28  ██                                  3%   ← the actual product
```

One number summarising that distribution describes nobody in it. The better instrument is the distribution itself — how many days out of the last 28 — because it shows immediately whether you have one population or two, and whether growth is adding core users or adding tail. A rising DAU/MAU with a flat core band is a mix change; a growing right-hand tail is the product working.

### Defining activation without fooling yourself

Activation is the claim that some early behaviour marks the point where a user has understood the value. The standard way to find it: take cohorts old enough to have an outcome, list the things people did in their first week, and see which of them best separates the users who stayed from the users who left.

That procedure produces candidates and is routinely mistaken for producing causes. The famous examples — a file in a folder, a number of messages sent, a number of connections in ten days — were all found this way, and the failure that follows is always the same: the team forces the behaviour, and retention doesn't move, because the behaviour was a **symptom** of a user who had already found the product useful, not the mechanism by which they found it. Auto-adding contacts does not manufacture the thing that made the contact count predictive.

So the honest sequence has four steps, and most teams stop after one: find the candidate by correlation; check that there is a plausible mechanism, in words, for why doing this *causes* someone to stay; run an experiment that causes the behaviour for a random group; and if forcing it doesn't move retention, discard it as a symptom and go back to the list. Most candidates are symptoms.

Two constraints on the definition itself. It needs a **time bound** — "within 7 days of signup" — or it can't be computed for recent cohorts at all. And it needs enough **coverage** to be a goal: a behaviour that 2% of users perform and that predicts 90% retention is not an activation definition, it's a description of your power users.

### Adoption has three depths and teams report the shallowest

**Reach** — ever used it. Mostly measures where you put the button and how loud the announcement was.

**Frequency** — used it in more than one week. This is where most feature launches are revealed.

**Feature retention** — still using it a month later, measured with the cohort machinery from 16.1b applied to a single feature rather than to the product. Rarely done, and it is the one that answers "should we invest more in this."

62% reach with 4% second-week return is not a partial success. It is a discovery success and a product failure, and reporting it as "62% adoption" prevents the team from learning that.

In B2B there is a fourth dimension — **breadth within the account**: one admin using it versus fourteen of twenty seats. Renewal risk lives in that number far more than in reach.

### Per user or per account

In B2B, user-level averages describe your largest customer. One account with 400 seats outweighs 200 small accounts in any mean computed over users, so "average projects per user" can fall while every single account improves, purely because a large customer onboarded.

The rule: compute per account, then aggregate across accounts, and say which you did. Both are legitimate, they answer different questions, and mixing them within one dashboard produces numbers that disagree for structural reasons nobody can find later.

### Goodhart, mechanically

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" is usually quoted as a warning about bad faith. In practice it operates through three mechanisms, and only the third looks like cheating.

**Redefinition.** Change what counts. This is the most common, the cheapest, and it is almost always proposed sincerely with a good argument — which is exactly why it works.

**Selection.** Change the population. Stop counting trial users, exclude a segment as "not our ICP," narrow the denominator. Same data, better number.

**Behaviour.** Change the product so the number moves without value moving. A forced step that guarantees the activation event. Notifications that raise DAU and raise unsubscribes. A flow that makes the measured action unavoidable rather than desirable.

Four defences, none of which is "trust people":

- Every primary has a named counter-metric, chosen before the target is set.
- Definitions are versioned artifacts with an owner and a change process, not settings in a dashboard (16.1b, L5).
- Targets are stated as a range with guardrails attached, not a single number that can be reached from any direction.
- Reviews ask what was *done* to move the metric, not only whether it moved. This is the only one that catches the third mechanism.

And a fifth that is rarely stated: **rotate what's on the wall.** The longer a metric is a target, the larger the fraction of its remaining movement that comes from the first two mechanisms. A metric that has been the headline for two years is no longer telling you anything you don't already know.

### Know your noise before you react to it

A metric moving three percent week-on-week is usually nothing, and the review meeting will discuss it anyway. Before a metric goes on a wall, compute its ordinary week-to-week standard deviation over a period when nothing notable happened, and write the band down next to it. Anything inside roughly two standard deviations is not an event.

Almost every B2B product also has a hard weekly cycle, which makes the raw series unreadable:

```
DAU, raw                          DAU, 7-day trailing mean

  ▁███████▁▁  ▁███████▁▁            ─────────────────────────
  M T W T F S S  M T W T F S S      the only line you can read a trend from
```

Compare like days to like days, or use a trailing seven-day window, and apply the same rule to month lengths — February is not a bad month.

## When It's Appropriate

- **More than one team.** The tree is the mechanism by which two teams avoid optimising against each other, and it is the main reason to build one at all.
- **Before setting quarterly goals**, so that the goal is an input metric someone can actually move rather than an outcome they can only hope for.
- **When the business metric is too slow to steer by.** Annual contracts, long sales cycles, anything where revenue resolves in months. This is the case that most justifies leading indicators.
- **Deciding whether to invest further in a feature** — which requires depth and breadth, not reach.
- **B2B account health and renewal risk**, where behaviour predicts the renewal conversation weeks or quarters ahead of it.
- **Before you're asked for numbers by an investor or a board**, because otherwise you will be given someone else's metrics and will spend a year defending them.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Pre-product-market-fit with a few dozen users.** The conversation beats the number, for the same reasons 16.1a gives.
- **A north star before a product thesis.** The metric is downstream of knowing what value you deliver; picking one first produces a number the team steers by and nobody believes, and the arguments it causes are unresolvable because they are actually arguments about the thesis.
- **Copying another company's primary metric.** Nights booked, time listening, messages sent — each encodes that company's business model and cost structure. The resemblance to your product is usually superficial and the borrowed metric will be wrong in ways that take a year to surface.
- **A metrics programme for three people who all talk to customers weekly.** The overhead exceeds the information.
- **Cumulative totals**, on any wall, ever. They only go up.
- **NPS at low volume.** With 50 responses the 95% interval is roughly ±20 points on a scale that runs from −100 to 100. Every quarter will differ from the last, and none of those differences will mean anything.

## Trade-offs

- **Putting a number on a wall starts a clock on its informational value.** From that moment, some fraction of its future movement comes from the measure being targeted rather than the product changing, and that fraction only grows.
- **A metric tree is a model of your business, and it will be wrong before it is retired.** It encodes an assumption — that more reports shipped means more value — and when that stops being true, the tree keeps directing work as though it were.
- **Simplification is the point and the cost.** A north star's value is coordination, not truth. Teams forget this and begin arguing about reality using a number that was only ever meant to align them.
- **Measurable work outcompetes unmeasurable work** in every prioritisation meeting, regardless of value. Accessibility, security hardening, and technical health lose that argument structurally, and a stronger metrics culture makes them lose harder.
- **Improving a definition costs you your history.** You are choosing between a better metric going forward and a comparable series going back, and you cannot have both.
- **Reporting cadence imposes a rhythm the product doesn't have.** Reviewing monthly a metric whose real signal takes a quarter guarantees decisions driven by noise.
- **Numbers travel and definitions don't.** The figure ends up in a deck, a job posting, a press release, and a competitor's analysis, stripped of the window, the counting basis, and the caveat.
- **Ongoing maintenance**: an owner per metric, a review when features change what the underlying events mean, and a standing "how will we know" question on every spec.

## Alternatives

**Revenue and churn alone.** Unarguable, hard to game, and lagging so badly that you cannot steer with them — worst on annual contracts, where the feedback loop is a year long. For a small business with monthly billing and a short cycle, this is a more reasonable position than metrics culture usually admits.

**Qualitative signal as the primary instrument.** Read every support ticket, five customer calls a week, a standing review of churn reasons in the customers' own words. Scales further than people expect, answers *why* rather than *what*, and is the correct primary instrument early. Its weakness is that it cannot detect a slow, broad change — only the loud ones write in.

**Experiment-led decisions with no north star.** Hold a fixed evaluation criterion and measure each change against it; don't try to summarise the product in one number. Strong for feature decisions, weak for coordination, and it provides no answer to "are we winning."

**Health scores** — a weighted composite of behaviours per account, ubiquitous in customer success tooling. The appeal is one number per account; the weakness is that a composite cannot be debugged. It moves, nobody can say which component moved it, and the weights were guessed by someone who has left.

**Working backwards.** Write the launch announcement and the FAQ before building, and define in prose what success would look like. Not a measurement system and not a substitute for one, but a genuinely effective way to choose *which* measurement matters before the incentive to pick a flattering one exists.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — no metrics.** Ship, and talk to users. Correct until there are more users than conversations.

**L1 — one primary metric with a written definition.** Pick a unit of value delivered, define it with a time bound and a counting basis, publish it weekly, and write down its ordinary variance. *Signal to start:* two teams setting goals that pull against each other.

**L2 — inputs that compose arithmetically, plus a named counter-metric.** *Signal:* the primary moved and nobody could say which input caused it.

**L3 — guardrails and noise bands.** Thresholds that trigger a look rather than a target, a seasonality-adjusted view, and an agreed rule for what counts as a real movement. *Signal:* a review spent discussing a change that later turned out to be nothing.

**L4 — segmentation, and account-weighting in B2B.** By cohort, plan, size, acquisition source. *Signal:* the aggregate contradicts what customer-facing teams are hearing, which is the classic symptom of a mean over a mixed population.

**L5 — definitions as versioned, owned artifacts.** Metric definitions in source control, computed once, consumed identically by the dashboard, the warehouse, and any in-product number — plus a rotation policy for what's on the wall. *Signal:* the third system disagreeing about a headline figure, or a metric that has been the target long enough that its movement no longer surprises anyone.

## Implementation

SQL, because the one genuinely hard computation in this article is the activation-candidate scan, and it is a query.

```sql
-- Which early behaviours separate the users who stayed from the ones who left?
with cohort as (
  select user_id, min(occurred_at) as t0
  from events
  where name = 'signed_up'
    and occurred_at >= now() - interval '180 days'
    and occurred_at <  now() - interval '90 days'   -- old enough to HAVE an outcome
  group by user_id
),
did as (                              -- everything they did in their first week
  select c.user_id, e.name as behaviour
  from cohort c
  join events e on e.user_id = c.user_id
   and e.occurred_at >= c.t0
   and e.occurred_at <  c.t0 + interval '7 days'
  group by c.user_id, e.name
),
outcome as (                          -- still doing the core action in week 12
  select c.user_id,
         bool_or(e.occurred_at >= c.t0 + interval '77 days'
             and e.occurred_at <  c.t0 + interval '84 days') as kept
  from cohort c
  left join events e
    on e.user_id = c.user_id and e.name = 'project_created'
  group by c.user_id
)
select d.behaviour,
       count(*)                                            as users,
       round(100.0 * count(*) / (select count(*) from cohort), 1) as coverage_pct,
       round(100.0 * avg(o.kept::int), 1)                  as retained_if_did,
       round(100.0 * (select avg(kept::int) from outcome), 1) as retained_overall
from did d
join outcome o using (user_id)
group by d.behaviour
having count(*) >= 200                -- below this the lift is noise
order by retained_if_did desc;
```

Three asides the query surfaces on its own.

**`coverage_pct` is why the result is ranked and not maximised.** The top row by lift is almost always a rare behaviour performed by people who were going to stay anyway. What you want is the best combination of lift and reachable coverage, and that is a judgement made by reading the table, not a number the query can produce.

**The cohort window is 90 to 180 days old**, because a user needs an outcome before they can be in this analysis. Which means your activation definition is always derived from a version of the product that is at least three months stale, and after a significant redesign it needs re-deriving rather than inheriting.

**Nothing here establishes causation.** The output is a list of candidates. The step that separates a real activation definition from an expensive misunderstanding is the experiment that *causes* the behaviour in a random group and checks whether retention follows.

The engagement distribution, which replaces DAU/MAU rather than supplementing it:

```sql
with days as (
  select user_id, count(distinct occurred_at::date) as active_days
  from events
  where name = 'project_created'                       -- "active" = the core action
    and occurred_at >= current_date - interval '28 days'
  group by user_id
)
select width_bucket(active_days, 1, 29, 5) as bucket,
       min(active_days) || '–' || max(active_days) as days_active,
       count(*) as users
from days
group by 1
order by 1;
```

DAU/MAU is the mean of this distribution divided by 28. Running both for a month is the cheapest way to find out whether your headline ratio is describing a population or averaging two.

## Further Reading

- **Amplitude's North Star Playbook.** Free, short, and the clearest public statement of the tree structure — primary metric, input metrics, and the requirement that inputs compose rather than merely relate.
- **Bryar & Carr, _Working Backwards_.** The Amazon distinction between controllable input metrics and output metrics, and the case for holding teams to the former. The most useful single framing in this article's subject.
- **Muller, _The Tyranny of Metrics_.** What measurement does to organisations, drawn mostly from healthcare and education, where the consequences are larger and better documented than in software. Read it as the counterweight to everything else on this list.
- **Goodhart's law in its original and in Strathern's more-quoted phrasing**, and Campbell's law alongside it — worth ten minutes to see that the two were formulated independently about entirely different fields.
- **Kohavi, Tang & Xu, _Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments_** — the OEC, guardrail metrics, and the discipline of fixing the criterion before the experiment. Cited in 16.1b for causality; here for how to choose the number in the first place.
- **Sean Ellis and the Reforge material on activation and the "aha moment."** The source of the technique in this article, and worth reading alongside the caveat this article attaches to it.
- **Croll & Yoskovitz, _Lean Analytics_.** The "one metric that matters" argument, with the stage-dependent framing that keeps it from being applied to a company that isn't ready for it.
- **dbt's metrics and semantic-layer documentation.** A concrete implementation of L5 — a metric as a versioned artifact in source control rather than a saved view in a vendor's UI.

**No language sibling is proposed for this article.** There is nothing here that a second language reveals; the SQL is the analysis, and rewriting it in pandas would change the syntax and nothing else. The companion that *would* add real value is an article on **experimentation** — randomisation, sample size, the criterion chosen before the test, and why an observational comparison between cohorts who saw different versions is usually confounded. Both this article and 16.1b hand work to it, and the map currently has no home for it: A/B testing appears only as a bullet inside admin tooling and inside model serving. Worth someone claiming.

---

The habit worth carrying past analytics: **a metric is a bet that a proxy will keep tracking the thing you actually want, and pushing on the proxy is what breaks the link fastest.** So make the bet explicit when you make it. Write down, next to the number, what you would expect to see if this metric improved while the product got worse — the unsubscribes, the deleted reports, the tickets, the second-week return. If you can't name that, you haven't chosen a metric yet; you've chosen a number that can only bring good news.
