# Dashboards & the Consumption Layer

_Module 16.5 — Analytics & Metrics_

## The Problem

The pipeline from 16.4 works. The data is correct, the definitions are enforced, the warehouse is fresh. And the organisation is no better at making decisions than it was before.

**The graveyard.** Someone audits the BI tool: 212 dashboards. Nine have been opened in the last thirty days. A hundred and forty were created by people who no longer work here. Four are called some variation of "Copy of Weekly Metrics (new)."

**The fourth definition.** A team needs active accounts, finds three dashboards showing three different numbers, gives up on all of them, and writes a correct query of their own. There are now four. The pipeline problem was fixed one layer up and reappeared one layer down: a governed warehouse with ad-hoc SQL in every tile is the same silo problem with better plumbing.

**Self-serve, as actually experienced.** The BI tool was bought on the promise that anyone can answer their own questions. What happens is that a PM joins events to accounts without collapsing the subscription history, gets a number about 30% high, puts it in a deck, and nobody notices for five weeks. Self-serve over unmodelled tables doesn't distribute the ability to answer questions. It distributes the ability to be confidently wrong.

**The queue.** Meanwhile the one data person has forty open requests, thirty of which are "can you add a filter to that chart." They have become a human `WHERE` clause.

**The unactionable chart.** A line moves. No annotation marks the release that shipped that morning, no owner is listed, no target is stated, nobody knows what the number was supposed to be. Six people speculate in Slack for forty minutes and the conversation ends without a decision, which is the normal outcome and costs about half a day of collective attention each time.

**The customer-facing one.** Sales sold "usage analytics" as a feature. Engineering points an embedded BI widget at a read replica. A customer opens a two-year date range; the query scans a year of raw events, the replica's load spikes, and the page takes eleven seconds. Tenant isolation is one `WHERE tenant_id = ?` away from a cross-customer data leak, supplied by the frontend.

The common thread is not that people can't get numbers. It is that numbers arrive stripped of the three things that make them usable — a definition, an owner, and an expectation. And of those, the expectation matters most: **a number you had no prior belief about cannot surprise you, and a number that cannot surprise you cannot change anything.** Most of what follows is machinery for delivering the expectation alongside the value.

## What It Is

The **consumption layer** is everything between a modelled table and a decision. It covers three delivery modes that look similar in a vendor demo and are different products:

**Monitoring surfaces** — a small set of curated dashboards and alerts answering known questions, read on a routine. **Exploration** — ad hoc querying to answer a new question, done once, mostly discarded. **Embedded analytics** — the same machinery pointed at customers as a product feature, with all the obligations that implies.

Underneath all three sits the **semantic layer**: metric definitions expressed once, in code, and consumed by everything.

The distinction worth making before anything else is that **self-serve is not one thing**. Self-serve *exploration* is the goal — anyone should be able to ask a question nobody anticipated, without a ticket. Self-serve *definition* is what produces the fourth number — anyone redefining what "active" means inside a tile, permanently, invisibly. The productive arrangement lets everybody write any query they like, and lets nobody re-implement a definition. Teams that treat governance as a dial between "locked down" and "open" end up choosing badly in both directions.

One more separation, because it explains most of the 212 dashboards. A **dashboard** is for monitoring: is anything different from what I expected? A **report** is an argument made once: here is what we found and what we think it means. An **alert** is a claim on someone's attention. Most dashboards are failed reports — an analysis that should have been written up, circulated and archived, frozen instead into a permanent surface with no author and no maintenance.

## Recognizing the Problem

- There are more dashboards than employees.
- The most-used reporting artifact in the company is a spreadsheet someone updates by hand on Mondays.
- Nobody can name the owner of your most-cited chart.
- Three tiles show the same metric with different values.
- The data team's backlog is mostly filter and date-range changes.
- Charts carry no annotations, so every movement triggers an investigation.
- Nobody can say what the number was supposed to be.
- A dashboard's SQL was copy-pasted from another dashboard, including a bug.
- You can't tell which dashboards are safe to delete, so none are deleted.
- Numbers reach executives as screenshots with no definition attached.
- Customer-facing charts query the production database.
- The same metric appears in the app, the BI tool and a vendor's UI with three values.
- Scheduled refreshes are a visible line on the warehouse bill and most of them feed dashboards nobody opens.

## How It Works

### Build the surface around a decision, not a topic

The test for every tile: **what would you do differently if this moved?** If there is no answer, it isn't a monitoring surface, it's decoration. Applied honestly, this deletes most of a typical dashboard.

```
BAD                                  GOOD
┌────┬────┬────┬────┐                ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
│ 12 │ 47 │ 3% │ 88 │                │ weekly active teams      1,284   │
│ ▁▂▃│ ▇▆▅│ ▃▃▃│ ▁▁▇│                │ ▁▂▃▄▅▆▇   target 1,200 · +4.1%   │
├────┼────┼────┼────┤                │        ↑ rel 4.2      ↑ outage   │
│ 91 │ 2.4│ 17 │ 55 │                ├─────────┬──────────┬─────────────┤
│ ▃▄▅│ ▁▁▂│ ▇▇▆│ ▂▃▄│                │new teams│ retained │ per team    │
└────┴────┴────┴────┘                │   412   │   78%    │   3.1       │
 eight numbers, no order,            └─────────┴──────────┴─────────────┘
 no expectation, no owner            owner: growth · fresh 09:00 · def ↗
```

The right-hand shape has four properties the left one lacks, and each is load-bearing.

**One primary tile with its expectation.** The target, the band, or the comparison — something that makes a value readable as good or bad without a second person present.

**Inputs beneath it in tree order** (16.2), so a movement at the top can be attributed downward without leaving the page. This is what turns a dashboard from a status display into a diagnostic.

**Annotations on the time axis** — releases, incidents, campaigns, pricing changes. This is the highest-value and most-skipped feature in the whole layer. Without it, every movement costs a forty-minute investigation. With it, most movements explain themselves at a glance, and the ones that don't are genuinely interesting. Wire it from the deploy pipeline and the incident tool rather than asking humans to remember, because the people who would have to remember get nothing from doing it.

**Freshness and ownership, displayed.** A stale dashboard that looks live is worse than no dashboard, because it converts an absence of information into wrong information.

### Three audiences, three artifacts

**Executives** want one number and whether it is acceptable. The effective delivery is a written summary containing the number, not a link to a dashboard. Organisations rediscover this repeatedly: senior people do not open dashboards, and building a beautiful one for them is a recurring and expensive act of hope.

**Teams** want their inputs and need to attribute movement. The effective delivery is a dashboard they own and review in a standing meeting, where "we don't know why that moved" is a sentence someone has to say out loud.

**Analysts and engineers** want SQL and the modelled tables. The effective delivery is warehouse access and good models, not a BI abstraction that they will spend their time working around.

One artifact for all three serves none of them, and the attempt is where most of the 212 came from.

### Push beats pull, consistently

A dashboard is a pull surface: it requires someone to remember to look, at the right time, for the right reason. Nearly everything that changes behaviour is push.

A weekly digest posted to a channel — with the numbers *and* a sentence of human interpretation, because numbers without interpretation get scrolled past. Alerts on the small number of metrics with real thresholds. And best of all, the number placed where the decision happens: account health on the account record in the CRM, feature usage on the ticket, error rate in the pull request.

**The closer a number is delivered to the moment of the decision, the more likely it is to change it.** A retention figure in a Monday digest shapes a roadmap conversation. The same figure on a dashboard changes nothing, because the person deciding is in a different tab and was never going to open it.

### The semantic layer is what stops the fourth definition

Define `active_account`, `activation`, `mrr` once — in version-controlled code, with tests — and have every consumer read that definition: the BI tiles, the digest job, the in-product number, the reverse-ETL sync from 16.4.

What this actually accomplishes is converting a social problem into a technical one. "Please everyone use the same definition" is a request that decays. "There is one implementation" is a property of the system. It also makes changing a definition a reviewable event with a diff and a blame history, which is the only way the 16.2 redefinition failure gets caught in advance rather than in retrospect.

The layer governs definitions, not queries. Anyone may write any query over the modelled tables; nobody redefines "active" inside a tile. That is the same split as the self-serve one above, made mechanical.

### Dashboards need a lifecycle

The 212 exist because creating one costs nothing and deleting one feels risky. Four rules, all cheap:

- **An owner is required at creation.** No owner, no publish.
- **Usage telemetry, which most BI tools already collect.** Unopened for ninety days, auto-archive.
- **Archive, never delete.** Restoring is cheap, and the fear of losing something irreplaceable is precisely what produced the graveyard.
- **Two tiers of certification.** A small "trusted" set and everything else, visibly marked. Five tiers is a taxonomy nobody will maintain.

### Embedded analytics is a product feature, with product obligations

When customers see the charts, five things stop being internal conveniences.

**Never query the transactional database.** A customer with a date-range picker is an unauthenticated load generator aimed at the store your product depends on.

**Tenant isolation must be structural, not a predicate.** Row-level security, per-tenant partitions, or a query builder that cannot express a cross-tenant query. Isolation that depends on every developer remembering a `WHERE` clause fails on the endpoint written in a hurry at the end of a sprint (Module 5).

**Pre-aggregate.** Customers pick arbitrary ranges. A page load that scans a year of raw events is both a latency problem and a bill, and it scales with your customers' curiosity rather than your revenue.

**Freshness becomes a promise.** "Updated hourly" is a specification you now have to meet, and customers do notice. State it in the UI, and monitor it like any other SLI (16.3).

**Their reporting becomes your contract.** Once a customer has built their own board reporting on your "active users" figure, changing that definition is a breaking API change with a migration path and a notice period. This is the non-obvious cost, and it arrives long after the feature ships: embedding a metric publishes it, and published metrics cannot be quietly improved.

### What the layer costs to run

BI tools issue expensive queries on refresh schedules, for dashboards nobody opens — which means warehouse spend scales with dashboards that *exist* rather than dashboards that *matter*, and the lifecycle rules above are a cost control as much as a hygiene one.

The performance answer is usually unglamorous: pre-compute the handful of things read often, leave exploration live, and build a few wide, pre-joined models rather than expecting people to join five tables correctly. Most wrong numbers in self-serve BI are wrong joins, and a wide table removes the opportunity.

## When It's Appropriate

- **The moment two people need the same number on a routine.** One curated surface plus a digest, and nothing more.
- **When a metric drives a recurring decision with a known threshold** — that is an alert, not a dashboard, and the distinction saves a lot of unread charts.
- **When the data queue is mostly repeat questions with different filters.** That is the signal for modelled tables and self-serve exploration; the queue is telling you which shapes to model.
- **After definitions have drifted twice.** The second occurrence is when a semantic layer pays for itself; the first is bad luck.
- **When customers ask to see their own usage** — and are willing to pay, or it closes deals. Then build it properly, once.
- **Before a recurring meeting exists to review numbers.** The surface should be designed for the meeting, not inherited by it.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A BI tool for five people who can all write SQL.** A scheduled query posting to a channel beats it on every axis including adoption.
- **A dashboard for a question asked once.** That is a report. Write it, circulate it, archive it, and let it be finished.
- **Self-serve BI on unmodelled tables.** You have distributed the ability to be wrong, at speed, with a chart attached.
- **Real-time dashboards for weekly decisions**, which is most decisions. The refresh rate should match the decision cadence, not the vendor's demo.
- **Embedded analytics before a customer has asked for it.** It is a permanent product surface with a permanent definitional contract, sold on a guess.
- **A certification programme at twelve dashboards.** Governance overhead should trail the problem, not precede it.
- **Building your own charting UI for internal use.** This is the undifferentiated-plumbing rule from 16.4, applied one layer up.

## Trade-offs

- **Every dashboard is a maintenance obligation with no failing test.** A model change breaks it silently, and the failure looks like a flat line.
- **Curation costs the speed that made self-serve attractive.** Governing definitions while leaving queries open is a compromise that mostly works, not a resolution — someone will still want a definition changed today and be told to open a PR.
- **A semantic layer is an abstraction people can route around**, and they will if it is slower than writing SQL. Its adoption depends on being genuinely convenient, which is an ongoing investment rather than a launch.
- **Push surfaces compete for attention with everything else in the channel.** A digest nobody reads is the same failure as a dashboard nobody opens, one layer up and harder to audit.
- **Scheduled refreshes spend money continuously**, in proportion to artifacts rather than to value.
- **Embedded analytics converts an internal definition into a public API**, coupling your modelling decisions to customer contracts and notice periods.
- **Making numbers easy to obtain makes them easy to misuse.** The screenshot travels; the definition, the window and the caveat do not.
- **Annotations require effort from people who receive none of the benefit** — the deployer, the incident responder. Automate it or it will not happen, and a dashboard without annotations quietly costs a recurring investigation forever.

## Alternatives

**A written weekly memo.** Numbers with interpretation, authored by a person, archived and searchable. Slower, doesn't answer arbitrary questions, and reliably better at changing decisions than any dashboard — because someone had to think about what the numbers meant before circulating them, which is the step dashboards remove.

**Notebooks.** Better for analysis-as-argument than any BI tool: version-controlled, reproducible, and they show the working. A poor monitoring surface, because nothing re-runs and nobody watches.

**Spreadsheets.** The most successful analytics tool ever built, and worth saying without embarrassment. Excellent as the last mile when numbers arrive through a reproducible pipeline. Catastrophic as the pipeline, which is where organisations actually put them.

**Alerts and nothing else.** For a mature set of metrics with known thresholds, this is a coherent position: no surfaces, no refresh bill, and attention spent only when something is different. It fails the exploratory case entirely.

**Internal surfaces inside your own admin tools** (Module 26). Putting account health on the account page means CS sees it while doing the thing it informs, which beats any dashboard they would have to remember to open.

**Buy the embedded case.** Customer-facing analytics vendors exist precisely because the rollup, isolation and multi-tenancy work is deep and generic. The 16.4 rule applies: build the definitions, rent the rendering.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — someone runs a query and pastes the number.** Entirely fine, and correct for a long time.

**L1 — one dashboard for the primary metric, with its expectation stated, plus a scheduled digest.** *Signal to start:* the same question asked twice.

**L2 — annotations wired automatically from deploys and incidents.** *Signal:* the second forty-minute investigation of a chart movement that turned out to be a release.

**L3 — wide modelled tables for open exploration.** Anyone queries, nobody redefines. *Signal:* a queue full of filter requests, or the appearance of a fourth definition.

**L4 — semantic layer, ownership, two certification tiers, archive-on-disuse.** *Signal:* the dashboard count passes something like a hundred, or an executive quotes a number that no longer means what they think it means.

**L5 — customer-facing analytics on pre-aggregated, structurally isolated rollups**, with freshness stated in the UI and the metric definition versioned as the API contract it now is. *Signal:* it's on the roadmap, and sales has already sold it.

## Implementation

SQL and Postgres, because the dangerous part of this article is the customer-facing path and its two hard requirements — pre-aggregation and isolation — are both schema-level.

```sql
-- Recomputed hourly from the modelled event tables. Customer dashboards
-- pick arbitrary date ranges; scanning a year of raw events on every page
-- load is a latency problem, a cost problem, and a denial-of-service
-- surface operated by your own customers.
create table usage_daily (
  tenant_id  uuid   not null,
  day        date   not null,
  metric     text   not null,
  dimension  text   not null default '',   -- '' = the total for that metric
  value      bigint not null,
  primary key (tenant_id, day, metric, dimension)
);

-- Surfaced in the UI as "updated at HH:MM". Once you display a chart to a
-- customer, freshness is a promise, and a promise you don't monitor is one
-- they will notice breaking before you do.
create table usage_rollup_state (
  metric       text primary key,
  computed_at  timestamptz not null
);
```

```sql
alter table usage_daily enable row level security;

create policy tenant_isolation on usage_daily
  using (tenant_id = current_setting('app.tenant_id')::uuid);
```

Two things about that policy carry the whole design.

The setting is established by the server from the authenticated session, once per connection checkout, and **never from a request parameter** — a tenant id that arrives in the request body is an authorization decision handed to the caller.

And the application must connect as a role that does not own the table. Table owners and superusers bypass row-level security by default, so a policy on a table the app owns is a policy that does nothing, tests green, and is discovered during an incident.

The endpoint's query then contains no isolation logic at all:

```sql
select day, sum(value) as value
from usage_daily
where metric = $1
  and day between $2 and $3
group by day
order by day;
-- No tenant predicate anywhere in application code, deliberately. Isolation
-- that depends on every developer remembering a WHERE clause fails on the
-- endpoint someone writes in a hurry, and that endpoint is the breach.
```

**No language sibling is proposed.** There is nothing here a second language reveals; the isolation mechanism is a property of the database rather than of the code around it. The companion that would add real value is an article on **multi-tenant analytics at warehouse scale** — row access policies in a column store, per-tenant query cost attribution, and how to stop one customer's date-range picker from consuming your compute budget. That belongs alongside Module 5's isolation material, it is a different problem from the Postgres case above, and the map has no home for it.

## Further Reading

- **Few, _Information Dashboard Design_.** The canonical treatment, and the source of the constraint most dashboards violate: it fits on one screen and exists for monitoring, not analysis.
- **Tufte, _The Visual Display of Quantitative Information_.** Data-ink, small multiples, and sparklines — which are the reason the "good" sketch above can fit a trend into a single line.
- **Bryar & Carr, _Working Backwards_.** The six-page narrative and the prohibition on slides. The strongest available case for the memo over the dashboard, and it is an argument about decision quality rather than aesthetics.
- **dbt's MetricFlow / semantic layer documentation and Cube's docs.** Two different implementations of define-once, worth comparing because they disagree about where the boundary between metric and query belongs.
- **Looker's LookML.** The earliest widely adopted semantic layer; worth understanding the model even if you never buy the product, because most later designs are reacting to it.
- **Wickham, "Tidy Data."** Why wide, well-shaped tables make self-serve analysis work and narrow normalised ones don't.
- **PostgreSQL's row-level security documentation**, particularly the notes on `BYPASSRLS`, table ownership, and `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` — the part that turns a policy from decorative into enforced.
- Module 5 for tenant isolation generally, Module 13 for caching the rollups, Module 26 for internal admin surfaces, and Module 34 for what exporting customer data obliges you to.

---

The habit worth carrying past analytics: **ship the expectation with the number.** A value alone is inert — it is only ever "good" or "bad" relative to a belief someone held beforehand, and the belief is the part that gets dropped when a figure moves from the person who computed it to the person who reads it. The target, the band, the last period, the annotation explaining the step change: those are not decoration around the metric, they are the thing that makes it capable of surprising anyone. A dashboard that displays only what is true is a very expensive way to be uninformative.
