# Noisy Neighbours

_Module 5.2b — Multi-Tenancy Architecture_

## The Problem

09:14 on a Tuesday. p99 latency across the entire product goes from 180ms to 4.2 seconds. Error rate climbs. There was no deploy, no configuration change, no traffic increase in aggregate — request volume is up 6%, which is nothing.

CPU across the application fleet is at 41%. Memory is fine. Disk is fine. The database's CPU is at 30%. Every capacity dashboard you own says the system is comfortable, and the system is not comfortable.

Here's what happened. Contoso connected your API to their internal system last night and set the sync loop to run without a concurrency cap. Each sync request runs a report query that takes about six seconds. They have eighteen in flight at any moment.

```
  connection pool: 20 connections, shared by everyone

  normal request:     holds a connection for  8 ms
  Contoso's report:   holds a connection for  6 s          (750× longer)

  throughput ≈ connections / holding time

  before:  20 / 0.008  =  2,500 req/s of headroom
  during:   2 / 0.008  =    250 req/s          ← 18 connections are gone
```

Ninety percent of your capacity is being held by one customer, and the resource that ran out is one nobody graphs. It isn't CPU, memory, disk, or bandwidth. It's *concurrency slots* — a scarce resource that exists only as a number in a configuration file.

Two things about this are worth sitting with.

**The system was never over capacity in aggregate.** Everything you monitor was healthy throughout. This is the normal shape of multi-tenant contention: the binding constraint is almost never the resource with a graph on the wall.

**Contention is regressive.** Contoso experienced almost none of the degradation, because their requests were already in flight holding the connections. The tenants who suffered were the ones sending short, cheap, well-behaved requests — they queued. The polite are punished and the greedy are insulated, automatically, by default, with no malice anywhere in the system.

### The over-correction

So you add a rate limit: 100 requests per minute per tenant, applied uniformly. It ships on Thursday.

On Friday your largest customer — $400k a year, three thousand seats — hits it during a nightly sync that has run without incident for two years. P1 ticket. You raise their limit. On Monday the second-largest hits it. By the end of the month you have a spreadsheet of per-tenant exceptions maintained by whoever is on call, no policy anyone can state, and a limit that is simultaneously too tight for legitimate large customers and too loose to have prevented Tuesday — because eighteen concurrent six-second requests is about 0.18 requests per second, which was never close to the limit.

### And the question from the other direction

At the end of the quarter, finance asks what gross margin looks like per plan. Your cloud bill is one number. The pooled architecture that drove the marginal cost of a tenant to approximately zero also made the cost of a tenant unknowable, and it turns out nobody can say whether the free tier is a customer acquisition channel or a subsidy.

Two questions, asked by two different departments:

- **Can one tenant degrade another?**
- **What does each tenant cost to serve?**

They're separate problems with separate controls, and they rest on the same foundation: per-tenant resource accounting. You can't control what you can't attribute, and you can't price what you can't attribute either.

## What It Is

**Performance isolation** is the property that one tenant's resource consumption cannot materially degrade another tenant's experience.

Note that it's a spectrum rather than a boolean, and that the perfect version of it is a dedicated deployment — which you already declined in 5.1 for cost reasons. Everything in this article is a way of buying most of that property back without buying the hardware.

**Cost allocation** (or tenant cost attribution) is knowing what it costs to serve each tenant, well enough to make decisions about pricing, plan design, and which customers are worth the trouble.

A distinction worth fixing early, because it's the reason the rate limit above failed:

**A limit is a ceiling. Fair sharing is an allocation.** A rate limit is a fixed number that applies whether the system is idle or on fire — which is exactly why it's wrong at both ends. At 3am, when there's spare capacity going to waste, it refuses work for no reason. At 09:14 on Tuesday, it permits a tenant to take everything as long as they do it slowly enough. Fair sharing sets no fixed ceiling and instead guarantees that *when there is contention*, no tenant gets more than its share.

Three related things that get called the same thing:

- A **rate limit** is protective and product-visible: 1,000 requests per minute, published, enforced, a line in your API docs (Module 18 covers the mechanics).
- A **quota** is a plan allowance over a longer window: 50,000 API calls a month, 100GB of storage. It's a billing construct that happens to be enforced technically.
- **Fair sharing** is a scheduling policy under contention. It's invisible when there's capacity and decisive when there isn't.

Most teams deploy the first, call it the third, and are surprised when it behaves like the first.

And one separation from the previous article: **performance isolation is not data isolation.** The failure mode there was a breach; here it's an outage. Row-level security does nothing whatsoever for a saturated connection pool, and a fair scheduler will happily schedule a query that reads the wrong tenant's rows. Different problems, different mechanisms, same word.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Latency spiked with no deploy, no aggregate traffic increase, and no resource graph above 50%.
- An incident review concluded "customer X ran a large import" and the action item was "ask them not to."
- You maintain a hand-edited list of per-tenant rate limit exceptions.
- Your dashboards are aggregates. You cannot slice p99 by tenant, and during an incident you find the culprit by guessing and then checking.
- Job queues are FIFO, and one tenant's fifty thousand enqueued jobs delay everyone's password-reset emails.
- A support request to "run this export for customer Y" gets scheduled for the evening out of fear.
- Your connection-pool saturation alert fires and nothing in it identifies a tenant.
- One tenant holds a large fraction of your rows, storage, or query time, and you discovered that by accident.
- You can't state gross margin on your smallest paid plan.
- Free-tier tenants generate most of your background job volume.
- Someone asks whether a particular customer is worth keeping and the conversation is entirely qualitative.
- Your bulk endpoints and your interactive endpoints share a connection pool, a worker pool, and a queue.

## How It Works

### Step one: find the resource that's actually scarce

The instinct is CPU, and it's almost never CPU. The exhaustible resources in a multi-tenant system, roughly in order of how often they're the culprit:

- **Connection pool slots.** The opening scenario. Small integer, shared, rarely graphed per tenant.
- **Application concurrency** — thread pool slots, worker processes, event-loop time.
- **Job queue workers**, and the queue's ordering policy.
- **Database internals**: locks, buffer cache, temp space for sorts, autovacuum falling behind, replication lag, WAL volume.
- **Cache memory.** One tenant's working set evicting everyone else's is a slow, invisible version of the same problem: your hit rate drops, your database load rises, and nothing anywhere says "tenant."
- **Your quota with someone else.** Your SES sending rate, your Stripe API limit, your third-party enrichment vendor's ceiling — all shared across tenants, all capable of being consumed entirely by one.
- **Search cluster shards**, network egress, and disk IOPS.

The practical move is to instrument the small-integer resources first, because those are the ones that saturate abruptly and invisibly.

### Step two: make consumption attributable

Nothing in this article works without this, and it's the same instrumentation cost allocation needs.

The tenant context from 5.2a already rides along with every request and job. Two additions make it useful:

**Tag queries so the database can be sliced by tenant.** Setting `application_name` per connection, or prefixing statements with a structured comment, means `pg_stat_activity` shows you who is holding what right now, and your slow query log becomes attributable after the fact.

```sql
/* tenant=8842,route=reports.export */ SELECT …
```

Check one thing before adopting this: tools that key on statement text — statement caches, some poolers, some monitoring products — may treat commented statements as distinct, which can defeat deduplication or plan reuse. `pg_stat_statements` normalizes them away, which is convenient for aggregate analysis and means the comment is most useful in `pg_stat_activity` and in logs.

**Roll up per-tenant counters in the application.** Requests, database time in milliseconds, job seconds, bytes stored, bytes egressed — accumulated in memory, flushed per minute to a table or a warehouse. This is deliberately not your metrics system: `tenant_id` as a metric label with fifty thousand values will break your metrics backend and your invoice. High cardinality goes to logs, traces, and a rollup table; metrics get a top-N tenant list and aggregates.

### Step three: control it

Ordered roughly by cost to build.

**Timeouts.** `statement_timeout`, `idle_in_transaction_session_timeout`, and a request deadline. This is hours of work and it converts "unbounded damage" into "bounded damage." Do it before anything else in this article.

**Bulkheads — separate pools per workload class.** Interactive requests, bulk operations, webhook delivery, and admin tooling get their own connection pools and worker pools. The opening incident is impossible in this shape: the report traffic can exhaust the bulk pool completely and the interactive pool never notices. This is often the highest ratio of protection to effort available, and it doesn't require per-tenant anything.

```
        before                          after
   ┌──────────────────┐        ┌──────────┐  ┌──────────┐
   │  one pool: 20    │        │ interact.│  │  bulk    │
   │  interactive +   │        │   16     │  │    4     │
   │  bulk + webhooks │        └──────────┘  └──────────┘
   └──────────────────┘         saturating the right-hand
    one slow class                pool degrades only the
    starves everything            slow class
```

**Per-tenant concurrency limits — the control that matches the failure.** Cap the number of *in-flight* requests per tenant rather than the rate. The reason this is better is one line of queueing arithmetic:

```
  concurrency = rate × latency
```

A rate limit holds the first term fixed and lets the product grow without bound as latency rises — which is precisely when you need it not to. A concurrency cap holds the product fixed, so a tenant whose requests get slower automatically gets fewer of them admitted. It self-tightens exactly at the moment of danger, with no tuning. Eighteen concurrent six-second reports would have been four, and Tuesday would have been a slow morning for one customer instead of an incident for everyone.

This is the single most valuable control here and the least commonly deployed, mostly because "requests per second" is the number everyone thinks in.

**Fair queueing for background work.** One FIFO queue means fifty thousand bulk jobs from one tenant sit ahead of everyone else's password resets. Per-tenant queues drained round-robin — or weighted round-robin, or deficit round robin if job costs vary a lot — bound the damage to a scheduling delay proportional to the number of *active* tenants rather than to the size of the largest backlog.

```
  FIFO                           ROUND ROBIN OVER TENANTS
  [A A A A A A A A A B C]        A: [A A A A A A A A A]
                                 B: [B]
   B waits behind 9 A jobs       C: [C]
                                 → A B C A B? C? A A A …
                                   B and C wait one turn
```

**Shuffle sharding, when you have many tenants and a pool of workers.** Instead of giving every tenant access to all workers, or dedicating workers per tenant, assign each tenant a small random subset. With eight workers and two per tenant, there are 28 possible assignments; a tenant behaving badly fully affects only the tenants who drew the same pair, and partially affects a minority of the rest.

```
  8 workers, each tenant gets 2  →  C(8,2) = 28 distinct shards

  tenant A → {3, 7}      A saturating both hurts:
  tenant B → {1, 4}        - fully:    tenants also on {3,7}
  tenant C → {3, 5}        - halfway:  tenants on 3 or 7 (C, …)
  tenant D → {2, 6}        - not at all: everyone else (B, D, …)
```

The appeal is that isolation improves combinatorially while the resource count stays flat — you're not reserving capacity, just restricting reach. It works best when there are many more tenants than workers and when a tenant's traffic can be routed deterministically.

**Admission control that sheds the right tenant.** Under real overload, something must be refused. The default is "whoever is unlucky." A better policy refuses the tenant furthest over its fair share first, and refuses low-criticality traffic before high-criticality traffic. This is the point where a scheduler becomes a business decision, which is the next paragraph.

**Physical separation.** Move the tenant out to their own database, or their own stack (5.1, level 3). This is the answer when a tenant's *legitimate steady-state* load simply doesn't fit alongside everyone else's — not when they're noisy because of a bug in your code.

### The part that isn't technical

"Fair" is a policy choice, and equal shares is only one option. Weighting by plan tier is entirely defensible — the enterprise customer paying 400 times as much arguably should get more than an equal slice of contended capacity — and is also a decision about who bears degradation, made by an engineer, in a config file, usually without anyone outside the team noticing. It's worth writing down and showing to whoever owns the customer relationships, because they will otherwise discover it during an incident.

### Step four: allocate cost

Some costs are directly attributable: a dedicated database, a dedicated stack, per-tenant storage, per-tenant egress. Tag them and you're done.

The shared bill needs a **consumption proxy**. Pick three to five measurable drivers, weight them, and allocate the shared cost proportionally:

```
  tenant units = (db_time_ms × w₁)
               + (storage_gb_month × w₂)
               + (egress_gb × w₃)
               + (job_seconds × w₄)
               + (requests × w₅)

  tenant cost ≈ shared_infra_cost × (tenant units / total units)
              + directly attributed costs
```

Database time is usually the best single proxy for compute in a SaaS backend, and you already have it from step two. Storage is the one that surprises people: it's cheap per gigabyte, which is exactly why nobody watches it, and the tenant with four terabytes of uploaded documents and eleven seats is frequently the least profitable account in the book.

**Precision is not the goal**, and pretending otherwise is how this work gets discredited. The decisions this feeds — is the free tier sustainable, which plan has inverted margins, is this one customer's contract actually underwater, is this query worth a week of optimization — need a correct *ordering* and an order of magnitude. They do not need accounting-grade numbers, and a model that claims them will be attacked on its assumptions rather than acted on.

The loop closes when the numbers feed back: into pricing dimensions that match what actually costs money, into plan limits that correspond to real constraints, and into engineering priorities — "this one query is $8,000 a month" is a far better ticket than "this query is slow."

## When It's Appropriate

- **Timeouts everywhere: immediately, at any size.** A `statement_timeout` of 30 seconds on the interactive role is an afternoon's work and it bounds every scenario in this article.
- **Bulkheads: as soon as you have two workload classes** — which is as soon as you have exports, reports, webhook delivery, or an admin console alongside interactive traffic. Doesn't need per-tenant instrumentation to be worth doing.
- **Per-tenant concurrency limits: once any single tenant is capable of occupying a meaningful fraction of in-flight slots.** With a pool of 20 and an API any customer can script against, that's day one of having a public API.
- **Per-tenant job queues: as soon as a tenant can enqueue in bulk** — imports, exports, bulk notifications, anything with a "select all" in front of it.
- **Fair scheduling with plan weights: when your tenant size distribution is heavy-tailed**, which it is. A hundred tenants where the top one is two hundred times the median is the normal shape, not an anomaly.
- **Shuffle sharding: many tenants, a pool of interchangeable workers, and routing you control.** Webhook delivery, per-tenant background processing, and API gateway assignment are the natural fits.
- **Cost allocation: before any pricing change, when you have a free tier, or when you can't answer a margin question.** Also when a single customer's usage looks like it might be underwater — the model exists to make that conversation quantitative.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

**Ten tenants of comparable size on a system running at 5% utilization.** Set the timeouts, add tenant tags to your logs, and spend the time elsewhere. Fair scheduling for a queue that is always empty is a control loop with nothing to control.

**Building a scheduler before you have per-tenant measurements.** You'll be tuning weights against intuition, and the first incident will reveal that the scarce resource was one you didn't partition. Attribution first, always.

**Rate limits as a substitute for fixing a slow query.** If p99 is six seconds because a report scans a table without an index, throttling the tenant who runs it hides a defect that will resurface as soon as anyone else runs it. Limits bound the damage from load you can't control; they're the wrong tool for load you caused.

**Dedicated infrastructure for a tenant who is noisy because of your bug.** Moving them out makes the symptom someone else's problem — specifically, yours, twice, on two sets of hardware.

**Per-tenant cost accounting to the cent.** The decisions have wide tolerances. A model accurate to ±30% delivered this quarter beats one accurate to ±5% delivered next year, and the second one won't be more accurate anyway once you've argued about how to allocate the NAT gateway.

**Reserving capacity per tenant.** Static partitioning guarantees isolation and guarantees waste: every reserved slice that's idle is capacity you bought and can't use. Fair sharing exists precisely to get the isolation property without paying for it in utilization, and reaching for reservations first is how multi-tenant economics get thrown away.

## Trade-offs

- **Every published limit is a contract and a support queue.** Once a number is in your documentation, changing it downward is a breaking change, and the first exception you grant is the first row of a spreadsheet you will maintain forever. Exceptions belong in the tenant record as a plan attribute, not in a config file.
- **Fair queueing costs latency when there's no contention** — scheduling overhead, per-tenant queue polling, and a scheduler whose cost grows with the number of *active* tenants. With tens of thousands of mostly-idle tenants, iterating queues naively is itself the bottleneck.
- **Concurrency limits can starve a tenant with itself.** A request that holds a slot and then makes an internal call needing another slot from the same limiter will deadlock under load. Fan-out workflows, synchronous internal calls, and recursive processing all hit this. Separate limiters per tier, or admit internal calls from a different bucket.
- **Debugging gains a new layer.** "Why was this job delayed?" now has an answer involving a scheduler, a weight, and other tenants' behaviour. Emit the reason — queue depth, position, share used — or you've traded one invisible failure for another.
- **Attribution has a running cost**: rollup tables, retention decisions, tagging overhead on every query, and the discipline to keep high-cardinality data out of your metrics system.
- **Cost numbers get weaponized.** An allocation model with honest error bars becomes, in a slide deck, a precise claim that a customer is unprofitable. State confidence alongside the number, and expect to restate it.
- **Whoever you protect, you're choosing who gets degraded.** Plan-weighted fairness means the free tier absorbs the pain first. That's probably correct and it should be an explicit decision with a name on it, not an emergent property of a config value.
- **None of this helps if the tenant's legitimate load genuinely doesn't fit.** At that point the controls are just a way of delivering a bad experience fairly, and the answer is 5.1's level 3.

## Alternatives

- **Overprovision.** Underrated, and correct more often than engineers like. Doubling the connection pool and adding a database replica is cheaper than a scheduler, ships this week, and requires no ongoing tuning. It stops being the answer when the tail tenant grows faster than the budget.
- **Move the expensive work off the request path.** Much of what looks like a contention problem is a synchronous-work problem: exports, reports, and bulk mutations that run inside an HTTP request. Making them jobs doesn't remove the load, it relocates it to a place where queueing is expected and controllable. Frequently this is the real fix, and Module 10 is where it lives.
- **Make the expensive operation cheap.** An index, a pagination limit, a precomputed aggregate, a cursor instead of an offset. Unglamorous, and it deletes the problem rather than managing it.
- **Pricing as a control.** If heavy usage costs money, tenants self-regulate, and the sync loop with no concurrency cap gets fixed by the customer's finance team rather than by your scheduler. Usage-based dimensions turn a technical control problem into an incentive one — which is Module 15, and is a genuine architectural alternative rather than a business aside.
- **Scheduled windows for bulk operations.** Crude, effective, and acceptable to more customers than you'd expect: exports run overnight, imports are queued for off-peak. Buys most of the isolation benefit for a fraction of the work.
- **Autoscaling the shared tier.** Helps with sustained load and not with the opening scenario, because a pool exhausted in ninety seconds is empty long before a new instance is in service. Worth knowing which of your problems is a capacity problem and which is a burst problem — they look identical on a graph and respond to completely different medicine.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — bound the damage.** `statement_timeout`, `idle_in_transaction_session_timeout`, request deadlines, and a maximum page size on every list endpoint. Hours, not weeks.
*Signal you're behind:* a single query can run for six minutes.

**Level 1 — attribute.** Tenant on every request, job, and query; per-tenant p99 and per-tenant database-time rollups; a dashboard that ranks tenants by consumption.
*Signal:* an incident where identifying the tenant took longer than fixing the problem.

**Level 2 — bulkhead by workload class.** Separate pools and workers for interactive, bulk, webhook, and admin traffic.
*Signal:* a report or an export saturating the pool that serves the UI.

**Level 3 — per-tenant concurrency limits and per-tenant job queues.** In-flight caps on the request path; round-robin draining for background work.
*Signal:* any one tenant is capable of holding most of your in-flight slots, or one tenant's backlog has delayed another's time-sensitive job.

**Level 4 — fair scheduling with plan weights, and admission control.** Shed the tenant furthest over share, shed low-criticality traffic first, and put the weights in the tenant record where the business can see them.
*Signal:* you're granting limit exceptions by hand, or the same customer name appears in three consecutive incident reviews.

**Level 5 — the cost model.** Consumption units, allocated shared cost, margin by plan and by tenant, reviewed on a schedule and fed into pricing.
*Signal:* a pricing or plan-design decision you can't make with evidence.

The escape hatch at every level is 5.1's level 3: move the tenant out. Keep it available, and don't reach for it while the controls above are still unbuilt, because a tenant who saturates a shared pool will often saturate a dedicated one too.

## Implementation

Go, because concurrency limiting and fair scheduling are exactly the shape of problem its primitives were built for, and the whole mechanism fits in a few dozen lines.

**Per-tenant concurrency limiting.**

```go
type TenantLimiter struct {
    mu       sync.Mutex
    limit    int64
    sems     map[string]*entry
}

type entry struct {
    sem      *semaphore.Weighted
    lastUsed time.Time
}

func (l *TenantLimiter) Acquire(ctx context.Context, tenantID string) (func(), error) {
    l.mu.Lock()
    e, ok := l.sems[tenantID]
    if !ok {
        e = &entry{sem: semaphore.NewWeighted(l.limit)}
        l.sems[tenantID] = e
    }
    e.lastUsed = time.Now()
    l.mu.Unlock()

    // ctx carries the request deadline: wait for a slot, but not forever
    if err := e.sem.Acquire(ctx, 1); err != nil {
        return nil, ErrTooManyConcurrentRequests   // → 429, with Retry-After
    }
    return func() { e.sem.Release(1) }, nil
}
```

```go
func (l *TenantLimiter) Middleware(next http.Handler) http.Handler {
    return http.HandlerFunc(func(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
        release, err := l.Acquire(r.Context(), TenantFrom(r.Context()))
        if err != nil {
            w.Header().Set("Retry-After", "1")
            http.Error(w, "too many concurrent requests", http.StatusTooManyRequests)
            return
        }
        defer release()
        next.ServeHTTP(w, r)
    })
}
```

> **The map grows forever.** One entry per tenant that has ever sent a request, held for the life of the process. With fifty thousand tenants that's a slow leak and a lock that every request contends on. Sweep entries idle for an hour on a ticker, and shard the map by hash of tenant id if the mutex shows up in a profile.
>
> **`Acquire` must take the request context, not `context.Background()`.** With the request context, a tenant that saturates its own limit sees requests fail fast at their deadline. Without it, they queue indefinitely, each one holding a goroutine, an open socket, and whatever the handler allocated before reaching the middleware — and you've converted a bounded rejection into an unbounded memory problem.
>
> **The fan-out trap.** If a handler holding a slot makes an internal HTTP call that passes through this same middleware with the same tenant, a tenant at its limit deadlocks against itself under load, and it will only happen in production. Internal traffic needs a separate limiter or an exempt bucket.

**Fair scheduling over tenants.**

```go
// Next returns the next job, rotating over tenants with pending work.
func (s *Scheduler) Next(ctx context.Context) (*Job, error) {
    s.mu.Lock()
    defer s.mu.Unlock()

    for range s.active {                 // at most one full rotation
        tenant := s.active[s.cursor]
        s.cursor = (s.cursor + 1) % len(s.active)

        if job := s.queues[tenant].Pop(); job != nil {
            return job, nil
        }
        s.retire(tenant)                 // empty: drop from the active ring
    }
    return nil, ErrNoWork
}
```

The ring holds only tenants with pending work, so the cost of a rotation scales with *active* tenants rather than total tenants — the difference between iterating twelve queues and iterating fifty thousand. Weighting by plan is a small change from here: give each tenant a deficit counter incremented by its weight on each pass, and let it dispatch while the counter covers the cost of the next job. That's deficit round robin, borrowed intact from network scheduling, and it handles jobs of wildly different costs without starving anyone.

**Attribution, on the way to the database.**

```go
func Tagged(ctx context.Context, query string) string {
    return fmt.Sprintf("/* tenant=%s,route=%s */ %s",
        TenantFrom(ctx), RouteFrom(ctx), query)
}
```

Three lines, and during the next incident `pg_stat_activity` answers "who is holding those connections right now" instead of leaving you to guess. Pair it with a deferred timer around every query that accumulates milliseconds per tenant into an in-memory counter, flushed every minute — that counter is simultaneously your contention dashboard and the largest term in your cost model.

## Further Reading

- **Google SRE Book, "Handling Overload" and "Addressing Cascading Failures."** The canonical treatment of per-customer quotas, request criticality, adaptive throttling, and what actually happens to a system as it tips over. The observation that retries amplify overload is worth internalizing before you deploy any of this.
- **Amazon Builders' Library, "Workload isolation using shuffle-sharding."** The clearest explanation of the technique, including the combinatorics and the conditions under which it stops helping.
- **Amazon Builders' Library, "Using load shedding to avoid overload" and "Avoiding insurmountable queue backlogs."** Two short pieces that between them cover admission control and why an unbounded queue is a worse failure than a rejection.
- **Netflix's concurrency-limits library and its accompanying write-up.** Adaptive concurrency limits derived from latency gradients — the limit tunes itself rather than being configured, which is the natural next step past a fixed cap.
- **Shreedhar and Varghese, "Efficient Fair Queuing using Deficit Round Robin" (1995).** The source of the scheduling algorithm above. Short paper, and reading it in its original networking context makes the generalization obvious.
- **PostgreSQL documentation on `statement_timeout`, `idle_in_transaction_session_timeout`, `pg_stat_activity`, and `pg_stat_statements`.** Everything in the attribution section comes from these four.
- **Google's sqlcommenter.** The convention for attaching structured application context to SQL statements, and the compatibility notes on which tools cope with it.
- **The FinOps Foundation's material on showback and chargeback.** The cost half of this article is a well-developed discipline outside engineering, and the vocabulary — allocation, amortization, shared-cost strategies — saves you from inventing it badly.

A **TypeScript/Node** sibling would reveal something this implementation structurally hides. Go's scheduler makes a blocked goroutine cheap, so a concurrency limiter is a semaphore and nothing else. Node has one event loop: a tenant's CPU-bound JSON serialization or synchronous crypto call blocks every other tenant's request in the same process regardless of any semaphore, so isolation has to be enforced at the process or worker-thread boundary rather than within one. The interesting content is how differently the same problem decomposes when concurrency is cooperative — why per-tenant limits protect the pool but not the loop, and why the bulkhead moves from a data structure to a deployment topology. Not yet written.

---

The closing habit is a question to ask during any incident where the graphs look healthy: **which resource is actually scarce, and does anything in this system know who is consuming it?** The answer is usually a small integer nobody has ever graphed — pool slots, worker slots, a third party's rate limit — and the tenant consuming it is the one least affected by its scarcity. Under pressure, someone is going to be degraded. The only real question is whether you chose who, or whether the queue chose for you.
