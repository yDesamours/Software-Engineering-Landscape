# Per-Tenant Configuration

_Module 5.3b — Multi-Tenancy Architecture_

## The Problem

Contoso signs a large contract on the Friday, conditional on two things: invoices carry their logo, and their fiscal year starts in April. On Monday someone writes this:

```java
if (tenant.getName().equals("Contoso")) {
    fiscalYearStart = Month.APRIL;
}
```

Three lines. Ships that afternoon. And it was the correct decision — the alternative was designing a configuration subsystem nobody had specified, on a Monday, in exchange for one customer's fiscal year.

Run it forward eighteen months. Nothing dramatic happens; the same decision is simply made again, correctly, by different people, about thirty more times:

- Four tenants with non-January fiscal years.
- Two with different approval workflows — one requires two approvers, one skips approval below €500.
- One whose SSO provider returns group names where everyone else's returns email addresses.
- One who needs invoice numbers formatted for their ERP.
- Three with custom terminology: "projects" are "matters," "clients" are "accounts."
- One who must never receive the weekly summary email, for reasons lost to history.
- Two with different data retention periods.
- Nine integration tests that assert behaviour for a named customer.

Thirty-one hardcoded tenant names across fourteen files. Every one of them was locally defensible. Collectively they are a fork of the product, living inside `if` statements, invisible to everyone who didn't write them.

The specific failures that shape produces:

**You can't state a tenant's behaviour without reading source.** Support asks why Contoso's invoice came out differently and the answer requires grep, a build tool, and someone who knows the codebase.

**The fifth customer who wants an April fiscal year is a deploy.** A code change, a review, a release, and a risk window — to express a fact the sales team already knew during the call.

**The test matrix is real and untested.** Nobody has ever run the suite with Contoso's fiscal year *and* Fabrikam's approval rule, so nobody knows whether those interact. They probably do.

**Renaming breaks logic.** The comparison is on a display name. A customer rebrands and their invoices silently revert to January.

**Churn leaves residue.** Contoso left a year ago and the branch is still there, because removing it requires being certain, and nobody is certain.

### The over-correction

So the next team builds a configuration system, and makes everything configurable, because flexibility is good.

Two years later there are 340 settings. Roughly 300 have never been changed from their default by any tenant. Each one is a branch that must work in both states. The nominal configuration space is 2³⁴⁰. A bug report arrives that reproduces only under a particular combination of eleven settings, and there is no way to determine whether that combination was ever intended to be supported, because nobody ever decided.

Both failures are the same failure:

```
  hardcoded branches   variation you can't see, and can't change without a deploy
  340 settings         variation you can see, and can't reason about
```

The question was never "configuration or code." It's **which variations are product surface and which are accidents** — and how to stop the second kind quietly becoming the first.

## What It Is

**Per-tenant configuration** is the mechanism by which one codebase behaves differently for different tenants, with the variation expressed as data rather than as control flow.

The useful part isn't the definition, it's the taxonomy. Four things get lumped into one `settings` blob, and they have four different owners, four different lifetimes, and four different correctness requirements:

**Entitlements** — what a tenant is *allowed* to do, derived from their plan. Seat limits, API rate limits, whether SSO is available, audit log retention. Owned by billing and product. Changes when the subscription changes. This is a security boundary: it must be enforced server-side, and getting it wrong means giving away revenue or taking away something a customer paid for.

**Settings** — how a tenant *wants* the product to behave, within what they're entitled to. Fiscal year, timezone, terminology, notification defaults, date format. Owned by the customer's administrator, changed in a UI, and wrong values are annoying rather than dangerous.

**Feature flags** — engineering-owned, temporary, for gradual rollout, experiments, and kill switches. Their defining property is that they should be *deleted* within weeks (Module 30).

**Customization** — structure the customer authors: custom fields, custom statuses, custom roles, branding. Not a switch with two positions; a schema someone else wrote.

The most consequential confusion is **feature flags versus entitlements**, because both are booleans and their lifetimes are opposite. A flag answers "is this code path enabled?" An entitlement answers "has this customer paid for this?" Flags are transient by design; entitlements persist as long as the plan does. Running entitlements through your flagging platform means your commercial model is expressed in a tool built for temporary state, usually evaluated by a client-side SDK with eventual-consistency semantics — which is a poor place to enforce what a customer bought, and an even poorer place to explain it during an audit.

The second confusion is **settings versus entitlements**, and it's the one with a security consequence. A setting a customer can edit must never be able to exceed an entitlement they haven't bought. That sounds obvious and it's violated constantly, because both end up as keys in the same table.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A tenant name or id appears in a conditional.
- A flag named after a customer — `contoso_mode` — exists. That's the `if` statement with extra steps.
- Onboarding a customer with a known, previously-implemented variation still requires a deploy.
- There's no single place that answers "what is this tenant's effective configuration."
- Your settings are a JSON column with no schema, no validation, and no list of valid keys.
- Two settings interact, someone found out in production, and it isn't written down anywhere.
- A plan limit is enforced only in the frontend.
- A feature flag added three years ago is still referenced in code.
- Support cannot change a customer setting without filing an engineering ticket.
- Custom fields are implemented as `custom_1` through `custom_20`.
- Nobody can say which settings are safe to delete.
- Your staging environment's configuration resembles no real tenant.

## How It Works

### Layered resolution

The mechanism is a fixed precedence order, resolved per request.

```
  ┌─ 5  request override      (admin impersonation, debug header) — rare, audited
  ├─ 4  user preference       (only for keys that are per-user)
  ├─ 3  tenant setting        (the customer's admin changed it)
  ├─ 2  environment default   (per cell, per region)
  └─ 1  product default       (in code, versioned with the code)

  effective(key) = the first layer, from the top, that defines it
```

Two properties make this cheap:

**Every key has a default in code.** A missing row is never an error, a new key works immediately for every existing tenant, and a configuration store outage degrades to defaults rather than taking the product down.

**Storage is sparse.** Only overrides are stored. You never write 340 rows per tenant, and "has this tenant customized anything" is answerable by looking.

### Entitlements are constraints, not a layer

Here's the subtlety that most treatments skip, and it's the one that causes revenue leaks.

Entitlements do not sit in the precedence stack. If they did, a tenant setting at a higher layer would override them, which is precisely backwards — the customer's preference must not beat the plan they bought. Entitlements **clamp**:

```
  tenant setting:   retention_days     = 365     (the customer asked for a year)
  plan entitlement: max_retention_days = 90      (they're on Standard)

  effective:        min(365, 90) = 90
```

So resolution has two stages: merge the value layers, then apply the entitlement constraints. And note that the direction of the clamp is a property of the key — `min` for ceilings, `max` for floors, set-intersection for enumerations — which the type system cannot infer for you. It has to be declared.

### The registry

One place where every key is declared: name, type, default, allowed range or enum, which layers may set it, which entitlement clamps it, and who owns it.

This is what makes "what is configurable?" answerable without grep. It's also what gives you validation at write time, an admin UI generated rather than hand-built, and a mechanical answer to "is this key still used."

### Provenance, or: the single most useful tool you can build

An endpoint that shows a tenant's effective configuration **and which layer each value came from**:

```
  fiscal_year_start   APRIL      ← tenant setting (set 2025-03-11 by admin@contoso)
  retention_days      90         ← clamped by entitlement (requested 365)
  approval_threshold  500        ← tenant setting
  date_format         ISO        ← product default
  new_editor          true       ← feature flag (rollout 40%)
```

Most of the support cost of a configuration system is someone asking "why did it do that for this customer," and this view answers it in seconds without anyone opening an IDE. Build it early; it pays for itself before the registry does.

### When configuration isn't enough: the escalation ladder

Three levels, each a substantially bigger commitment than the last. The important thing is to know which one you're on, because teams routinely arrive at level three by accident.

**Level 1 — bounded choices.** Settings you enumerated. The customer picks from options you designed and tested. Cheap, safe, and covers the large majority of real requests.

**Level 2 — customer-authored structure.** Custom fields, custom statuses, custom roles. The customer defines shape, you store and query it. The data-model question is the whole problem:

- `custom_1 … custom_20` columns: awful to work with, indexes beautifully, and that's why it persists in systems built decades ago.
- Entity-attribute-value tables: flexible, and every query becomes a pivot.
- A JSONB column plus a per-tenant schema definition stored alongside: the common modern answer. Flexible, queryable, and the constraint is indexing — a GIN index covers containment queries broadly, and the fields that appear in hot filters usually need expression indexes created deliberately.

Whichever you choose, the consequences propagate: search indexing, exports, reporting, the API's response shape, and every migration now has to cope with structure you didn't design.

**Level 3 — customer-authored behaviour.** Workflow rules, formulas, conditional automations, scripts. You have now built a platform, and you own the semantics, the debugging, the performance, and the security of code you didn't write. Their infinite loop is your incident. Their expensive rule is your p99 (5.2b). Their bug generates a support ticket that says your product is broken, and the correct response — "your rule is wrong" — is one nobody enjoys delivering.

The usual escape from level 3 is to move execution to their side: expose events and an API, let them write the automation in their own infrastructure (Module 17). You give up the seamless in-product experience and you give up owning their runtime, which is generally a good trade until the contract value says otherwise.

### White-labelling, and the part of it that isn't data

Logo, colours, email sender name, terminology — all straightforwardly settings. Two pieces are not:

**Custom domains** require certificate issuance and renewal per tenant, which is a provisioning step with a slow path and an external dependency (5.3a).

**Sending email from the customer's domain** requires DNS records the customer must add, verification you must check, and a state where it's configured but not yet verified.

Both are lifecycle problems wearing configuration clothing, and both are where "just let them white-label it" turns out to be a quarter of work.

### The road out of the `if`

Mechanical, one branch at a time:

1. Name the **behaviour**, not the customer. `fiscal_year_start`, never `contoso_mode`. A setting named after a customer has changed nothing — it's still a fork, now with indirection and worse discoverability.
2. Declare it in the registry with the current behaviour as the default, so the change is a no-op for everyone else.
3. Replace the conditional with a resolution call.
4. Set the value for the tenant that needed it.
5. Delete the name comparison and the test that asserted it.

No new infrastructure is required to start. The registry can be a map in a file for the first dozen keys, and the discipline — behaviours, not customers — is what actually matters.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A typed registry with defaults: once you pass roughly ten settings, or once more than one team is adding them.** Below that, a map and a code review is fine.
- **Layered resolution: as soon as you have both plan-derived and customer-chosen values**, which is as soon as you have a second paid plan.
- **Entitlements as a distinct, server-enforced concept: with the second plan.** Not the second hundred customers — the second plan.
- **Customer-authored custom fields: when three or more customers ask for the same shape of extension**, or when you're losing deals over it. Three is a real threshold; one is a request, two is a coincidence.
- **Customer-authored behaviour: when workflows genuinely differ in ways you cannot enumerate, and contract values support the permanent cost.** Rare, and almost always later than it first seems.
- **White-labelling: when it's a priced tier**, because the custom-domain work alone is worth charging for.
- **Per-setting usage telemetry: from the first day you have a registry.** It's the only mechanism that ever lets you delete a setting.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

**The first `if` for the first customer.** This article opened by defending it and that defence stands: three lines on a Monday, with a ticket and a behaviour-shaped name, beats a subsystem designed under deadline. The failure isn't the first branch. It's the thirty-first, and the absence of anyone tracking the count.

**Making everything configurable for flexibility.** Each setting is a permanent branch, a test dimension, a support question, and a thing you can never remove without evidence. The default should be *not* configurable; the bar for adding a setting is a second customer who needs it differently.

**A rule engine because one customer wants two approvers.** Two settings — `approvers_required` and `approval_threshold` — cover that customer and the next four. A DSL covers all possible customers and costs you a language.

**Per-tenant UI layout customization nobody requested.** Extremely expensive, almost never the reason a deal was won.

**Full query and index support for custom fields that customers only want to display.** Ask what they'll do with the field before deciding whether it needs to be filterable, sortable, and searchable, because those three words are most of the cost.

**A configuration service on the request path** when a table and a cache would do. You've added a network dependency to every request in exchange for an abstraction.

## Trade-offs

- **Settings are permanent and combinatorial.** *n* booleans is 2ⁿ nominal configurations and you will test perhaps three. The mitigation isn't more tests, it's designing for orthogonality — and declining settings that interact with existing ones, which means someone must be able to see the existing ones.
- **Configuration as data means configuration bugs are data bugs.** No compiler, no code review, no deploy pipeline, no gradual rollout. A bad value reaches production instantly, which is the same property that made no-deploy changes attractive. Validation at write time, a typed registry, an audit trail, and the ability to revert a change are what you buy back with.
- **Resolution is on the hot path.** Per-request lookups need caching; caching creates staleness; invalidating across instances is a distributed problem; and a configuration store outage becomes a product outage unless defaults are local. Which is why defaults live in code.
- **Entitlement defaults must fail closed.** A missing row for a setting should default to the sensible value; a missing row for an entitlement must default to the *most restrictive* value. Getting this backwards means an outage in the entitlement store silently upgrades everyone to enterprise.
- **"Works for me" gets harder to resolve.** Reproduction now requires a tenant's configuration, and bug reports without it are unactionable.
- **Customer-authored structure costs you control of your own schema.** Migrations, search indexing, exports, reporting, and API response shapes all have to handle data you didn't design. Query performance is the usual casualty, and it degrades per tenant, which makes it hard to see in aggregate.
- **Customer-authored behaviour means you own their defects.** Sandboxing, CPU and memory limits, timeouts, and a support process for telling customers their logic is wrong. This is a business model change, not a feature.
- **Enforcing entitlements in many places guarantees drift.** One authoritative check, called from everywhere, or you'll find a path where the enterprise feature is free.
- **Deleting a setting is far harder than adding one**, because you can't know who depends on it. Per-setting usage telemetry is the only exit, and nobody adds it until they're already drowning.

## Alternatives

- **Say no.** A product with one behaviour is cheaper to build, test, document, support, and explain. Opinionated products win deals too. The cost of a variation is permanent; the revenue attributed to it usually isn't.
- **Generalize it into the product.** When four customers want the same thing, it isn't customization, it's a missing feature with a bad default. Ship it for everyone and delete the variation.
- **Extension via API and webhooks.** Emit events, expose an API, let customers build the behaviour on their own infrastructure. Execution, debugging, and performance move to their side of the boundary. Usually the right answer whenever level 3 comes up (Module 17).
- **An adapter layer per customer.** A separately deployed component that translates between your product and one customer's requirements, owned by a services team, versioned independently. Common in enterprise software, honest about its costs, and it keeps the core product clean.
- **Per-tenant builds.** An actual fork, deployed separately, for a handful of very large contracts. Occasionally correct. Catastrophic if the handful becomes a dozen, and it always wants to.
- **Buy a flagging or entitlement platform.** Reasonable, with the caveat above: check that what you're buying is built for durable commercial entitlements rather than transient rollout flags, and that enforcement can happen server-side.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — count them.** Grep for tenant names, tenant ids, and plan-tier comparisons. The resulting list is the first draft of your registry and the only honest estimate of your problem's size.
*Signal you're behind:* nobody knows how many there are.

**Level 1 — behaviours, not customers.** Mechanically convert each branch into a named, defaulted setting. No infrastructure needed; a map and a lookup function is enough.
*Signal:* the second customer wants a variation that already exists in code for the first.

**Level 2 — a typed registry.** Key, type, default, allowed values, permitted layers, owner. Validation on write. One place that answers what's configurable.
*Signal:* two settings interacted in production, or nobody can enumerate the keys.

**Level 3 — layered resolution with entitlement clamping**, sparse storage, request-scoped resolution and caching, and the provenance view.
*Signal:* a customer setting exceeded a plan limit, or support is asking engineering what a tenant's configuration is.

**Level 4 — hand it over.** An admin UI, validation, audit history, and support-visible change logs. The measure of success is that engineering stops receiving configuration tickets.
*Signal:* engineers are running config updates as a routine chore.

**Level 5 — customer-authored structure**, and only if the business genuinely requires it, customer-authored behaviour, with sandboxing, resource limits, and an explicit support model.
*Signal:* deals are lost over extensibility, repeatedly, with the same shape of request.

Running alongside all of it: **per-setting usage telemetry, and a deletion review.** Any key that has sat at its default for every tenant for a year is a candidate for removal, and that review is the only thing standing between you and 340 settings.

## Implementation

TypeScript, because a registry whose types flow through to every call site is the whole point, and TypeScript expresses that with very little ceremony.

**The registry declares everything about a key in one place.**

```ts
type Layer = "default" | "environment" | "tenant" | "user";

interface Key<T> {
  default: T;
  layers: Layer[];                      // where it may be set
  validate: (v: unknown) => v is T;
  clampedBy?: { entitlement: string; direction: "max" | "min" };
  owner: string;                        // the team that answers questions about it
}

export const registry = {
  "fiscal_year_start": {
    default: "JANUARY",
    layers: ["tenant"],
    validate: isMonth,
    owner: "billing",
  },
  "retention_days": {
    default: 30,
    layers: ["tenant"],
    validate: isPositiveInt,
    clampedBy: { entitlement: "max_retention_days", direction: "max" },
    owner: "platform",
  },
} as const satisfies Record<string, Key<unknown>>;

export type SettingKey = keyof typeof registry;
```

**Resolution merges the value layers, then applies the constraints.**

```ts
export function resolve<K extends SettingKey>(
  key: K,
  ctx: RequestContext,
): Resolved<ValueOf<K>> {
  const spec = registry[key];

  // value layers, highest precedence first; product default always terminates
  for (const layer of ["user", "tenant", "environment"] as const) {
    const raw = ctx.overrides[layer]?.[key];
    if (raw !== undefined && spec.validate(raw)) {
      return clamp(key, raw, layer, ctx);
    }
  }
  return clamp(key, spec.default, "default", ctx);
}

function clamp<K extends SettingKey>(
  key: K, value: ValueOf<K>, from: Layer, ctx: RequestContext,
): Resolved<ValueOf<K>> {
  const c = registry[key].clampedBy;
  if (!c) return { value, from };

  const limit = ctx.entitlements[c.entitlement];          // fails closed — see below
  const clamped = c.direction === "max"
    ? Math.min(value as number, limit as number)
    : Math.max(value as number, limit as number);

  return clamped !== value
    ? { value: clamped as ValueOf<K>, from: "entitlement", requested: value }
    : { value, from };
}
```

> **The return type carries provenance, not just a value.** `{ value, from, requested }` is what powers the support view above, and threading it through from the start costs nothing. Retrofitting it later means touching every call site.
>
> **Entitlement lookup must fail closed.** If `ctx.entitlements` is empty because the billing service was unreachable, `Math.min(365, undefined)` is `NaN` and the clamp silently disappears. Load entitlements with a restrictive fallback — the free-plan values, not an empty object — so a billing outage degrades customers downward rather than upgrading everyone to enterprise. This is the one place in a configuration system where a missing value must not mean "unconstrained."
>
> **The clamp direction can't be inferred.** `retention_days` clamps with `min` against a maximum; `password_min_length` clamps with `max` against a floor. Both are numbers, both are entitlement-bounded, and the type system has nothing to say about which is which. It's declared in the registry because there's nowhere else it can live.

**Resolving once per request, not once per lookup.**

```ts
const snapshot = await configFor(tenantId);   // cached: key `cfg:${tenantId}:${version}`
```

Cache by tenant *and* a configuration version that increments on every write. Invalidation across instances then needs no coordination at all — a write bumps the version, and the next request for that tenant misses the cache naturally. Note the tenant in the cache key, for the reason in 5.2a: a configuration cache keyed only by setting name is a cross-tenant leak with a particularly bad blast radius.

## Further Reading

- **Pete Hodgson, "Feature Toggles (aka Feature Flags)" on martinfowler.com.** The canonical taxonomy — release, experiment, ops, and permission toggles — with different lifetimes and different homes. Its "permissioning toggles" category is exactly the entitlement confusion described above, and it's the piece to hand to anyone arguing that flags and entitlements are the same thing.
- **OpenFeature (CNCF).** A vendor-neutral flag evaluation API. Useful as a design reference even if you don't adopt it, particularly its separation of evaluation context from flag definition.
- **Martin Fowler, "Dealing with Properties."** An old paper and still the clearest treatment of the fixed-schema versus flexible-properties trade-off that custom fields force on you, including why the obvious answers are worse than they look.
- **PostgreSQL documentation on JSONB, containment operators, and GIN indexing** — including `jsonb_path_ops`. Read it before committing to JSONB for custom fields; the index you need depends on the query shape you'll allow, and that decision is hard to reverse once customers have data.
- **Salesforce's published papers on their metadata-driven architecture.** Cited in 5.1 for tenancy; here for the opposite reason — it's the most complete public example of customer-authored structure taken to its limit, where the schema itself is data and the platform is the product. Worth reading as the terminal state of the escalation ladder, and as an argument for not starting up it.
- **Open Policy Agent and the Rego language.** When entitlements stop being scalar limits and become rules, this is the mature option, and its decision-logging model is a good answer to "why was this denied."
- **Cloudflare's writing on V8 isolates**, or any serious treatment of sandboxing untrusted code. Required reading before agreeing to run customer-authored logic in your process.
- **Your billing platform's entitlements primitive**, if it has one — several now expose plan-derived feature access directly, which removes a synchronization problem between the subscription and your own entitlement store.

A **Go** sibling would reveal something the caching paragraph above skates over. In Go the natural shape isn't a cache with a TTL and an invalidation story — it's an immutable configuration snapshot held in an `atomic.Pointer`, rebuilt on change and swapped in wholesale, so resolution is a plain map read with no locking, no expiry, and no per-request cache lookup at all. That changes the failure modes completely: staleness becomes bounded by publish latency rather than by TTL, readers never block, and the interesting question moves to memory (one snapshot per tenant, resident, for a large tenant count) and to how a partially-updated snapshot is prevented from ever being visible. Publish-and-swap versus cache-and-invalidate is a genuine fork in the design, and only one of them is visible from the TypeScript version. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this one is to ask two questions about every variation before it exists. **Is this a product decision or an accident?** — because product decisions deserve a name, a default, and a place in the registry, and accidents deserve to be refused. And **who owns this value, and how long should it live?** Entitlements belong to billing and last as long as the plan. Settings belong to the customer and last until they change them. Flags belong to engineering and should be dead within a month. Customization belongs to the customer and outlives everything, including the people who agreed to it.

Four owners, four lifetimes. Putting them all in one JSON column is why configuration systems rot.
