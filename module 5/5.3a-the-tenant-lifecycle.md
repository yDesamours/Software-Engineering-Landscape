# The Tenant Lifecycle

_Module 5.3a — Multi-Tenancy Architecture_

## The Problem

`create_tenant.sh` began as one `INSERT`. Eighteen months later it does eleven things:

```
   1  insert tenant row                    ✓ committed
   2  create owner user, hash password     ✓ committed
   3  create Stripe customer + subscription ✓ external — no rollback
   4  create object-storage prefix + policy ✗ timeout
   5  create search index alias             — never ran
   6  seed roles, permissions, templates    — never ran
   7  register subdomain                    — never ran
   8  add row to warehouse dimension table  — never ran
   9  provision dedicated DB (enterprise)   — never ran
  10  send welcome email                    — never ran
  11  notify the CS channel                 — never ran
```

Step 4 timed out. Consider the state of the world now.

The tenant row exists, so the customer can log in. Stripe has an active subscription, so they are being billed — today, and every month. There's no storage prefix, so every upload returns a 500. The welcome email never went out, so they don't have a password and haven't tried to log in yet, which is the only reason nobody has noticed.

The operator on call does the natural thing and re-runs the script. Step 3 executes again. The customer now has two subscriptions.

This is not a scripting failure. **Tenant provisioning is a distributed transaction across systems that have no shared coordinator** — your database, a payment processor, an object store, a search cluster, a DNS provider, an email service — and it is almost always first written as a sequence of imperative steps by whoever needed one on a Tuesday.

### The other end of the same problem

Fourteen months later that customer churns and submits an erasure request. You have thirty days. Where is their data?

Forty tables in Postgres. Object storage. The search index. Redis. The warehouse, plus three BI extracts derived from it. Your error tracker, which has request payloads in stack traces. Application logs with ninety-day retention. Database backups with thirty-five-day point-in-time recovery. Your email provider's contact list. Stripe, which holds invoices you are legally required to keep for seven years. And a CSV an account manager exported to their laptop in March.

Two things become obvious at once. **Deletion is the provisioning list read backwards** — and you don't have the list. And **"delete everything" is not a coherent instruction**, because some of that data you are required to destroy and some of it you are required to keep, and nothing in your system knows which is which.

### And the states nobody modelled

In between, there's a set of situations the schema never anticipated. The trial expired. The card failed. The account is under investigation for abuse. The customer asked to pause for three months. The customer deleted their account and emailed four days later asking for it back.

Most systems encode this as `is_active`, `is_trial`, `suspended_at`, and `deleted_at` — four columns with sixteen combinations, of which perhaps five are meaningful, and no two services agree on what the other eleven mean. Suspension is implemented by blocking logins, so background jobs keep running, webhooks keep firing, metering keeps accruing, and the suspended tenant is still costing you money and still sending email to their customers.

**The tenant is an object with a lifecycle, and it's usually modelled as a row with a boolean.**

## What It Is

**Tenant lifecycle management** is treating the tenant as a first-class entity with an explicit set of states, explicit transitions between them, and a declarative description of the resources that must exist in each state — rather than as a side effect of a signup form.

Three pieces of vocabulary:

**Provisioning is not onboarding.** Provisioning creates resources: rows, buckets, indexes, subscriptions, credentials. Onboarding gets humans to value: invitations, seed data, imports, the first real task completed. They fail differently and they're owned by different people. A provisioning failure is an incident; an onboarding failure is churn that shows up in a dashboard six weeks later. Conflating them produces a signup flow that is technically flawless and commercially useless.

**The tenant catalogue** is the authoritative record: state, tier, placement (5.1), configuration, quotas, and the provisioning status of each resource. Everything else in this article assumes it exists and is the single source of truth.

**"Delete" means at least three different things** — deactivate (reversible, data intact), soft-delete (invisible, recoverable for a window), and erase (data destroyed, irreversible). Systems that use one word for all three eventually do the wrong one.

One neighbour worth separating: **the tenant lifecycle is not the subscription lifecycle.** The subscription is a billing object with its own states — trialing, active, past due, canceled — and it lives in Module 15, frequently inside Stripe. The tenant is an access and infrastructure object. They're coupled and they are not the same, and collapsing them produces the failure where a card expires on a Friday and a customer's data is gone on Saturday. A past-due subscription should usually mean a read-only tenant. It should never, by itself, mean a purged one.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Provisioning is a script a human runs, or a script triggered by signup with no retry and no record of what completed.
- There is a runbook titled something like "signup half-failed."
- Re-running provisioning is considered dangerous, and everyone knows it, and nobody has fixed it.
- Tenant state is spread across three or four boolean columns, and some combinations are meaningless but possible.
- "How many tenants are in state X" requires a query nobody can write from memory.
- Suspension blocks logins, and background jobs, webhooks, scheduled exports, and metering carry on.
- You cannot put a tenant into read-only mode.
- Deleting a tenant requires a human with a checklist, and the checklist lives in someone's notes.
- You have never tested deleting a tenant, or restoring one.
- A churned customer from last year is still in the warehouse, the error tracker, and the email provider's contact list.
- Onboarding requires seeding demo data by hand.
- Nobody can enumerate every system a tenant exists in without a meeting.
- A tenant created two years ago is missing a resource that tenants created last month all have.

## How It Works

### Model the states explicitly

```
                    ┌──────────────┐
    signup ───────► │ PROVISIONING │──fail──► FAILED ──► retry / purge
                    └──────┬───────┘
                           ▼
      trial ends /    ┌─────────┐      payment ok      ┌───────────┐
      payment fails   │ ACTIVE  │ ◄──────────────────► │ READ_ONLY │
      / over quota    └────┬────┘                      └─────┬─────┘
                           │                                 │
              abuse, legal hold, request                     │
                           ▼                                 │
                     ┌───────────┐                           │
                     │ SUSPENDED │                           │
                     └─────┬─────┘                           │
                           │         churn / erasure request │
                           ▼                                 ▼
                     ┌──────────────────┐   retention window ends
                     │ PENDING_DELETION │ ──────────────────────► PURGED
                     └──────────────────┘                        (tombstone)
```

Four observations about this diagram, because the value is in the states most systems lack.

**`READ_ONLY` is the one that's almost always missing and almost always needed.** It's the humane, reversible response to non-payment and to quota exhaustion — the customer can still see and export their data, which removes the worst kind of pressure from both sides of a billing dispute. It's also the state you need for a tenant migration cutover (5.3c), for an export-before-delete flow, and for "we think this account is compromised but we're not sure." Systems without it have exactly one lever, and it's the big one.

**`SUSPENDED` must stop everything, not just logins.** Enumerate it once and keep the list with the state definition: interactive login, API keys, webhook delivery, scheduled jobs, outbound email, inbound integrations, and usage metering. What should usually continue: data retention, export capability (sometimes a legal requirement), and admin access. A suspension that only blocks the front door is theatre — the tenant's automation carries on undisturbed.

**`PENDING_DELETION` with a retention window is the highest-value state for support.** Thirty days is the common choice. It converts "a customer deleted their account by accident" from a catastrophe into a ticket, and regulation generally accommodates a reasonable, documented delay rather than demanding instantaneous destruction.

**`PURGED` leaves a tombstone.** The tenant id, when it was purged, who requested it, what was destroyed, and what was retained under which legal basis. You need this to avoid reusing the identifier, to keep foreign keys in audit records resolvable, and to have an answer when someone asks you to prove the deletion happened.

The implementation discipline is one enum column, transitions in one module, every transition audited, and illegal transitions rejected rather than merely undocumented.

### Provisioning as a manifest, not a script

The shift is from *a sequence of things to do* to *a description of what must exist*, plus a runner that converges toward it.

```
      SCRIPT                          MANIFEST + RUNNER

  do step 1                     desired:  [tenant row, owner, billing,
  do step 2                                storage, index, seed data, …]
  do step 3                     observed: [tenant row, owner, billing]
  ...                           ────────────────────────────────────────
  (on failure: unknown state)   missing:  [storage, index, seed data, …]
                                → apply only what's missing, repeatedly,
                                  until desired == observed
```

Three properties make the runner safe:

**Every step is idempotent**, which in practice means every externally-created resource has a *deterministic* name derived from the tenant id. A Stripe customer created with idempotency key `tenant-8842-customer`, a bucket prefix of `t/8842/`, an index alias of `tenant-8842`. Random identifiers generated at creation time are what make re-running dangerous, because the second run has no way to discover that the first one succeeded.

**Every step's outcome is recorded**, per step, per tenant, durably. This is a small table and it's what converts "unknown state" into "steps 1–3 done, step 4 failed with this error, steps 5–11 pending."

**Every step declares its inverse**, or declares that it has none. This is what makes the deletion section below possible, and writing it at creation time is the only moment anyone will ever have full context on it.

### Fast path and slow path

Some steps are slow or unreliable in ways you don't control: provisioning a dedicated database, DNS propagation, a search index that takes minutes to build, a third party having a bad afternoon. If signup blocks on all of them, your signup availability is the product of every dependency's availability, and it will be worse than any of them.

So provisioning is asynchronous, and it splits:

- **Fast path** — the minimum set of resources needed for the user to log in and see something: tenant row, owner user, session. Synchronous, in one database transaction, fails cleanly.
- **Slow path** — everything else, reconciled in the background, with the UI showing an honest "still setting up" state for the features that aren't ready.

The cost of this is a requirement that propagates through the application: **it must tolerate partially provisioned tenants**, feature by feature rather than globally. "Search is still being prepared" is a state your search endpoint has to be able to return. That's a real ongoing tax, and it's smaller than the alternative.

### Onboarding, briefly

Distinct problem, worth naming so it doesn't get lost inside provisioning:

- **Seed data should be data, not code.** Default roles, permission sets, project templates, example content — all rows copied from a template tenant or a versioned fixture. The moment seeding is a code path, it drifts from what the product actually does.
- **The empty state is a product surface**, and for most B2B tools it's the highest-leverage screen in the application.
- **Import from a competitor** is the single most effective onboarding feature and the one that will generate the load pattern from 5.2b on its first day. Queue it, bulkhead it, and never run it on the interactive pool.

### Deletion: the manifest, backwards

Now the symmetry pays off. Deletion iterates the manifest in reverse and calls each step's inverse — and the interesting part is where the inverse doesn't exist.

Every resource falls into one of three classes, and the class has to be declared, not inferred:

```
  PURGE    personal data, content, credentials, derived indexes
           → destroy within the window

  RETAIN   invoices and financial records (statutory, often 7 years)
           security and audit logs (pseudonymize, keep the events)
           → destroy would be unlawful; keeping it is the compliant act

  NONE     emails already sent, webhooks already delivered,
           aggregate metrics already computed
           → no inverse exists; say so explicitly
```

Declaring `RETAIN` with a reason is not a loophole — it's the thing that makes your deletion defensible. "We kept the invoices because tax law requires it" is a correct answer. "We kept the invoices because nobody wrote that step" is the same outcome with no explanation available.

The steps that cause real trouble:

**Backups.** You cannot surgically remove one tenant from a point-in-time backup, and you should stop trying. The accepted approach is threefold: keep backup retention short and documented, ensure backups expire on a schedule you can state, and make sure any restore procedure re-applies pending deletions afterwards. Regulators have generally accepted this reasoning; engineers panic about it more than lawyers do.

**Logs and error trackers.** Retention-based expiry plus, more importantly, not writing tenant content into them in the first place (Module 19).

**The warehouse and everything downstream of it.** Deletion has to propagate through the same pipeline the data arrived by — typically as a deletion event on the stream, handled by each consumer. If your ETL is a one-way pull, you have no deletion path at all and you'll discover that during the request.

**Third parties.** Your email provider, analytics vendor, support desk, and error tracker all hold tenant data, and each has its own deletion API and its own latency. These are manifest steps like any other, and they're the ones that take days rather than seconds.

### Crypto-shredding

The technique that makes the immutable stores tractable: encrypt each tenant's data with a per-tenant key, keep the keys in a KMS, and destroy the key instead of the data. Ciphertext in backups, in archives, in append-only logs, and in object storage becomes permanently unreadable in one operation.

NIST recognizes cryptographic erase as a legitimate sanitization method, which is what makes this defensible rather than clever. The constraints are real, though: the data must have been encrypted with that key from the outset, keys must be genuinely per-tenant, and it does nothing for data that some downstream system decrypted and re-stored in the clear. It's the answer for the hard 10% of deletion, not a replacement for the ordinary 90%.

## When It's Appropriate

- **An explicit state enum: immediately, at any tenant count.** It costs one column and a module, and retrofitting it means reverse-engineering the meaning of sixteen boolean combinations from behaviour.
- **Idempotent, recorded provisioning: once provisioning touches three or more systems**, or once a single failure requires a human to reason about what happened.
- **Asynchronous provisioning: once any step can take more than a couple of seconds or depends on a third party.** The moment you sell a tier with a dedicated database, this is mandatory.
- **A `PENDING_DELETION` window: always.** It's a timestamp and a scheduled job, and it's the difference between a support ticket and an apology.
- **Declared retention classes: before your first erasure request**, because writing them under a thirty-day clock with a lawyer on the call is not when you want to be discovering which systems hold what.
- **Crypto-shredding: when you have hard erasure obligations plus immutable stores**, or when you already hold per-tenant keys for other reasons — the enterprise tier from 5.1 usually already does.
- **Self-serve deletion: when regulation requires it, or when deletion requests are a recurring support load.** Building it also forces the manifest to be complete, which is a benefit independent of the feature.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

**Five tenants onboarded by a salesperson.** A checklist in a shared document is the honest answer, and a reconciliation engine for five accounts is a hobby. Do write the checklist down — that checklist is the first draft of the manifest.

**A durable workflow engine for a three-step provisioning path.** Temporal or Step Functions solve this properly and they are a significant operational dependency. If you already run one, use it. Adopting one to provision a row, a user, and a bucket is a large answer to a small question.

**Crypto-shredding with no erasure obligation** and backups that expire in thirty-five days anyway. You've taken on key management and the risk of unrecoverable tenants in exchange for solving a problem time was already solving.

**Fourteen states when you have four.** Each additional state multiplies the transitions someone has to reason about, and unused states rot into ambiguity faster than missing ones do.

**Hard-deleting immediately because it feels more compliant.** It isn't — regulation contemplates a reasonable, documented period — and you've given up every accidental-deletion recovery in exchange for nothing.

**Building tenant-move tooling before anyone needs to move.** That's 5.3c, and the seam from 5.1 is what keeps it cheap to build later.

## Trade-offs

- **The manifest is a permanent maintenance surface.** Every new resource type — a new cache namespace, a new third-party integration, a new index — is now a provisioning step *and* a deletion step *and* a retention decision. Teams add the first and forget the other two, which is exactly how the warehouse ends up holding data from a customer who left last year.
- **Manifests drift, and old tenants fall behind.** A tenant provisioned under manifest v1 lacks resources that v6 assumes exist. This produces a recurring "backfill provisioning" chore and a class of bug that only affects your oldest and most valuable customers. Versioning the manifest and reconciling continuously is the fix, and it's ongoing work rather than a one-time build.
- **Asynchronous provisioning leaks into the application.** Every feature that depends on a slow-path resource needs a not-ready path. That conditional shows up in more places than the design suggests.
- **Soft delete complicates every query** and interacts with the isolation policies from 5.2a — a deleted tenant's rows still occupy tables, indexes, and storage quotas, and still have to be excluded everywhere, including in the reports nobody remembered.
- **The retention window is a liability window.** For thirty days you are holding data you have promised to destroy, and you need to be able to explain the policy if asked.
- **Idempotency constrains your integrations.** Deterministic external identifiers mean you can't use whatever id a provider hands back, and some providers make this awkward.
- **Testing the lifecycle touches real systems.** A test that creates a real Stripe customer on every run is a mess you'll be cleaning up for years; one that mocks everything proves nothing about the step that actually fails. Most teams end up with a small number of end-to-end provisioning tests against sandbox environments, run on a schedule rather than per-commit, and treat that compromise as permanent.
- **State machines accumulate.** Legal hold, pause-and-resume, partner-managed accounts, and trial extensions all arrive as requests for new states, and each one is cheap alone and expensive collectively.
- **Crypto-shredding adds key management as a critical dependency**, and a key lost by accident is a tenant destroyed by accident, with no recovery at all.

## Alternatives

- **Infrastructure as code, per tenant.** A Terraform workspace or a Kubernetes custom resource per tenant, reconciled by tooling that already exists and already handles drift, partial failure, and retries. This is the right answer for the siloed tier from 5.1, where a tenant genuinely is a set of infrastructure. It's heavy for a pooled tenant whose entire existence is four rows.
- **A durable workflow engine.** Temporal, Step Functions, or a saga in your own job system (Module 37). You get retries, compensation, timeouts, and a visible execution history without building any of it. The trade is a substantial new dependency on the signup path.
- **Lazy provisioning.** Create nothing at signup beyond the tenant row; create each resource on first use. There is no partial state because there is no state — the search index is created the first time someone searches, the bucket prefix the first time someone uploads. It genuinely dissolves the problem this article is about, at the cost of first-use latency and creation logic scattered across the codebase rather than described in one place. Underused, and worth seriously considering for anything that isn't slow to create.
- **Reduce the number of systems a tenant exists in.** The complexity here is directly proportional to that count. Every "let's put tenant data in this third-party tool" decision adds a provisioning step, a deletion step, and a retention question. Sometimes the best lifecycle improvement is deleting an integration.
- **Manual provisioning with a written checklist**, for low tenant counts with high contract values. Honest, cheap, and the checklist converts directly into the manifest when volume justifies it.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — write two lists.** The states your system actually has, including the implicit ones encoded in column combinations. And every system a tenant exists in, including the third-party tools. Both lists will be longer than expected, and the second one is the first draft of your deletion plan.
*Signal you're behind:* you can't produce either list in an afternoon.

**Level 1 — one state column, one transition module, every transition audited.** Replace the boolean soup. Add `READ_ONLY` even if nothing uses it yet.
*Signal:* two services disagree about what "inactive" means.

**Level 2 — make provisioning idempotent and recorded.** Deterministic external identifiers, per-step status, safe re-runs. The success criterion is that an on-call engineer can re-run provisioning without thinking.
*Signal:* the first half-failed signup, or the first duplicate subscription.

**Level 3 — split fast path from slow path, and reconcile.** Asynchronous provisioning with a converging runner, and an application that handles partial tenants per feature.
*Signal:* a third-party outage that blocked signups, or a tier whose provisioning takes minutes.

**Level 4 — deletion as the inverse manifest.** Retention classes declared per step, a retention window, downstream propagation to the warehouse and to third parties, and a tombstone record.
*Signal:* the first erasure request, or the first accidental deletion — whichever arrives first, and one of them will.

**Level 5 — continuous verification.** A scheduled job that reconciles every tenant against the current manifest version and reports drift, plus a deletion audit that can demonstrate what was purged and what was retained under which basis.
*Signal:* an audit, or discovering that tenants created before some date are quietly missing a resource.

## Implementation

Java and Spring, because the manifest pattern is mostly about types and because the most expensive mistake in this code is a transaction-boundary mistake that Spring makes easy to commit.

**A step declares what it does, how to undo it, and whether it can be undone.**

```java
public interface ProvisioningStep {
    String id();                                   // stable; stored in the ledger
    void apply(TenantContext ctx);                 // idempotent
    default Retention retention() { return Retention.PURGE; }
    default void revert(TenantContext ctx) {       // only called for PURGE
        throw new UnsupportedOperationException();
    }
}

public enum Retention {
    PURGE,                    // must be destroyed
    RETAIN,                   // must be kept — requires a documented reason
    NONE                      // no inverse exists (email sent, webhook delivered)
}
```

```java
@Component
class CreateBillingCustomer implements ProvisioningStep {
    public String id() { return "billing.customer"; }

    public void apply(TenantContext ctx) {
        // deterministic key: re-running returns the existing customer,
        // it does not create a second one
        stripe.customers().create(
            CustomerCreateParams.builder()
                .setName(ctx.tenantName())
                .putMetadata("tenant_id", ctx.tenantId())
                .build(),
            RequestOptions.builder()
                .setIdempotencyKey("tenant-" + ctx.tenantId() + "-customer")
                .build());
    }

    public Retention retention() { return Retention.RETAIN; }   // invoices: 7 years
}
```

**The runner skips what's already done and records what happens.**

```java
@Service
class ProvisioningRunner {

    private final List<ProvisioningStep> manifest;   // ordered; Spring injects all beans
    private final StepLedger ledger;

    public void reconcile(TenantContext ctx) {
        for (ProvisioningStep step : manifest) {
            if (ledger.isComplete(ctx.tenantId(), step.id())) continue;
            try {
                step.apply(ctx);                                  // no surrounding @Transactional
                ledger.markComplete(ctx.tenantId(), step.id());   // own transaction
            } catch (Exception e) {
                ledger.markFailed(ctx.tenantId(), step.id(), e);
                throw new ProvisioningIncomplete(step.id(), e);   // retried by the scheduler
            }
        }
    }
}
```

> **The transaction-boundary mistake.** The instinct is to annotate `reconcile` with `@Transactional` so that a failure rolls everything back. It does not roll everything back — Stripe, the object store, and the DNS provider have never heard of your transaction — and it does something worse: it holds a database connection open for the entire duration of every external call. One slow third party now occupies a connection per in-flight signup, which is precisely the pool-exhaustion failure from 5.2b, triggered by someone else's outage. Each step manages its own transaction, and the ledger write is its own commit.
>
> **`markComplete` after `apply` is a deliberate choice.** If the process dies between them, the step runs twice on the next pass — which is exactly why every step must be idempotent, and why the idempotency key above is load-bearing rather than decorative. Recording first would produce the opposite and much worse failure: a step marked done that never ran.

**Deletion is the same list, reversed, with the classes honoured.**

```java
public void purge(TenantContext ctx) {
    for (ProvisioningStep step : reversed(manifest)) {
        switch (step.retention()) {
            case PURGE -> { step.revert(ctx); ledger.markPurged(ctx, step.id()); }
            case RETAIN -> ledger.markRetained(ctx, step.id(), step.retentionReason());
            case NONE   -> ledger.markNoInverse(ctx, step.id());
        }
    }
    tombstones.record(ctx.tenantId(), ledger.summaryFor(ctx.tenantId()));
}
```

> The output of this loop is the artifact that matters. A tombstone that lists every step, what happened to it, and why anything retained was retained is the difference between "we deleted their data" as a claim and as something you can show. Write it at deletion time; reconstructing it later from logs is not possible.

## Further Reading

- **GDPR Articles 17 (right to erasure) and 5(1)(e) (storage limitation), plus Article 17(3) on the exemptions.** Short, readable, and the exemptions are the part engineers skip — the legal obligation to retain financial records is written into the same regulation that requires erasure. Read the actual text before designing the retention classes; this is not legal advice and the text is clearer than most summaries of it.
- **NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 1, _Guidelines for Media Sanitization_.** The source that makes cryptographic erase a recognized method rather than a clever trick, and the vocabulary to use when explaining it to an auditor.
- **Stripe's documentation on idempotent requests.** The clearest public treatment of idempotency keys — scope, lifetime, and what happens on replay. The pattern generalizes to every external step in a manifest.
- **The Kubernetes documentation on the operator pattern and controllers**, and the discussion of level-triggered versus edge-triggered reconciliation in _Programming Kubernetes_ (Hausenblas & Schimanski). This is the fully worked version of the manifest idea, and the level-triggered framing — converge from whatever state you find, rather than replaying a recorded sequence — is a genuine step beyond the runner above.
- **Temporal's documentation on durable execution**, especially the treatment of activities, retries, and compensation. Even if you never adopt it, its model is the clearest available description of what a correct provisioning workflow has to guarantee. Sagas get their proper treatment in Module 37.
- **Tod Golding, _Building Multi-Tenant SaaS Architectures_ — the onboarding chapters.** Treats onboarding as a control-plane concern with its own availability requirements, which is the framing that makes the fast-path/slow-path split obvious.

A **Go** sibling would show the level-triggered version of this and reveal what the step ledger is hiding. Written as a Kubernetes-style controller with a Tenant custom resource, the runner stops replaying a recorded list and instead *observes* what exists, diffs it against the desired spec, and applies the difference — every loop, forever. That handles three things the ledger can't: resources deleted out of band, manifest version drift across old tenants, and the state where the ledger and reality disagree. It also brings its own problems, chiefly that observation costs an API call per resource per tenant per loop, which is fine for hundreds of tenants and ruinous for hundreds of thousands. The interesting content is exactly that boundary. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying: for any entity in any system, ask **what is the complete list of places this exists, and what happens if step four of eleven fails?** Almost nowhere is that list written down, and almost everywhere it's longer than the team believes.

There's a sharper version of the same question, and it's the one to actually use: **can you delete it?** Creation and destruction are the same list read in two directions, so an inability to delete a tenant cleanly is not a gap in your deletion code. It's proof that nobody knows what creating one does.
