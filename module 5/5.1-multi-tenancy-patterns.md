# Multi-Tenancy Patterns

_Module 5.1 — Multi-Tenancy Architecture_

## The Problem

Take it in the order it actually happens.

You've built a project-management tool. Your first customer is Northwind. You provision a VM, a Postgres instance, and a DNS record — `northwind.yourapp.com` — and it works.

Contoso signs a month later. The fastest path to a second working deployment is the one you already know works, so you clone it. Its own VM, its own database, its own DNS record. Two customers, two stacks. Still entirely reasonable.

Now run that forward to forty.

- **A security patch is forty deployments.** At twenty minutes of attention each — including the three that fail for unrelated reasons — that's a two-day job, and customer number forty runs the vulnerable build for two days.
- **A schema migration is forty migrations**, and number twenty-three fails on the one customer who has a duplicate row from an incident in March. You now have thirty-nine customers on the new schema and one on the old, and the application code has to tolerate both until you fix it.
- **Utilization is terrible.** Every instance is sized for its own customer's peak. Average CPU across the fleet is about 4%. The customer paying $49 a month costs $70 a month to host, and you can't see that in aggregate because your cloud bill is one number.
- **"It's slow for Contoso" is a different investigation every time.** Different box, different data volume, possibly a different build. The fix you applied to Northwind in March may not exist on Contoso's instance.
- **Onboarding requires a human.** A new customer is a ticket, a provisioning script someone babysits, and a DNS change. Self-serve signup is not available to you at any price.

So you do the obvious thing. One deployment, one database, and a column:

```sql
ALTER TABLE projects ADD COLUMN tenant_id BIGINT NOT NULL;
```

One deploy. One migration. One set of dashboards. The marginal cost of customer forty-one is approximately zero, and onboarding is an `INSERT` rather than a ticket. This is a genuinely enormous improvement and most SaaS products should start here.

And immediately, a different list:

- Someone writes `SELECT * FROM projects WHERE archived = false` and ships it. One missing predicate, and Contoso's board renders on Northwind's screen. That isn't a bug, it's a breach, and it's reportable.
- Contoso runs a quarter-end export that scans four million rows. Every other customer's p99 triples for eleven minutes.
- Northwind's security review asks whether their data is stored separately from other customers'. The honest answer — "it's in the same table, filtered by a column" — is true, and is also the sentence that turns a signature into a six-week security review.
- A customer deletes a project by accident and asks for a restore to 3pm yesterday. Your backup is one database containing forty customers, thirty-nine of whom have done real work since 3pm yesterday.
- A German customer's procurement team requires their data to remain in the EU. Your database is in `us-east-1`.

Neither list is the wrong answer. They are the two ends of one axis:

```
        ISOLATION ──────────────────────────────────────────────►
        ◄────────────────────────────────────────── COST EFFICIENCY

   one DB, tenant_id column                    one full stack per tenant
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   cost per tenant      ≈ 0                    ≈ a whole deployment
   blast radius         every tenant           one tenant
   schema migration     1                      N, with partial failure
   restore one tenant   bespoke script         restore the backup
   noisy neighbours     yes                    no
   onboarding           an INSERT              a pipeline run
   "is our data separate?"   no                yes
```

The useful realization is not which end is correct. It's that **you do not have to pick one point on this axis for the whole system, and you do not have to pick the same point for every customer.**

## What It Is

**Multi-tenancy** is one running system serving multiple tenants — organizations, workspaces, accounts — whose data, configuration, and ideally performance are isolated from one another on infrastructure that is at least partly shared.

Three pieces of vocabulary, because the rest of the module leans on them.

**A tenant is not a user.** It's the unit of isolation, billing, and configuration. One tenant has many users, and one human can belong to several tenants — the consultant who works in three client workspaces, the developer who has a personal account and a work account. Modelling the tenant as "a user who has sub-users" is a decision that looks fine for eighteen months and then requires a migration when that consultant shows up.

**Pool, bridge, silo.** The common names for three points on the axis: everything shared, partially shared, nothing shared. You'll meet them in AWS's SaaS literature and in vendor architecture documents, and they're worth knowing because they let people compare designs in three words.

**Multi-tenancy is not one decision.** It's decided independently per layer — compute, database, object storage, cache, search index, message queue — and real systems mix them constantly. Pooled compute in front of siloed databases is an extremely common shape, and describing that system as "multi-tenant" or "single-tenant" loses all the information.

Two neighbours worth separating right now:

**Multi-tenancy vs. multi-instance.** The forty-deployments scenario above is multi-*instance*: one tenant per running copy. It's a legitimate architecture, not a failure to be multi-tenant, and it's what your customers mean when they ask for a "dedicated instance." The difference is the operational model, not the code.

**Tenancy vs. sharding.** Sharding splits data to get past the capacity of one machine. Tenancy splits data to keep parties apart. They're different goals that happen to be solved by the same key remarkably often, because `tenant_id` is usually an excellent shard key — but "we're sharded by tenant" and "tenants are isolated" are separate claims, and only one of them is about security.

## Recognizing the Problem

You're facing this decision, or your current answer to it is failing, if:

- You have a deploy script that takes a customer name as an argument.
- Your migration runbook contains the line "repeat for each customer."
- Reviewers catch missing `tenant_id` predicates in pull requests. Sometimes.
- One customer's usage spike is visible in every customer's latency graph.
- You cannot restore a single tenant to a point in time without writing a bespoke script under pressure.
- Someone asks what customer X costs to serve, and answering requires a spreadsheet and a guess.
- A prospect's security questionnaire asks how tenant data is segregated, and the honest answer requires a paragraph.
- One tenant holds 60% of your rows and three hundred tenants share the rest.
- Onboarding a customer takes a human being more than five minutes.
- A per-customer config file has grown a per-customer code path inside it.
- You're being asked for data residency, customer-managed encryption keys, or a per-customer uptime SLA, and your architecture has no place to put any of them.

## How It Works

### The one idea that organizes the whole space

The patterns differ in a lot of ways, but the difference that matters is **where the tenant boundary is enforced.**

```
POOL      enforced in every query        SELECT … WHERE tenant_id = 42
BRIDGE    enforced in the connection     SET search_path = tenant_42
SILO      enforced in the topology       connect to db-tenant-42.internal
```

In a pooled system, every query is an opportunity to get it wrong, forever, including the ones written by the intern in eighteen months and the ones in the analytics job nobody owns. In a bridged or siloed system the decision is made once, at the edge of the request, and everything downstream is confined whether or not it knows it.

That's the real currency of this trade: not "shared or separate" but **how many places have to be correct.**

### What's shared, layer by layer

```
                 POOL ──────────────────────────────► SILO

  compute        shared app instances          instances per tenant
  database       one DB, tenant_id column      DB per tenant
  schema         one schema                    schema per tenant
  object store   one bucket, key prefix        bucket per tenant
  cache          one Redis, key prefix         instance per tenant
  search         one index, tenant filter      index per tenant
  queue          shared topics                 queue per tenant
```

Each row is independent. A very common production shape is pooled compute, pooled cache, pooled search, and a database that's pooled for the standard tier and siloed for enterprise.

### Pattern A — shared database, shared schema (pool)

Every table carries `tenant_id`. Every query filters on it. Every index leads with it.

```sql
CREATE TABLE projects (
  tenant_id  BIGINT NOT NULL,
  id         BIGSERIAL,
  name       TEXT NOT NULL,
  PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, id)
);
CREATE INDEX ON projects (tenant_id, created_at DESC);
```

The composite primary key isn't decorative. Leading every index with `tenant_id` means a query for one tenant touches a contiguous range of the index rather than scanning across everyone's rows, and it makes later physical separation (partitioning, sharding) possible without re-indexing the world.

Cheapest to run, cheapest to operate, worst isolation. Connection pooling is trivial: one pool, sized for total load, shared by everyone. Migrations are one `ALTER TABLE` — though on a table holding every tenant's rows, a long-held lock is now an outage for all of them rather than one.

**The important sub-variant:** pooled storage with connection-level enforcement. PostgreSQL's row-level security lets you set a tenant on the connection and have the database itself append the predicate to every query, including the ones you forgot. That's pool economics with something close to bridge-level enforcement, and it's the subject of the next article.

### Pattern B — shared database, schema per tenant (bridge)

One database instance, one schema per tenant, identical table definitions inside each. The tenant is selected by the connection's search path, and queries contain no tenant predicate at all.

Isolation moves from the query to the connection, which is the meaningful upgrade. A forgotten `WHERE` clause now returns the right tenant's data instead of everyone's. You also get per-tenant operations that pooled can't offer cheaply: `pg_dump -n tenant_northwind` is a real, single-tenant backup, and per-tenant schema extensions become possible without a nullable column for every customer's custom field.

What it costs is catalogue pressure. Every schema multiplies your table, index, and constraint count. Two hundred tables across three thousand tenants is six hundred thousand relations, and the consequences are indirect and unpleasant: system-catalogue bloat, autovacuum falling behind, `pg_dump` of the whole database taking hours, planner memory per connection climbing. There's no hard ceiling; teams typically start feeling it somewhere in the low thousands of schemas. Migrations are now N schema migrations with partial-failure semantics, executed against one instance.

### Pattern B′ — one schema, partitioned by tenant

Worth naming because it's regularly overlooked: declarative partitioning by `tenant_id` keeps a single logical table and single set of application queries while giving each tenant (or each group of tenants) its own physical storage.

You get per-tenant vacuum behaviour, per-tenant detach-and-archive, and the ability to drop a departing tenant's data instantly instead of running a cascading delete across forty tables. You don't get any additional access-control boundary — a missing predicate still reads everything. It's a physical-layout tool, not an isolation tool, and treating it as the latter is a mistake.

### Pattern C — database per tenant

Each tenant gets its own database, usually on shared hardware, sometimes on its own. The application resolves the tenant at the edge and routes to the right connection.

This is where the properties customers actually ask for become available: per-tenant backup and restore, per-tenant encryption keys, per-tenant placement for residency, the ability to move one noisy tenant to its own hardware without touching anyone else's. It's also where "our data is separate" stops being a euphemism.

The constraint that decides whether you can do it is connection arithmetic, and it's rarely stated up front:

```
  30 app instances × 500 tenants × 5 connections
  = 75,000 connections

  PostgreSQL, comfortably:  a few hundred per instance
```

Per-instance-per-tenant pools cannot be the design. What's actually workable is lazily created, aggressively evicted pools sized at one or two connections, an external pooler in transaction mode, or a routing tier that keeps far fewer application instances talking to the databases than you have serving HTTP. Any of these is a real piece of infrastructure with its own failure modes, and it's the cost of this pattern that people underestimate.

Migrations are now N migrations across N databases, which needs an orchestrator that tracks per-tenant schema version, retries, reports, and tolerates a fleet where tenant 37 is one version behind for six hours. Your application has to run correctly against both versions during that window, which is a discipline — expand, migrate, contract — not a tool.

### Pattern D — full stack per tenant (silo)

Separate compute, database, and everything else, per tenant. The forty-deployments scenario, done deliberately and with automation rather than accidentally.

This is the answer for a genuinely small number of genuinely large customers: regulated industries, customer-managed keys, contractual isolation, or bring-your-own-cloud deployments where the software runs inside the customer's own account and you don't hold their data at all. The unit economics only work when the contract is large enough to absorb an entire deployment's cost and the operational load of keeping it patched.

The thing that makes it survivable is that it's produced by the same pipeline as everything else. A silo that is deployed by hand is the original problem with a nicer name.

### Pattern E — hybrid and tiered

What most systems above about thirty customers actually are.

The standard tier is pooled. The enterprise tier is a dedicated database, or a dedicated stack. Individual outlier tenants get moved out of the pool when they become a problem, regardless of what they pay. One codebase, one deploy, several placements.

The architectural requirement that makes this possible is small and needs to exist from very early: **the application asks a catalogue where a tenant lives, rather than knowing.** If every data access begins with something equivalent to `connectionFor(tenantId)`, then moving a tenant is a data copy and a row update. If it begins with a global `db` handle, moving a tenant is a refactor of the entire codebase performed under commercial pressure.

### Pattern F — cells (pods)

The variation you'll meet once the tenant count is large, and the one that solves a different problem from everything above.

A cell is a complete, fixed-capacity copy of the stack that serves a *group* of tenants — say five thousand of them. A routing layer maps tenant to cell. Cells never talk to each other.

```
                    ┌── router: tenant → cell ──┐
                    ▼            ▼              ▼
              ┌─────────┐  ┌─────────┐   ┌─────────┐
              │ cell 1  │  │ cell 2  │   │ cell 3  │
              │ app+db  │  │ app+db  │   │ app+db  │
              │ 5k tnts │  │ 5k tnts │   │ 5k tnts │
              └─────────┘  └─────────┘   └─────────┘
                 deploy sequentially: a bad release
                 reaches 1/N of customers, not all
```

Note what this buys and what it doesn't. It doesn't improve isolation between two tenants inside the same cell — inside a cell you're still pooled. What it bounds is **blast radius**: a bad deploy, a corrupted index, a runaway migration, a saturated connection pool now affects one Nth of your customers. It also gives you a unit of capacity you can reason about, because you've measured what one cell holds, and growth becomes "add a cell" rather than "hope."

The costs are real: the routing layer becomes critical infrastructure, tenant migration between cells has to be a supported operation, cross-cell reporting needs somewhere else to live, and your per-cell fixed costs multiply. It's the answer when blast radius is your binding constraint, not before.

## When It's Appropriate

**Pooled, shared schema** — many tenants, most of them small, self-serve signup, low per-tenant revenue. Ten thousand tenants with a median of 40 MB each and a 99th percentile fifty times that. The marginal cost of a tenant has to approach zero because a lot of them will pay you $29 a month or nothing at all. Start here unless you have a specific reason not to.

**Schema per tenant** — tens to low hundreds of tenants, where you need per-tenant backup and restore, or genuine per-tenant schema extension, and one database instance still comfortably holds everyone. A B2B tool with 150 customers, each with real but bounded data, is a good fit.

**Database per tenant** — when a specific customer requirement names it. Contractual data separation. Data residency in a particular region. Customer-managed encryption keys. A per-tenant restore SLA. A per-tenant performance guarantee you can't make while sharing an instance. Or plain physics: one tenant whose working set no longer fits alongside everyone else's. Typically a five- or six-figure annual contract, because it has to pay for the operational load.

**Full silo** — healthcare, government, defence, or bring-your-own-cloud, where the customer's requirement is that your software runs in their environment. The deal is large, the deployment is automated, and you've decided to be in this business on purpose.

**Hybrid** — once you have a mixed book. Two hundred self-serve tenants, twelve enterprise contracts, and one self-serve tenant who somehow generates a third of your write traffic. Almost everyone ends up here.

**Cells** — when a single bad deploy or a single saturated resource currently affects one hundred percent of customers and that's no longer acceptable. Usually tens of thousands of tenants, or a smaller number with an availability commitment you're contractually exposed on.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

**You have one tenant.** An internal tool used by one company doesn't need a tenancy model. Adding a `tenant_id` column now is cheap insurance and worth doing; building resolution middleware, provisioning workflows, and connection routing for a hypothetical second tenant is not.

**The tenant is the user.** In a consumer product where each person's data is their own, your isolation key is `user_id` and you're done. Introducing a workspace layer because SaaS architecture articles have one is a whole subsystem in exchange for nothing. The honest caveat: if "shared with my team" is plausibly on the roadmap, the cheap version is a workspace table where every workspace has exactly one member, because retrofitting a tenant boundary into a system that doesn't have one is genuinely expensive.

**Database per tenant on a free tier.** Twenty thousand free-signup databases is not a scaling problem, it's an operational catastrophe — twenty thousand migrations, twenty thousand things to monitor, twenty thousand connection pools, and a provisioning path that becomes your least reliable component. Free tiers belong in the pool.

**Building the isolated tier before anyone has paid for it.** "Enterprise customers will want dedicated databases" is a plausible prediction and a terrible reason to build routing infrastructure this quarter. Build the *seam* — the catalogue lookup — and build the rest when a contract names it.

**Cells at two hundred tenants.** You've bought a routing tier, N× fixed costs, and a tenant-migration tool to reduce a blast radius that a good deployment strategy would have reduced for free.

**Per-tenant customization as code branches.** The first `if (tenant == "contoso")` is defensible. The fortieth is a fork with extra steps, and the way out is configuration and feature flags rather than tenancy architecture.

## Trade-offs

Even the right choice here costs you, permanently:

- **Everything becomes tenant-scoped, forever.** Every query, cache key, log line, metric, background job, webhook payload, export, and search document. Not one of them can be written tenant-agnostically again, and forgetting once in a pooled system is a disclosure incident rather than a bug.
- **Migrations get worse in both directions.** Pooled: one migration, but a lock on a table containing everyone's rows, so every schema change is a fleet-wide risk. Siloed: N migrations with partial failure, version skew across the fleet, and application code that must work against two schema versions simultaneously.
- **Cross-tenant questions get hard exactly where isolation got good.** "How many tenants use feature X", admin dashboards, usage-based billing rollups, and product analytics are a `GROUP BY` in the pool and an ETL pipeline into a warehouse once tenants live in separate databases. You will build that pipeline; budget for it.
- **Single-tenant restore is the capability people discover they lack during the incident.** In a pooled database it means restoring a full copy to a scratch instance, extracting one tenant's rows in dependency order, and reconciling foreign keys against live data that has moved on. It's bespoke, it's slow, and it gets written at 2am unless it's written beforehand.
- **Noisy neighbours are structural in the pool.** One tenant can consume shared connections, shared CPU, shared cache, and shared queue workers. Per-tenant quotas and rate limits stop being a nicety and become load-bearing (Module 18).
- **Cost attribution is free in a silo and genuinely hard in a pool.** "What does this customer cost us to serve?" requires per-tenant instrumentation of queries, storage, and compute that you have to build deliberately.
- **Observability cardinality bites.** `tenant_id` is an excellent log field and a ruinous metric label — fifty thousand label values will break your metrics backend and your invoice. The usual compromise is high-cardinality in logs and traces, low-cardinality in metrics with an explicit top-N tenant list.
- **Tenant deletion is a project.** "Delete this tenant" spans every table, plus object storage, plus search indexes, plus caches, plus the analytics warehouse, plus backups you can't selectively edit. Regulation has opinions about how completely and how fast (Module 34).
- **You need tests that prove isolation**, not just tests that prove features work. A test asserting that tenant B's request returns 404 for tenant A's resource is the one nobody writes and the only one that would have caught the missing predicate.
- **The choice is expensive to reverse.** Moving from pooled to per-tenant databases is a per-tenant data migration with a cutover window, run hundreds of times. It's doable, and nobody enjoys it.

## Alternatives

**Don't be multi-tenant — ship an installable product.** Customers run it themselves; you ship releases. This solves data isolation and residency completely and hands you a different set of problems: versions in the field, support against environments you can't see, and no ability to fix anything by deploying.

**Automated single-tenant deployments (BYOC).** The silo pattern as a product, where the stack runs in the customer's own cloud account. Increasingly common for enterprise data tooling. The economics only work if provisioning and upgrading are fully automated, because the human cost per deployment is what kills it.

**Region-level separation instead of tenant-level.** Often what a customer actually needs when they say "our data must be separate" is "our data must be in the EU." Deploying one pooled stack per region is dramatically cheaper than per-tenant isolation and satisfies most residency requirements. Ask which one they're asking for — the answer changes the architecture by an order of magnitude.

**Distributed SQL sharded by tenant.** Citus, and its equivalents, distribute a pooled schema across nodes keyed on `tenant_id`. Application code stays pooled while the physical layout behaves like one tenant per shard. Buys horizontal scale and physical separation without per-tenant databases; costs a more constrained query surface and a heavier database platform.

**Managed per-tenant databases as a primitive.** Some platforms make database creation cheap enough that per-tenant databases stop being absurd at scale. Worth evaluating on the honest question: not whether creating ten thousand databases is possible, but whether migrating and monitoring ten thousand databases is something you want to own.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — `tenant_id` on every table, from the first migration.** Composite primary keys, tenant-leading indexes, and a foreign key to a `tenants` table. Do this even with one customer. It is nearly free now and it is the single most expensive thing to retrofit later, because the retrofit means backfilling every table, rewriting every query, and doing it while the system is in use.
*Signal you're behind:* you don't have it.

**Level 1 — enforce the boundary in one place instead of every place.** Row-level security, a session-scoped connection setting, or at minimum a data-access layer that no query can bypass. The goal is that a developer writing a new query cannot forget, rather than usually remembering.
*Signal:* the first near-miss in code review, or the first security questionnaire you answer with "developer discipline."
This is the next article.

**Level 2 — make the tenant a first-class operational object.** Tenant in every log line and trace. Per-tenant rate limits and quotas. Per-tenant feature flags and configuration in data rather than in code. A provisioning path with no human in it.
*Signal:* an incident took forty minutes longer because you couldn't filter logs by tenant; or onboarding still requires a person.

**Level 3 — introduce the placement seam, then use it.** Add a catalogue: tenant → where its data lives. Route through it everywhere, even while every tenant resolves to the same pool. Then move one tenant out — the loudest one — and confirm the application code didn't change.
*Signal:* one tenant exceeds 20% of load, or a contract requires something the pool cannot give.

**Level 4 — tiers as a product decision.** An isolated tier with a price attached, provisioned by the same pipeline, migrated by the same orchestrator. What makes this sustainable is that isolation level is a *property of the tenant record*, not a fork of the codebase.
*Signal:* a deal lost or discounted over isolation requirements.

**Level 5 — cells.** Group tenants into fixed-capacity stacks, deploy cell by cell, and make tenant migration between cells a routine operation rather than an incident response.
*Signal:* blast radius is the constraint you keep hitting, and per-tenant isolation isn't.

Almost nobody needs to go past level 2 in their first two years. The seam at level 3 is the one worth building early, because it's cheap while the answer is always the same and expensive once it isn't.

## Implementation

Java and Hibernate, for a specific reason: Hibernate has first-class support for two of these patterns and a different mechanism for the third, so the same ORM makes visible what actually changes between them — and what doesn't.

**Pooled: the tenant is a column.**

```java
@Entity
public class Project {
    @Id @GeneratedValue
    private Long id;

    @TenantId                 // Hibernate 6+: filters reads, populates writes
    private String tenantId;

    private String slug;
    private String name;
}
```

```java
@Component
public class RequestTenantResolver implements CurrentTenantIdentifierResolver<String> {
    @Override public String resolveCurrentTenantIdentifier() {
        return TenantContext.require();     // set by a filter — Module 5.4
    }
    @Override public boolean validateExistingCurrentSessions() { return true; }
}
```

That's the whole mechanism. Hibernate adds `tenant_id = ?` to the SQL it generates and sets the field on insert, so `projectRepository.findBySlug("roadmap")` is scoped without the repository mentioning it.

> **The gap to be clear-eyed about.** This filters queries *Hibernate generates*. Native SQL, `JdbcTemplate`, the reporting job somebody wrote against a read replica, a stored procedure, and the nightly export script are all outside it. In a pooled system the leak will be in one of those, not in your entity mappings — which is exactly the argument for pushing enforcement down into the database itself.

**Bridge and silo: the tenant is the connection.**

```properties
spring.jpa.properties.hibernate.multiTenancy=SCHEMA   # or DATABASE
```

```java
public class TenantConnectionProvider implements MultiTenantConnectionProvider<String> {

    @Override
    public Connection getConnection(String tenantId) throws SQLException {
        Connection c = pool.getConnection();
        c.setSchema("tenant_" + tenantId);        // SCHEMA mode
        return c;
    }

    @Override
    public void releaseConnection(String tenantId, Connection c) throws SQLException {
        c.setSchema(defaultSchema);               // ← reset before returning to the pool
        c.close();
    }
}
```

The entity loses `@TenantId` entirely. The repositories, services, and controllers are unchanged — byte for byte identical across all three patterns. **That property is the thing to design for**, because it's what makes moving a tenant from the pool to its own database a configuration change rather than a rewrite.

> **Two failure modes live in that second method.** Forget the reset in `releaseConnection`, and a pooled connection carries the previous request's schema into the next one — intermittent, load-dependent, invisible in testing, and a cross-tenant read. And `setSchema` on a pooled connection is a real round trip; at high request rates the reset is measurable, which is one of the reasons bridge patterns often sit behind a connection pool per tenant rather than a shared one.
>
> In `DATABASE` mode, do the arithmetic before you commit: thirty application instances multiplied by the number of tenants each may serve, multiplied by pool size, against a database that tolerates a few hundred connections. The routing tier that resolves this is the subject of Module 5.4.

**One more, because it's the leak nobody expects.** Hibernate includes the tenant identifier in its second-level cache key under `SCHEMA` and `DATABASE` modes. A cache you wrote yourself does not:

```java
cache.get("project:slug:" + slug);          // wrong — slugs are unique per tenant
cache.get("project:" + tenantId + ":" + slug);
```

Primary keys are usually globally unique, so caching by id hides this. Slugs, email addresses, external reference numbers, and invoice numbers are unique *within* a tenant — and those are precisely the keys people cache by. Every cache namespace, every search index document id, every object storage key prefix, and every idempotency key needs the tenant in it, and the bug is silent until two tenants happen to pick the same slug.

## Further Reading

- **AWS, _SaaS Tenant Isolation Strategies_ (whitepaper), and the SaaS Lens of the Well-Architected Framework.** The origin of the pool/bridge/silo vocabulary and the most complete public treatment of tiering and isolation enforcement. Read the isolation whitepaper first; it's short and it's the one that changes how you think about enforcement points.
- **Tod Golding, _Building Multi-Tenant SaaS Architectures_ (O'Reilly, 2024).** Book-length version of the above, and notably good on the parts that aren't architecture — tenant onboarding, tiering, and cost attribution as design inputs rather than afterthoughts.
- **Microsoft, Azure Architecture Center — "Architectural approaches for storage and data in multitenant solutions."** The clearest side-by-side of the storage patterns, with explicit notes on the scaling limits of each. Vendor-flavoured but the reasoning is portable.
- **Citus documentation, "Multi-tenant applications."** The best written explanation of the pooled model taken seriously — why `tenant_id` belongs in every primary key, every index, and every join condition, with the consequences spelled out. Worth reading even if you never use Citus.
- **PostgreSQL documentation on row-level security and on table partitioning.** The two mechanisms behind Pattern A′ and Pattern B′. Short, and both are more capable than the tutorials suggest.
- **AWS Well-Architected, "Reducing the scope of impact with cell-based architecture."** The cell pattern stated generally, including cell sizing and the tenant-to-cell placement problem.
- **Salesforce's published multitenant architecture papers.** Dated, and worth reading as the extreme case: a metadata-driven pooled model where even the schema is virtualized, plus their pod model, which is cells before the word was common.
- **Hibernate ORM User Guide, the multitenancy chapter.** Precisely what each strategy does and doesn't cover, including the second-level cache behaviour mentioned above.

A **Go** sibling would reveal something this one hides. With no ORM offering a tenancy mode, every choice made by a Hibernate configuration flag becomes explicit application code: the tenant travels in `context.Context`, connection acquisition is a function you write, and there is no framework to forget on your behalf. That makes the placement seam impossible to skip — and it makes the opposite risk vivid, because nothing stops a `*sql.DB` from being used directly by a package that never heard of tenants. The interesting content is the pattern for making the wrong thing hard to reach: unexported pools, a `TenantDB` type that can only be obtained from a context, and what a linter can enforce that a type system can't. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying out of this article is to stop asking whether a system is multi-tenant and start asking two narrower questions about it. **Which layers are shared?** — because the answer is usually different for compute, database, cache, and search, and describing it as one thing loses all the detail. And **where is the boundary enforced?** — in the query, the connection, or the topology. Nearly everything else in this module follows from those two answers: what a mistake costs, what a customer can be promised, what a migration means, and what has to be true before you can move one tenant somewhere else.
