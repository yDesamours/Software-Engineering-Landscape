# Connection Routing and Placement

_Module 5.4b — Multi-Tenancy Architecture_

## The Problem

The dedicated-database tier works. Three enterprise customers, three databases, a lookup table, and a pool per tenant created on first use. It has been fine for eight months, so you sell it to more people.

At forty dedicated tenants, do the arithmetic nobody did at three.

```
  HikariCP defaults, per tenant, per application instance:
      maximumPoolSize = 10,  minimumIdle = 2

   3 tenants × 2 idle ×  12 instances  =     72 connections   ← fine
  40 tenants × 2 idle ×  12 instances  =    960 connections   ← idle, doing nothing
```

Nine hundred and sixty connections that exist solely to not be used. Each PostgreSQL backend costs single-digit megabytes of server memory, so several gigabytes of the database's RAM is now dedicated to idleness. Everything still works.

Then you deploy. For ninety seconds, twelve new instances are warming up while twelve old ones drain:

```
  80 tenants-worth of pools × 24 instances  =  1,920 connections
  max_connections = 500
```

The database refuses connections. Not just for the dedicated tenants — for every tenant sharing that instance, including the pooled tier that had nothing to do with any of this.

Two things about this failure are worth noting. **It arrives during a deploy, not during a traffic peak**, so no amount of capacity planning based on request volume predicted it. And **it arrives suddenly**: connection limits don't degrade, they refuse. There is no gentle shoulder on that curve.

### The route that went stale

A tenant was moved at 14:00 using everything from 5.3c — backfill, sync, verify, fence, flip. Instance 7 has the old placement cached with a fifteen-minute TTL. For the next twelve minutes it sends that tenant's writes to a database that now rejects them.

That's the *good* version, and only because of the fence. Without it, those writes would have succeeded into a database nobody reads any more.

### The cold start nobody attributes

A fresh instance boots and starts taking traffic for two hundred tenants. It opens two hundred pools: two hundred TCP handshakes, TLS negotiations, authentications, and session setup statements, all in the first minute, all on the request path. p99 after every deploy is terrible for two minutes, and the graph is blamed on JIT warmup for a year.

### What's actually going on

5.1 introduced `connectionFor(tenantId)` as the seam that makes tenant placement changeable. It looks like a function call. It is a caching problem, a capacity problem, a failure-isolation problem, and a scheduling problem, and each of those has a way of arriving during a deploy.

## What It Is

**Placement** is the mapping from a tenant to where its data physically lives: which database, schema, cluster, cell, and region.

**The catalogue** is the authoritative store of that mapping.

**Routing** is turning a tenant id into a usable connection to the right place.

Two distinctions worth fixing.

**Placement is not sharding.** Sharding *derives* location from a key by a function — hash the tenant id, take the modulus, done. Placement *looks it up* in a table. The difference is the whole reason this article exists: a lookup costs you a dependency on every request and buys you the ability to move one tenant without moving any others. A hash function costs nothing per request and makes moving a single tenant impossible without rehashing a whole population. In the sharding literature these are directory-based and algorithmic routing, and multi-tenant systems almost always want the first, because tenants differ enormously in size and customers buy placement guarantees individually.

**Routing is not resolution.** 5.4a answered "which tenant is this request for." This article answers "where does that tenant's data live." They're consecutive steps that frequently share a middleware and should not share a cache, because they have completely different invalidation triggers.

One more piece of vocabulary: an in-process **connection pool** (HikariCP, pgx, node-postgres) and an external **connection pooler** (PgBouncer, pgpool, RDS Proxy) are different things with overlapping names, and at scale you generally need both.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Nobody has multiplied instances × tenants × pool size.
- A deploy has caused connection exhaustion, or you can't rule it out.
- `max_connections` has been raised more than once, each time as a fix.
- Idle connections dominate your database's memory usage.
- Placement lives in a config file, an environment variable, or a hardcoded map.
- Moving a tenant requires restarting application instances to pick up the change.
- One tenant's database being unreachable makes the whole process unhealthy.
- The first minute after every deploy has visibly worse latency.
- "Which database is tenant X on?" requires SSH or a runbook.
- Read replicas exist and each query's routing decision is made by hand, differently.
- Onboarding a dedicated-database customer requires a config change and a release.
- Your cache, search, and object-storage routing were each implemented separately and don't agree about where a tenant lives.

## How It Works

### The catalogue

```
  tenants     tenant_id | placement_id | state | epoch

  placements  id | kind        | credential_ref     | region | replica_ref
              ---+-------------+--------------------+--------+------------
               1 | pool        | secret://pg-main   | eu-w1  | secret://pg-main-ro
              14 | dedicated   | secret://pg-t8842  | eu-c1  | null
              22 | cell        | secret://pg-cell-3 | us-e1  | secret://…
```

Four properties that matter more than the schema:

**Credentials are references, not values.** The catalogue row names a secret; the routing layer resolves it from a secret store when it creates the pool. A table containing DSNs with passwords is a table you can never grant read access to, which means it can never be the operational tool it needs to be.

**An epoch, incremented on every move.** This is what makes cache invalidation deterministic instead of hopeful, and it's used below.

**The catalogue is on the request path**, so it is cached aggressively and needs a local fallback — a snapshot held in memory and on disk — so that a catalogue outage degrades to "routing as of five minutes ago" rather than "the entire fleet is down." Serving from a stale snapshot is only safe because of the fence from 5.3c; the two mechanisms are designed together.

**The catalogue does not live inside a tenant's database.** That sounds obvious written down and it happens anyway, usually by putting the tenants table in the pooled database — which works until the pooled database is the thing you're trying to route around.

### The routing path

```
  tenantId ─► placement cache ─► placement ─► pool registry ─► pool ─► connection
                   │ miss                          │ miss
                   ▼                               ▼
               catalogue                    create pool (bounded, lazy)
              (snapshot fallback)
```

### Pooling strategies, with the arithmetic

**A — one pool per tenant, in every instance.** The default, and the arithmetic that opens this article:

```
  connections = instances × tenants × pool_size
```

Workable when instances × tenants is in the tens. Mitigations that buy you an order of magnitude: `minimumIdle = 0`, aggressive idle timeouts, LRU eviction of entire pools, and a hard cap on pools per instance. None of them changes the shape of the formula.

**B — a shared pool, switched per checkout.** One pool against one database instance; set the schema or role on checkout and reset it on release. This is the mechanism behind schema-per-tenant from 5.1. It costs a round trip per checkout, it requires the reset discipline this module keeps returning to, and it doesn't apply to database-per-tenant at all — different databases need different connections, full stop.

**C — an external pooler in transaction mode.** PgBouncer, RDS Proxy, or equivalent between the application and the database. Application instances open client connections, which are cheap; the pooler multiplexes them onto a small number of real server connections. This is the standard answer once the numbers get large.

The constraint is significant and permanent: transaction-mode pooling forbids session-level state. No session-scoped `SET`, no advisory locks, no `LISTEN`/`NOTIFY`, no session temp tables, and historically constrained prepared statements. Note that 5.2a's insistence on `SET LOCAL` and transaction-scoped tenant context was not arbitrary — that choice is precisely what keeps this option available. Teams that used session-level settings for tenant context discover, years later, that the pooler they now need is incompatible with their isolation mechanism.

**D — reduce the multiplier with tenant affinity.** Route each tenant's requests to a subset of instances, so no instance holds pools for every tenant.

```
  without affinity:  30 instances × 500 tenants × 5  =  75,000 connections

  with affinity (each instance serves ~50 tenants):
                     30 instances ×  50 tenants × 5  =   7,500 connections
                     and behind a pooler, a few hundred server-side
```

This is 5.2b's shuffle sharding applied to routing rather than to workers, and it's how large fleets actually survive. The cost is that application instances stop being interchangeable: autoscaling, rolling deploys, and instance failure all become rebalancing events, and you need a routing layer that knows the tenant-to-instance map.

### Placement caching and the epoch

Cache the placement in-process, with a TTL for freshness and an epoch for correctness:

- The catalogue exposes a cheap current-epoch read, or publishes changes.
- A routing layer whose cached epoch is behind refreshes its placements.
- The TTL is a backstop, not the mechanism.

State the rule explicitly, because it's the one that keeps moves safe: **TTL expiry is for freshness; correctness comes from the fence.** A routing cache that is merely usually right is a data-loss mechanism unless the old placement refuses writes. With the fence, a stale route produces a retryable error. Without it, it produces a successful write into an abandoned database.

### Cold start

`minimumIdle = 0` is the right setting and it moves the cost to the first request per tenant per instance — connect, TLS, authenticate, set session state. Three things help:

- **Warm the top N tenants on boot**, and let the long tail be cold. Placement data tells you who they are.
- **Don't report ready until warm.** A readiness probe that passes before pools exist routes production traffic into connection setup.
- **Stagger the roll.** Twenty-four instances reconnecting simultaneously is a thundering herd against a database that is also serving traffic.

### Failure isolation per placement

This one deserves emphasis because it's where the isolation you paid for gets silently destroyed.

Tenant 8842's dedicated database goes down. That should be a single-tenant outage — it's the entire reason they have a dedicated database. But if every request thread that touches it blocks for a thirty-second connect timeout, the shared application thread pool fills with requests waiting on a database that isn't coming back, and every other tenant's requests queue behind them.

**You bought isolation at the storage layer and gave it back at the application layer.** The fix is per-placement circuit breakers and short connect timeouts, so a dead placement fails fast and locally (Module 36 for the mechanics, and 5.2b for why the shared pool is the thing being protected).

### Read replicas

Put the replica reference in the placement row, so read routing is a property of placement rather than a separate parallel system. Two consequences to plan for:

Not every placement has a replica — a small dedicated tenant may not justify one — so read routing must degrade to the primary rather than assume.

And read-your-writes breaks per tenant rather than globally: after a write, pin that tenant's reads to the primary for a few seconds. Doing it per tenant rather than per user is usually the right granularity, because a tenant's users see each other's writes.

### The same question for every other store

Routing isn't a database-only problem, and the common failure is four separate implementations that disagree during a move.

**Cache.** Shared instance with a key prefix is the default and is almost always right. Separate logical databases do not isolate memory and don't scale past a handful. A dedicated instance is worth it for a tenant whose working set evicts everyone else's — which is a measurement from 5.2b, not a guess.

**Search.** One index with a tenant filter is the default. Index-per-tenant is seductive and collapses at scale, because every index carries shards and every shard carries fixed overhead; a few thousand tenants becomes tens of thousands of shards and a cluster that spends its life on cluster-state management. The middle option people miss is **routing**: keep one index, and use the tenant id as the routing key so all of a tenant's documents live on one shard. Queries then hit one shard instead of all of them, which is both an isolation win and a large latency win.

**Object storage.** Prefix per tenant is the default. Bucket-per-tenant hits per-account bucket limits sooner than expected. Where isolation must be enforced rather than conventional, scoped temporary credentials restricted to one prefix are stronger than any application-level check.

**The unifying rule: one catalogue, all stores.** If the placement row knows where a tenant's database, cache, index, and bucket live, then a move updates one record and every subsystem follows. If each subsystem has its own answer, a move leaves one of them behind, and you find out which one when a customer's search results come back empty.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A catalogue and a `connectionFor()` seam: from the first tenant.** 5.1 made this case; it remains the cheapest insurance in the module.
- **An external transaction-mode pooler: before you launch a database-per-tenant tier**, not after. Retrofitting it means auditing the codebase for session-state usage under time pressure.
- **Pool-per-tenant with eviction: tens of dedicated placements.** Simple and adequate at that size.
- **Tenant affinity routing: hundreds to thousands of placements.** Below that you're trading statelessness for arithmetic you didn't need to fix.
- **Per-placement circuit breakers and short connect timeouts: as soon as a second placement exists.** Two placements is enough for one to take down the other.
- **Replica routing: when read load justifies it and you've decided the staleness policy**, in that order.
- **Dedicated cache or search resources for a tenant: when measurement shows they're evicting or crowding others** — the same trigger as moving them to their own database.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

**One pooled database.** `connectionFor()` returns the same pool for everyone, the catalogue is a table with one placement row, and that's the entire implementation. Build the seam, skip the machinery.

**A pooler in front of one application instance and one database.** You've added a process, a failure mode, and a set of protocol restrictions to solve arithmetic that currently reads 1 × 1 × 10.

**Affinity routing at twenty tenants.** Statelessness is worth a great deal operationally, and you'd be trading it for a connection count that fits comfortably already.

**Per-tenant cache instances because it sounds more isolated.** Measure eviction first. Most tenants' working sets are small enough that a shared instance isolates them fine, and dedicated instances multiply your operational surface by the tenant count.

**Index-per-tenant search for a large tenant population.** The shard arithmetic is unforgiving and the failure mode — a cluster destabilized by its own metadata — is much worse than slow queries.

**Replica routing before there's a replica**, or a staleness policy before anyone has asked what staleness is acceptable.

## Trade-offs

- **Every request now depends on the catalogue**, which makes it a component whose outage is total. The local snapshot fallback is mandatory rather than optional, and it introduces its own coherence questions.
- **Lazy pools trade cold-start latency for idle connections, and there's no setting that avoids both.** Pick which one your traffic shape can absorb, and revisit it when the deploy cadence changes.
- **Transaction-mode pooling permanently constrains what your code may use.** The constraint is invisible until someone adds an advisory lock or a session temp table, and then it's a mystery bug in one environment.
- **Affinity routing sacrifices statelessness.** Scaling events become rebalancing events, instance failure becomes a placement question, and your deploy strategy now interacts with your routing map.
- **Per-tenant pool metrics are a cardinality problem** — the same one from 5.1 and 5.2b. Aggregate by placement, keep a top-N tenant list, and resist the instinct to label everything by tenant.
- **Circuit breakers per placement mean configuration per placement**, and alert noise that scales with the number of dedicated tenants.
- **Replica routing introduces timing-dependent read-your-writes bugs** that reproduce only under load and only for tenants with replicas.
- **A multi-store catalogue is more state to keep consistent**, and a partially-applied placement change during a move leaves a tenant split across two locations — which is the failure the single catalogue was supposed to prevent, now concentrated in one transaction.
- **Connection limits have no graceful degradation.** Unlike CPU or memory, which degrade, a connection ceiling refuses. There's no warning band unless you build one, so alert on utilization rather than on failure.

## Alternatives

- **Stay pooled.** One database, one pool, no routing layer, none of this article. Still the best available answer when it's available.
- **Algorithmic sharding.** Hash the tenant id to a shard and skip the catalogue entirely. No lookup, no cache, no epoch — and no ability to move one tenant, promise a specific customer a specific location, or rebalance a hot shard without moving a population. Correct for systems with uniform tenants; wrong for systems that sell placement.
- **Connectionless database access.** Some managed platforms expose an HTTP or data-API interface where there are no persistent connections to pool, which erases the arithmetic at the top of this article completely. Worth checking before building a pool registry, and worth weighing against the per-query latency it adds.
- **A dedicated data-access tier.** A small number of processes hold all the pools; application instances call them over the network. The connection count becomes a function of that tier's size rather than your application fleet's. This is, structurally, what a pooler is — the difference is whether you own the logic inside it.
- **A deployment per tenant.** Routing becomes DNS again, and the process only knows one answer. The recurring theme of this module: the strongest version of every solution here is not having the problem, and the price of that is 5.1's operational bill.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — the seam.** Every data access starts with `connectionFor(tenantId)`, returning the same pool for everyone. No global handle exists anywhere.
*Signal you're behind:* a module-level `db` that any file can import.

**Level 1 — a real catalogue.** Placement and epoch in a table, read through a cache, with a local snapshot fallback and credentials held by reference.
*Signal:* placement lives in configuration, or a move requires a deploy.

**Level 2 — do the arithmetic before selling the tier.** Model instances × placements × pool size against `max_connections`, *including* the overlap during a rolling deploy. Write the number down.
*Signal:* you can't state your peak connection count.

**Level 3 — bound the registry.** `minimumIdle = 0`, LRU eviction of whole pools, a cap on pools per instance, short connect timeouts, and a circuit breaker per placement.
*Signal:* idle connections dominate, or one placement's outage affected other tenants.

**Level 4 — an external pooler**, plus an audit for session-state usage that would break under transaction mode.
*Signal:* connection utilization above roughly half, or a planned expansion of the dedicated tier.

**Level 5 — affinity routing, replica routing with an explicit staleness policy, and a catalogue covering every store.**
*Signal:* hundreds of placements, or a tenant move that left the search index behind.

## Implementation

TypeScript, because the interesting code here is caching and lifecycle rather than SQL, and both read clearly with async/await.

**The catalogue lookup: cached, epoch-checked, and degraded rather than down.**

```ts
class PlacementResolver {
  private cache = new Map<string, { placement: Placement; epoch: number }>();
  private snapshot: Map<string, Placement>;          // last known good, persisted
  private currentEpoch = 0;

  async resolve(tenantId: string): Promise<Placement> {
    const hit = this.cache.get(tenantId);
    if (hit && hit.epoch === this.currentEpoch) return hit.placement;

    try {
      const placement = await this.catalog.lookup(tenantId);
      this.cache.set(tenantId, { placement, epoch: this.currentEpoch });
      this.snapshot.set(tenantId, placement);
      return placement;
    } catch (err) {
      const stale = this.snapshot.get(tenantId);
      if (!stale) throw err;                          // never seen it: genuinely can't route
      metrics.increment("placement.served_stale");
      return stale;                                   // safe *because* of the fence
    }
  }
}
```

> **Serving stale placement is a deliberate choice that depends on another component.** If the old placement still accepts writes, this code silently routes writes into an abandoned database during a catalogue outage. If the fence from 5.3c is in place, the worst case is a loud error and a retry. The fallback and the fence are one design, and implementing either without the other is worse than implementing neither — because the fallback makes failures rarer without making them safer.
>
> **The epoch is checked on read, not pushed on write.** A background poller updates `currentEpoch` every few seconds; a mismatch invalidates every cached placement at once. This is deliberately coarse: a move is rare and re-resolving every tenant costs one catalogue read each, whereas targeted per-tenant invalidation requires reliable delivery to every instance, which is a much larger thing to get right.

**The pool registry: bounded, lazy, and drained rather than destroyed.**

```ts
class PoolRegistry {
  private pools = new Map<string, { pool: Pool; lastUsed: number }>();

  async get(placement: Placement): Promise<Pool> {
    const existing = this.pools.get(placement.id);
    if (existing) { existing.lastUsed = Date.now(); return existing.pool; }

    if (this.pools.size >= this.maxPools) await this.evictLeastRecentlyUsed();

    const credentials = await this.secrets.resolve(placement.credentialRef);
    const pool = new Pool({
      ...credentials,
      min: 0,                          // no idle connections for cold tenants
      max: placement.kind === "dedicated" ? 5 : 20,
      connectionTimeoutMillis: 2_000,  // fail fast; a dead placement must not block
      idleTimeoutMillis: 30_000,
    });

    this.pools.set(placement.id, { pool, lastUsed: Date.now() });
    return pool;
  }

  private async evictLeastRecentlyUsed() {
    const [id, entry] = [...this.pools].sort((a, b) => a[1].lastUsed - b[1].lastUsed)[0];
    this.pools.delete(id);
    await entry.pool.end();            // drains in-flight queries; does not kill them
  }
}
```

> **Eviction must drain, not terminate.** `pool.end()` waits for checked-out connections to be returned. Destroying a pool with queries in flight turns a capacity optimization into random query failures for whichever tenant was unlucky, which is a spectacularly confusing bug to diagnose because it correlates with *other* tenants' traffic.
>
> **`connectionTimeoutMillis` is the isolation control.** Two seconds means a dead placement produces fast errors that a circuit breaker can count. The default in most drivers is tens of seconds or unlimited, which means a dead placement produces blocked requests that consume application capacity — and the dedicated database that was supposed to contain one tenant's problems becomes the mechanism that spreads them.
>
> **Note the `max` differing by placement kind.** A dedicated tenant genuinely needs fewer connections than the pooled tier, and uniform pool sizing across placements is how the arithmetic at the top of this article gets out of hand. Small pools are also usually *faster* — a queue in front of a well-sized pool beats contention inside an oversized one.

**Tying it together.**

```ts
export async function connectionFor(
  tenantId: string,
  intent: "read" | "write" = "write",
): Promise<PoolClient> {
  const placement = await placements.resolve(tenantId);
  const useReplica = intent === "read"
    && placement.replicaRef
    && !recentWrites.has(tenantId);          // read-your-writes: pin after a write

  const pool = await registry.get(useReplica ? placement.replica() : placement);
  return breaker.for(placement.id).run(() => pool.connect());
}
```

Every data access in the system goes through this function, and `pool` is not exported anywhere. That's the same structural point 5.2a made about `withTenant`: a seam is only a seam if there is no way around it.

## Further Reading

- **PgBouncer documentation, particularly the pooling modes page and the feature-compatibility notes.** The definitive statement of what transaction-mode pooling forbids. Read it before you commit to session-level state anywhere, not after.
- **PostgreSQL documentation on `max_connections` and per-backend resource consumption**, plus the project wiki's pages on connection pooling. The source of the memory-per-connection reasoning that makes idle pools expensive.
- **HikariCP's "About Pool Sizing."** Short, counterintuitive, and correct: small pools outperform large ones, and the arithmetic for why is worth internalizing before you tune anything.
- **AWS RDS Proxy documentation**, or your platform's managed equivalent, for what a pooler looks like when someone else operates it — including which session features it pins connections for, which is where the performance surprises live.
- **Vitess's documentation on vindexes**, especially lookup vindexes versus hash vindexes. The clearest available treatment of directory-based versus algorithmic routing, written by people who had to support both because their users needed both.
- **Elasticsearch documentation on custom routing and on shard sizing.** The routing page is the technique most multi-tenant search implementations miss; the shard sizing guidance is why index-per-tenant fails.
- **Redis documentation on memory management and eviction policies**, and its notes on why multiple logical databases are not an isolation mechanism.
- **Your database platform's serverless or HTTP driver documentation**, if it has one. Worth a read purely to see how much of this article it makes unnecessary.

A **Java** sibling would close a loop this module opened. 5.1 showed Hibernate's multi-tenancy as a configuration flag and a `MultiTenantConnectionProvider` with a two-line `getConnection(tenantId)`; everything in this article is what lives behind those two lines, and the framework helps with none of it — no pool registry, no eviction, no epoch, no circuit breaker. The genuinely interesting part is the collision: Hibernate's session-per-request model holds a connection for the duration of a request and assumes session-scoped state is available, which is precisely what transaction-mode pooling forbids. Resolving that means either abandoning the pooler, restructuring transaction boundaries so connections are acquired late and released early, or accepting connection pinning and losing most of the pooler's benefit. Three unattractive options that the TypeScript version never has to confront, because nothing was managing the session on its behalf. Not yet written.

---

The habit from this article is to treat **`connectionFor(tenantId)` as an architecture rather than a function.** Behind that signature sit a lookup with an availability requirement, a cache with a correctness requirement, a pool with a capacity ceiling that refuses rather than degrades, and a failure boundary that determines whether one tenant's bad day stays theirs.

And for the module as a whole, the through-line has been the same question in seven different costumes: **where is this decision enforced, and what happens at the boundary?** In the query, the connection, or the topology (5.1). At the row, or everywhere the row also went (5.2a). In the pool, the scheduler, or the bill (5.2b). At creation, and again in reverse (5.3a). In the code, or in the registry (5.3b). Halfway through, for a week (5.3c). At the edge, and at every hop after it (5.4a). Multi-tenancy isn't one technique. It's the accumulated consequence of having decided, in each of those places, who the request belongs to — and the systems that stay comprehensible are the ones where each of those decisions was made deliberately, once, somewhere you can point to.
