# Technology Stack Selection Criteria

_Module 1.3 — SaaS Fundamentals & Business Models_

## The Problem

A three-person team is starting a document collaboration product. Before writing anything, they do the responsible thing and compare stacks. They read benchmark roundups, open a framework throughput comparison, and build a table:

```
                 req/s (plaintext)   memory idle   startup
  Go / Fiber          ~380,000          14 MB        4 ms
  Node / Fastify      ~180,000          48 MB       80 ms
  Java / Spring Boot  ~140,000         210 MB      2.4 s
```

Go wins by a wide margin, so they pick Go. It's a defensible decision, made with real data, by people acting in good faith.

Eighteen months later, here is what actually happened:

- Peak traffic is 40 requests per second. The benchmark measured a difference two orders of magnitude above anything they will experience this decade. It has never once mattered.
- The product needed real-time collaborative editing. The mature conflict-resolution libraries — the CRDT implementations with years of production use behind them — were all JavaScript. They wrote their own, badly, twice.
- The rich text document model has to exist in the browser anyway. Now it exists twice, in two languages, and the two versions disagree in edge cases that produce corrupted documents.
- Hiring took five months, because their city has a lot of Node and Java developers and very few Go developers who also want to work on text editors.

Meanwhile, the three properties of Go they *did* benefit from — 12 MB containers, four-millisecond startup, a single binary they could hand to their first on-premise customer — appeared nowhere in their evaluation. They got those by luck.

So the decision failed twice over. The criteria they used didn't predict anything, and the criteria that would have predicted the outcome weren't on the table. Both halves of that are normal. Stack selection is one of the few architectural decisions where the most-discussed inputs and the most-consequential inputs barely overlap.

## What It Is

Choosing a stack is not a ranking exercise. There is no ordering of languages that's true independent of a situation, and every comparison that produces one has smuggled in assumptions about what you're building.

It's better understood as **constraint satisfaction**: most of the criteria that matter are *disqualifiers* rather than scores. A language either has a maintained, production-grade library for the thing your product fundamentally does, or it doesn't. Your team either knows it, or they'll spend six months finding out. These are binary. You find the candidates that aren't disqualified, and among those you pick by whatever's cheapest — which, most of the time, is the one people already know.

Worth separating four things that get bundled into the phrase "choosing a language," because the consequences live in different ones:

- **The language** — syntax, type system, memory model. What people argue about.
- **The runtime** — how it executes concurrency, how much memory it wants, how long it takes to start. Where the real technical differences are.
- **The framework** — Spring, NestJS, Gin. Often a bigger commitment than the language, and much harder to leave.
- **The ecosystem** — available libraries, dependency culture, hiring pool, how a typical codebase in it is structured. Where most of the cost and most of the benefit actually sit.

You pick the first and inherit the other three. Recognising that reorders the questions you should be asking.

## Recognizing the Problem

Signals that a stack decision is being made on the wrong axis:

- Your comparison document has a benchmark table and no sentence about your team.
- You can't name one library your product needs that exists in only some of the candidates.
- The decision is being made by someone who won't maintain the result.
- The justification is "it's what we'd want at scale," and you have no users.
- You're comparing throughput and you don't know your current request rate.
- Nobody has checked what's hireable in the market you can actually hire from.
- A working prototype already exists in a language the team is quietly embarrassed by.
- Someone came back from a conference with the answer.
- You now have three languages, one on-call rotation, and one person who understands the third.
- Somebody proposed a rewrite to fix a problem nobody has traced to the language.

## How It Works

The criteria, ordered by how often they actually decide the outcome. This ordering is roughly the inverse of how much airtime each one gets.

### Tier 1: usually decisive

**Team fluency.** What the people who will build and operate this already know. Getting a competent engineer to *productive* in a new language takes a few weeks; getting them to *idiomatic* — writing code the next person can maintain, knowing which library is the real one, recognising the ecosystem's traps — takes six months to a year. That cost lands precisely during the period when velocity matters most, and it lands on every person you add.

This isn't a concession to laziness. Fluency is worth more than any property in Tier 2 for almost every system operating below serious scale, and "the team knows it" is a legitimate technical argument that should win most of the time.

**Ecosystem fit for your specific domain.** The disqualifier test, and the one the team in the opening skipped. Before evaluating anything, write down the five to ten things your product fundamentally requires — not "a web framework," but the specific hard ones. Then check each candidate for a maintained, production-proven library. Not a promising GitHub project with 400 stars and a last commit in 2023. A real one.

Examples of requirements that decide this outright:

| Requirement | What it implies |
|---|---|
| Share a domain model between browser and server | TypeScript, effectively without competition |
| Kafka with schema registry, exactly-once semantics | The JVM ecosystem is years ahead here |
| Ship a binary into a customer's environment | Go, or accept packaging a runtime |
| Heavy numerical work, embeddings, model training | Python-adjacent, or a service boundary to it |
| SAML/SCIM enterprise SSO, deep IdP compatibility | JVM and .NET have the most complete libraries |
| Cold-start-sensitive serverless | Go or Node; the JVM needs GraalVM and its costs |
| Complex multi-step transactional workflows | JVM's transaction tooling is unusually mature |

**Hiring pool, in the market you can hire from.** Not globally — in your city, or your timezone band, or your remote policy's reach, at your salary. The general availability of a language says little about whether you can find three of them who also want to work on your problem.

### Tier 2: decisive under specific, nameable pressures

**The concurrency model — specifically, what a blocking call costs.** This is the one genuine technical difference in this whole article, and it's not about throughput.

In a **thread-per-request** runtime (the JVM, and Go's goroutines in a different shape), a slow or CPU-heavy operation occupies its own thread. Other requests keep being served on other threads. Under enough load you exhaust the pool and *degrade* — latency rises across the board, gradually.

In an **event-loop** runtime (Node), everything shares one thread per process. An operation that doesn't yield — a synchronous CPU task, a tight loop, a large JSON parse — blocks every other in-flight request in that process until it completes. You don't degrade gradually; you *stall*, and the stall appears on endpoints with no relationship to the code that caused it.

Neither model is better. They fail differently, and the useful question is which failure you'd rather diagnose at 2am. The Implementation section below shows the event-loop case concretely, because it's the one that surprises people.

One piece of folklore worth retiring: "the JVM can't handle high concurrency because threads are expensive." That was true and it drove a decade of reactive programming. Virtual threads landed in JDK 21 (2023), and blocking a virtual thread is now cheap. A large amount of reactive code in the wild was adopted to solve a problem that no longer exists, and it's now paying for that solution in stack traces nobody can read.

**Startup time and memory footprint.** Irrelevant if you run a fixed fleet of long-lived instances. Decisive if you're on serverless where cold starts hit user-facing latency, or if you autoscale aggressively, or if you run many small services and the per-instance floor multiplies. A 200 MB JVM baseline across forty services is 8 GB of memory spent before any work happens. GraalVM native image and CRaC close most of this gap, at the cost of build complexity and reflection configuration.

**Type safety under team scale.** All three candidates have static types, but they're not the same guarantee. TypeScript's types are **erased at runtime** — they constrain your code and validate nothing arriving from outside it. A handler that types `req.body` as an object with three fields and then trusts it is lying, and the compiler will not tell you. Runtime boundary validation is mandatory in TypeScript rather than optional. Java and Go enforce their types by construction, which shows up most in large codebases with turnover, where the question "if I change this, what breaks" is answered by the compiler rather than by a test suite you hope is complete.

**Deployment shape.** How much has to be true about the target machine. A Go binary requires nothing. Node requires a runtime and `node_modules`. The JVM requires a JVM. This barely matters if you deploy containers to your own cluster, and matters enormously the day an enterprise customer asks to run it in their environment — the BYOC and on-premise models from Module 1.1 are much cheaper to support from a single-binary starting point.

### Tier 3: endlessly argued, rarely decisive

**Raw throughput.** Do the arithmetic from Module 1.2 before engaging with a benchmark. A million requests a day is twelve per second. All three candidates handle that on the smallest instance any cloud will sell you, with the machine mostly asleep. The gap between them opens up at a scale most B2B products never reach, and even then it usually opens up somewhere other than the language — in a query plan, an N+1, a missing index, a cache you don't have.

Throughput becomes a real criterion in two cases: when compute is a dominant share of your cost of goods sold, so efficiency is a margin decision; and when latency is itself a product feature, as in ad bidding or trading. If neither is true, the benchmark table is entertainment.

### What the three are actually good at

With that ordering in mind, the honest version of the per-language summary.

**Java and the JVM.** The real strength isn't the language, it's ecosystem depth in exactly the places enterprise SaaS lives: transaction management, connection pooling, Kafka and the surrounding data infrastructure, enterprise identity protocols, batch processing, observability. "Mature" here isn't a vibe — in those specific niches the JVM libraries are measurably more complete, and you'll find the edge case you hit has already been handled.

Spring is better understood as an application platform than a framework. That buys enormous leverage — an extraordinary amount of correct behaviour arrives configured — in exchange for a lot of implicit machinery, which is wonderful until something misbehaves and the stack trace is sixty frames of proxy. The costs are memory footprint, startup time, and an ecosystem whose default answer to any problem is another framework.

**Node and TypeScript.** The real strength is one language across browser and server, and the argument for that is structural rather than a matter of convenience. It lets you share validation schemas, API contract types, and domain logic between client and server as *actual shared code* rather than as two implementations you keep in sync by discipline. For any product whose browser side is substantial, that's a genuine architectural advantage no other option offers.

Beyond that: npm's breadth for anything web or integration adjacent, and the shortest path from idea to running thing of the three. The costs are the event-loop stall described above, erased types requiring real boundary validation, and a dependency culture that pulls in transitive trees deep enough to be a standing supply-chain and maintenance concern.

**Go.** The real strength is operational simplicity, and it's underrated because it's boring. A static binary, a container measured in megabytes, millisecond startup, no runtime on the target machine, and a memory footprint that lets you pack services densely. If you run many services, deploy into environments you don't control, or care about cold starts, that's a structural advantage rather than a preference.

Goroutines give you cheap concurrency while writing straight-line blocking code, and `context` propagation for cancellation and deadlines is a first-class idiom that most ecosystems bolt on afterwards. The language is deliberately small, so new hires are productive in weeks and codebases read similarly across teams — a real team-scaling property.

The costs come from the same smallness: more code for the same work, an ecosystem that thins out quickly outside infrastructure and networking (document generation, complex ORMs, data science, enterprise integrations), and error handling verbose enough that people either accept it or spend years not accepting it.

### The map is not the territory

Three candidates is the scope of this module, not the scope of reality. **C#/.NET** is a genuine peer of the JVM with a stronger story on Windows-heavy enterprises. **Python** is the default anywhere machine learning is the product rather than a feature. **Elixir** on the BEAM is unusually good at soft-realtime systems with many stateful connections. **Rust** wins where a memory-safety guarantee or the absence of GC pauses is a hard requirement. **Kotlin** gets you the JVM ecosystem without the ceremony. If your disqualifier list points at one of these, follow it.

### The cost of language number two

Every additional language in production multiplies a fixed set of things: CI pipelines, base images, dependency scanning, observability instrumentation, deployment tooling, the internal libraries for auth and logging and tenant context, the pool of people who can review a PR, and the pool who can debug it on call at 3am.

The rule that follows: **a second language should be justified by a disqualifier in the first, not a preference.** "Go would be nicer for this service" is not a reason. "This service ships into customer environments and we can't package a JVM there" is. And when you do add one, put the boundary at a network protocol and keep it to an entire deployable unit — a service is a reasonable language boundary, a module inside a service is not.

## When It's Appropriate

A deliberate, effortful selection earns its cost when:

- **You're genuinely greenfield with no incumbent skill** — a new company hiring from scratch. Rare, and the one case where the full exercise is obviously worth it.
- **You're standing up a new service at a clean network boundary**, which is the only place a second language can enter cheaply.
- **A requirement looks like a disqualifier.** Browser code sharing, on-premise binary delivery, heavy numerics, hard latency floors. Check this before anything else; it can end the discussion in an afternoon.
- **The current stack is failing against a named, measured pressure.** Cold starts dominating a serverless bill. CPU work stalling the event loop after you've already offloaded what's offloadable. Compute costs eating margin at a scale where that's real. Named and measured, not felt.
- **You're hiring aggressively and the market is fighting you.** A stack you can't staff is a business problem regardless of its technical merits.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **You have a team, they know a language, and it isn't disqualified.** The decision takes an hour, not a quarter. Write down the disqualifier check so the reasoning survives, and move on.
- **Rewriting a working system for language properties.** Rewrites consume everything and the language is rarely the actual cause. Trace the pain first — most of the time it lands on a data model, a missing boundary, or an architecture decision, all of which survive a rewrite and greet you cheerfully in the new language.
- **Choosing for a scale you don't have.** Adopting the stack you'd want at a million users costs you velocity now, in exchange for a benefit that's conditional on surviving long enough to need it.
- **Introducing a second language for one service because it's nicer.** See the multiplication above.
- **Benchmark-driven selection two orders of magnitude below where the differences appear.** Which is nearly everyone.

## Trade-offs

What a stack choice costs you once you've made it:

- **It's a permanent hiring constraint.** Every future hire is filtered by it, and the filter tightens as your codebase becomes more idiomatic.
- **You inherit the ecosystem's culture, including its characteristic failures.** npm's dependency depth, Spring's implicit machinery, Go's tendency to reimplement rather than adopt. These aren't bugs you can opt out of; they're how a typical codebase in that ecosystem ends up looking.
- **You've placed a bet on the ecosystem's future.** Libraries get abandoned, major versions break compatibility, licences change. Python 2→3, Angular 1→2, and Oracle's JDK licensing shifts were all multi-year taxes paid by people who made a reasonable decision years earlier.
- **The choice shapes your architecture more than expected.** Node pushes CPU work out into separate services. The JVM pushes you toward frameworks and dependency injection. Go pushes you toward explicit wiring and hand-rolled infrastructure. Six months in, your system looks like the language's idioms whether or not you decided that.
- **Time spent choosing is time not spent learning whether the product matters.** For a decision that's usually predetermined by team fluency anyway, a long evaluation has a poor expected value.
- **Consistency has a price too.** Standardising on one language is right most of the time, and it taxes the one case per year that genuinely needed something else. Enforcing it there is a real cost, not a free win — just usually a smaller cost than the alternative.

## Alternatives

Other ways to make this decision:

- **Use what the team knows.** The default, correct most of the time, and improved at almost no cost by spending one hour on the disqualifier check first. That hour is the difference between a good decision and a lucky one.
- **Let the deployment platform decide.** If you've committed to edge functions, a specific serverless platform, or a BaaS, the supported runtimes are a short list and the choice is largely made. You've traded the decision away, which is fine if the platform is the bigger commitment.
- **Polyglot by service boundary.** Standardise on one, allow exceptions at whole-service boundaries with a written justification. Gets you escape hatches; costs you the multiplication described above, so the tax has to be paid before the first exception, not after.
- **Spike both against your hardest requirement.** One week, two implementations of the thing you're least sure about. Converts an unfalsifiable argument into an observation, which is the same move Module 1.2 recommends everywhere. Cheap relative to the decision's lifespan.
- **Follow your vertical's dominant choice.** Fintech leans JVM, developer infrastructure leans Go, media and real-time lean Node. This is a crude signal and a surprisingly good one, because it's a proxy for where the domain libraries and the domain-experienced hires already are.

## How to Achieve It

**1. Write the disqualifier list before looking at any language.** Five to ten things your product fundamentally must do, stated concretely enough to check. "Real-time collaborative editing with conflict resolution," not "good performance." *Signal you skipped this:* your evaluation document leads with a benchmark.

**2. Check each candidate against the list. Binary, not scored.** A maintained production library exists or it doesn't. Resist converting this into a weighted matrix — weighting lets a strong score in an irrelevant column outvote a hard requirement, which is exactly how the opening scenario happened.

**3. Among the survivors, pick by team fluency and stop.** If one candidate survives and your team knows it, you're finished. Write down the disqualifier list and the reasoning as an ADR (Module 2.5) so that when the assumptions change, someone can tell.

**4. If genuinely tied, spike for a week.** Build the hardest requirement in both. Time it, and note which one you'd rather debug. *Signal you're here:* two survivors, comparable fluency, and the argument has run more than a day.

**5. Before adding language number two, pay the platform tax first.** A service template, a CI pipeline, a base image, observability instrumentation, and ports of your internal auth, logging and tenant-context libraries. If you can't afford to build those, you can't afford the language — and discovering that before the first service exists is much cheaper than after. *Signal you skipped this:* the second-language service has no dashboards and one person who can deploy it.

## Implementation

One property in this article changes how you structure a system rather than just how you write code, and it's worth seeing concretely. This is TypeScript on Node.

```ts
app.get("/invoices/:id/pdf", async (req, res) => {
  const invoice = await loadInvoice(req.params.id);  // I/O — yields the loop
  const pdf = renderPdfSync(invoice);                // 200ms of CPU — does not
  res.type("application/pdf").send(pdf);
});
```

The `await` yields control back to the event loop, so other requests progress while the database responds. The render doesn't. For those 200 milliseconds the process runs nothing else — not a queued login, not a health check, not an unrelated request that arrived a microsecond ago.

```
  single event loop, one process

  A  /invoices/9/pdf  ──►  I/O 40ms  ──►  renderPdfSync ███████████████  200ms
  B  /login               queued at t=5ms   │                         │
  C  /clinics             queued at t=6ms   │  both wait the whole time
                                            └─────────────────────────┘

  B and C each report ~235ms of latency for work that takes 3ms to perform.
```

The damage is **non-local**, and that's what makes it hard to diagnose. Your dashboard shows a latency spike on `/login`, so you go and look at the login code, where you find nothing wrong, because there is nothing wrong with it. The cause is an endpoint in a different module doing something that never yields.

The fix is structural rather than a matter of writing better code — move the CPU off the loop:

```ts
const pdf = await pdfPool.run(invoice);  // worker_threads pool, or a separate service
```

Which is the actual point. The runtime's concurrency model pushed a piece of architecture on you: in this ecosystem, CPU-bound work wants to live outside the request-handling process, and that's a decision you make on day one or discover on a bad afternoon.

Compare the shape of the failure elsewhere — in prose, because the code would look identical and teach nothing. In a thread-per-request runtime, the same 200ms render occupies one thread and everything else proceeds normally. Under sustained load you exhaust the pool and latency rises across the board, gradually and proportionally. That's a nicer failure to be paged for, and it's also a failure that hides longer before you notice it. Different trade, not a free one.

## Further Reading

- **Node.js documentation, "Don't Block the Event Loop."** The official treatment of the failure above, including the less obvious blockers — large `JSON.parse`, some regex patterns, synchronous crypto.
- **JEP 444: Virtual Threads (JDK 21).** Read this before repeating anything you've heard about JVM concurrency. Early versions pinned virtual threads inside `synchronized` blocks, which later releases addressed; worth knowing which JDK you're actually on.
- **Rob Pike, "Concurrency Is Not Parallelism" (2012)** and the Go blog's **"Go Concurrency Patterns: Context."** The first corrects the most common confusion about what goroutines buy you; the second explains the cancellation idiom that shapes most Go service code.
- **Dan McKinley, "Choose Boring Technology" (2015).** The innovation-token framing, and the best short argument for why your budget of novel technologies is much smaller than it feels.
- **Fred Brooks, "No Silver Bullet" (1986).** Essential versus accidental complexity. Language choice addresses only the accidental kind, which is why it can't rescue a product whose difficulty is in the domain.
- **TechEmpower Framework Benchmarks.** Worth reading specifically to learn what they do and don't measure — the methodology notes are more educational than the numbers, and they'll immunise you against the table in this article's opening.
- **Sam Newman, _Building Microservices_**, on technology heterogeneity. The clearest accounting of what the second language actually costs at an organisational level.
- **GraalVM Native Image documentation.** If JVM startup or footprint is one of your disqualifiers, read this before concluding that it is.
- **Stack Overflow Developer Survey** and the **JetBrains State of the Developer Ecosystem.** Crude proxies for the hiring pool, and better than the intuition you'd otherwise use.

A Java sibling to the Implementation section would teach something this one can't. The same overload under virtual threads doesn't stall — it surfaces as carrier-thread pinning and memory pressure instead, and the diagnostic path is entirely different: JFR events rather than event-loop lag metrics. Showing the same overloaded PDF endpoint on a virtual-thread executor, and what its failure looks like on a dashboard, would make the "different failure, not no failure" point concrete rather than asserted. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying out of this one: when someone recommends a technology, ask which constraint of yours it satisfies that the incumbent doesn't. If the answer is a property rather than a constraint — "it's faster," "it's type-safe," "it's modern" — the conversation is about the machine but the decision is about the team. That's usually fine. It's only a problem when nobody says so out loud.
