# Architectural Principles for SaaS

_Module 1.2 — SaaS Fundamentals & Business Models_

## The Problem

Two engineers are reviewing a design for a report-export feature in a clinic scheduling product. Exports take a while; the question is whether to generate them inline or hand them to a queue.

> **Dana:** We should push this to a background worker. Doing it inline won't scale.
>
> **Priya:** A queue adds a moving part. Simplicity is a principle too — and we'd be adding operational burden for a feature five clinics use.

Both sentences are true. Neither is decidable. "Won't scale" has no number attached, so there is no observation that could prove it wrong; "adds operational burden" has no number either. The disagreement can't be settled by evidence, so it gets settled some other way: by seniority, by whoever has more stamina in the meeting, or by whichever option sounds more like what a serious company would do.

Say the queue wins. Six months later:

- Exports are still five clinics, a few times a week. Throughput was never the constraint.
- The at-least-once delivery semantics produced duplicate exports, so someone added an idempotency key.
- There's a dead-letter queue. Nobody has looked at it since March; it has 340 messages in it.
- Debugging a failed export now means correlating a request ID across two systems and a broker.

Nothing here was incompetent. Every individual decision followed from the last. The root cause is upstream: the original argument was conducted in a vocabulary that couldn't distinguish a good idea from a bad one.

And there's a second problem underneath, which shows up as soon as you try to take the principles seriously rather than rhetorically. They pull against each other. Every availability mechanism costs money. Every security control costs latency, developer velocity, or both. Every scaling mechanism costs debuggability. You cannot maximise "scalable," "available," "secure," and "cheap" at once, because three of them are paid for with the fourth. A system that is maximally secure is unusably slow. A system that is maximally cheap is one server with no backups.

So the real work isn't adopting the principles. It's converting them into something you can argue about with evidence, and then choosing a point in a space where every direction costs something.

## What It Is

The four principles you'll see in every SaaS architecture document — **scalability, availability and fault tolerance, security, cost efficiency** — are not goals. They are *directions with prices.* On their own, none of them is a design input, because "more of this is better" is true of all four and tells you nothing about how much to buy.

What makes them usable is a conversion. Each vague principle has a measurable form, and the measurable form has three parts: **a number, the pressure that justifies that number, and the budget you'll spend to hit it.**

| Principle | Vague form | Measurable form |
|---|---|---|
| Scalability | "it should scale" | what breaks first, at what load, and whether adding resources fixes it |
| Availability | "highly available" | an SLO, an error budget, and RTO/RPO for recovery |
| Security | "security first" | a threat model: who the adversary is, what they want, what the blast radius is |
| Cost | "cost-efficient" | cost per tenant, per request, or infrastructure as a share of revenue |

The conversion is the whole technique. Everything else in this article is either how to perform it, or what happens when you skip it.

One neighbour worth separating right now, because conflating them causes more bad architecture than any other confusion here: **scalability is not performance.** Performance is how fast one request is under today's load. Scalability is how the system's behaviour changes as load grows, and whether throwing resources at it helps. These are independent. A single-process in-memory service can be extremely fast and completely unscalable. A system that spreads work across fifty nodes can be perfectly scalable and slower per request than the version it replaced — because coordination isn't free. When someone says "this won't scale" but means "this is slow," the fix they'll propose is usually wrong for the problem they actually have.

## Recognizing the Problem

You're making architectural decisions without the conversion if:

- A design document contains the word "scalable" and no number.
- Nobody can say what your uptime was last month, or what it's supposed to be.
- Nobody can say what the system costs per customer, and the infrastructure bill is a monthly surprise.
- The answer to "why is it built this way" is "best practice" or "that's how [large company] does it."
- Two engineers disagree, and the tiebreaker is who's more senior.
- You have a multi-AZ deployment and have never actually failed over to the other one.
- Security review happens at the end, as a checklist, by someone who wasn't in the design discussions.
- You know your P99 latency but can't name a customer-visible thing that depends on it.
- Someone optimised something without measuring it first, and the change shipped anyway.
- A decision made under old assumptions is still in force, and nobody remembers what the assumptions were.

The last one is the quietest and the most expensive. Designs don't announce their own expiry.

## How It Works

The mechanism is the conversion. Here's what it looks like for each principle, including the part everyone skips: what the number costs you.

### Scalability: find the constraint, then check the arithmetic

Scaling is never general. A system doesn't fail to scale — one specific thing saturates first: CPU, memory, database connections, a lock, a single writer, disk IOPS, an external API's rate limit. Until you can name that thing, "making it scale" has no target and any work you do is a guess.

The second half is arithmetic, and it deflates most of these conversations:

```
  1,000,000 requests/day  ÷  86,400 s   ≈   12 req/s  average
  with a 5× daily peak                  ≈   60 req/s  peak
```

A million requests a day sounds like a lot. It's 12 requests per second. A single modest server running an ordinary web framework handles that with most of its capacity idle, and a single Postgres instance serves tens of thousands of simple indexed reads per second. Meanwhile 100 req/s — a number that feels small in a design meeting — is 8.6 million requests per day, which is more traffic than the large majority of B2B products will ever see.

Do this arithmetic before proposing any scaling mechanism. It frequently ends the discussion.

When it doesn't, the distinction that matters is **scaling up** (a bigger machine) versus **scaling out** (more machines). Up is dramatically simpler and goes much further than people expect; the ceiling is real but high, and you reach it with a credit card rather than a rearchitecture. Out is what you need past that ceiling, and it has a hard precondition: the thing being scaled has to be stateless, because if a request can only be handled correctly by the one machine holding its session, adding machines doesn't help. That precondition is the single most useful early design constraint in this whole article, because honouring it is nearly free today and retrofitting it isn't.

*What it costs:* horizontal scaling turns local calls into network calls, and network calls fail in ways function calls don't — partially, slowly, ambiguously. Every scale-out step trades debuggability for headroom.

### Availability: nines, error budgets, and the multiplication problem

"Highly available" becomes measurable as an SLO: a target, over a window, on a specific measurement. And a target is easier to reason about once you convert the percentage into time you're allowed to be down:

| Target | Unavailable per 30 days | What it implies |
|---|---|---|
| 99% | 7 hours 12 min | A person can notice, investigate, fix it |
| 99.9% | 43 min | A person needs to be paged and respond quickly |
| 99.99% | 4 min 19 s | No human is in the loop; recovery must be automatic |
| 99.999% | 26 s | Failure must be invisible; every dependency is redundant |

That table is the cost of a nine stated honestly. Going from 99.9% to 99.99% isn't "a bit more reliable," it's a different operational model: automated failover, tested regularly, because at four minutes nobody can open a dashboard and decide anything.

The complement of the target is the **error budget** — if you promise 99.9%, you have 43 minutes a month to spend. That reframe is what makes the number useful rather than aspirational. It turns "don't break things" into a quantity, which means shipping a risky change is a purchase rather than a sin, and running out of budget is a signal to slow down rather than a moral failure.

Separate two neighbours here: **availability** is whether the system responds now, **durability** is whether the data is still there afterwards, **reliability** is the broader property of behaving correctly over time. They have different mechanisms. Replication buys availability; backups buy durability; a replica is not a backup, because it faithfully replicates your `DELETE` too.

Then there's the arithmetic that surprises people. Dependencies in series multiply:

```
  client ──► CDN ──► API ──► auth service ──► database
            99.99%   99.95%     99.9%          99.95%

  composite  ≈ 99.79%   →   about 1h 31m unavailable per month
```

Every component you add in the request path multiplies its unavailability into the total. A service that's "three nines" contributes three-nines-worth of failure to everything that depends on it, which means a system of four well-built components is meaningfully worse than any one of them. This is why the availability conversation and the microservices conversation are the same conversation, and why "extract it into a service" is never free.

You buy availability back with redundancy — but only redundancy you have actually exercised. A standby you've never failed over to is not a standby, it's an untested assumption with a monthly bill. The relevant numbers are **RTO** (how long recovery takes) and **RPO** (how much data you're willing to lose), and both are fiction until someone has run the drill.

*What it costs:* money, roughly linearly with nines; complexity, because every redundancy mechanism is a new failure mode; and sometimes consistency. That last one is where CAP actually applies, and it's narrower than the folklore: during a network partition, a distributed system has to choose between remaining available and remaining consistent. It is not a menu you pick two items from on a normal Tuesday.

### Security: identify the decisions that are irreversible

"Security first" invites eye-rolling because it's usually stated as zeal rather than reasoning. The reasoning is specific: **a small number of security decisions are structurally hard to reverse, and almost all the others are cheap to add later.** "First" means identifying which ones are in the first category.

The irreversible ones tend to be:

- Where authentication and identity live, and what a principal is.
- Where authorization is enforced — in each handler, or in one chokepoint. (In a multi-tenant system this is *the* decision; see Module 1.1 and Module 5.)
- Whether secrets were ever in the repository. Rotating is easy; un-publishing history isn't.
- Whether you have an audit log at all. You can't retroactively record what happened last year.
- What the blast radius of one compromised component is — which is set by your trust boundaries, and those are set by your architecture.

The reversible ones — rate limiting, security headers, dependency scanning, CSP, MFA — are genuinely fine to add in month eight. They're additive.

The measurable form is a **threat model**, and it's less ceremonious than it sounds: who might attack this, what are they after, how would they get it, and what happens if they succeed. Three honest paragraphs beat a forty-page document nobody reads. The value is mostly in noticing that your adversary is probably a curious authenticated customer or a leaked API key, not a nation-state — which changes what's worth building.

*What it costs:* latency on every request that carries a check, developer velocity on every workflow that requires approval, and a permanent tension with usability. Any security control that makes the legitimate path annoying enough gets routed around by the people it's protecting.

### Cost: infrastructure is a margin decision, and so is your salary

In SaaS, infrastructure is cost of goods sold — it recurs for as long as the customer does, so it comes directly out of gross margin. The measurable forms are **cost per tenant**, **cost per request**, and **infrastructure as a percentage of revenue**. Any of them is a large improvement over a total bill nobody has decomposed, because a total tells you the number is big but not which query made it big.

The reframe that changes engineering behaviour: a report that runs nightly for every tenant and takes 90 seconds isn't a slow report, it's a recurring monthly charge that grows with sales.

But the honest version of this principle points the other way at small scale. Engineer time usually dominates the bill until quite late. Two weeks of work to cut a $200/month bill in half is a clear loss — you spent several thousand dollars of salary to save $1,200 a year, and you added whatever complexity the optimisation required. The rule is boring and effective: compare the annual saving to the fully-loaded cost of the person doing the saving.

*What it costs:* cost optimisation trades headroom for efficiency. A right-sized system has no slack, and slack is what absorbs the traffic spike you didn't forecast. Aggressive cost work also tends to push toward tighter coupling and more shared infrastructure, which is the noisy-neighbour problem arriving by a different road.

### The four conflict, and that's the actual design work

| Buying more… | Is paid for with… |
|---|---|
| Availability | Money, and complexity that creates new failure modes |
| Scalability | Debuggability, operational surface, sometimes latency |
| Security | Latency, developer velocity, usability |
| Low cost | Headroom, isolation, redundancy |

There is no arrangement that maximises all four. What you're actually doing when you "design an architecture" is choosing a point in this space, for a specific set of pressures, at a specific moment.

Which means the output of the work is not a diagram. It's a decision plus the numbers that justified it plus the load it assumed — so that when one of those numbers changes by an order of magnitude, someone can tell that the design point has expired. That's what Architecture Decision Records (Module 2.5) are for, and it's why the format insists on recording context and consequences rather than just the choice.

## When It's Appropriate

Doing this explicitly — real targets, written down, with the reasoning attached — earns its cost when:

- **The decision is expensive to reverse.** Data model, tenancy strategy, authentication, storage engine, the public shape of an API. These are one-way doors and deserve the ceremony.
- **A disagreement is blocking work.** The conversion exists precisely to make an argument decidable. If two people are stuck, converting both positions into numbers usually dissolves it in an afternoon.
- **You have customers with expectations**, especially written ones. The moment availability appears in a contract, it needs a measurement definition, because otherwise you and your customer will discover you had different ones during an incident.
- **The system already failed in a way you don't want repeated.** The cheapest time to set a target is right after an outage, when everyone agrees it matters.
- **A load-bearing number moved 10×.** Users, request rate, data volume, tenant count, largest customer. The old design point was correct for the old number.
- **Someone external is asking.** Enterprise security questionnaires, SOC 2, and procurement reviews all want the written version, and assembling it retroactively is worse than having it.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Before product-market fit.** For a system with twelve users and an unvalidated premise, the correct architecture is the one you can throw away. Formal targets on software that may not exist in six months are a tax on learning.
- **Internal tools with a handful of known users.** An SLO for the deploy dashboard used by four engineers who sit near each other is theatre. When it breaks, they'll say so.
- **Multi-region before single-region is boring.** If you can't reliably operate one region, a second one doubles your failure modes and gives you a new class of problem (data consistency across regions) in exchange for protection against a failure you haven't yet earned the right to worry about.
- **Threat-modelling a system with no sensitive data and no untrusted users.** Spend the hour somewhere it changes a decision.
- **Any process heavier than the decision it governs.** A design document for a two-week feature that touches one service is a tell that the process is being applied for its own sake.

The general failure mode is **designing for the load you hope to have.** It's seductive because it feels responsible and because the hoped-for numbers are the ones in the pitch deck. But complexity you adopt today is paid for today, in velocity, while the load that justifies it is hypothetical. Plenty of companies have built carefully for a million users and then failed to reach four hundred, partly because building for a million is slow.

The defence isn't "never think ahead." It's asking which preparations are cheap now and expensive later — stateless services, a tenant column, a correlation ID on every request, structured logs — and doing only those. Everything else waits for the pressure to be real.

## Trade-offs

Once you have explicit targets and written decisions, they cost you things:

- **A published target is a commitment**, and the first instinct on missing it is to argue with the measurement rather than fix the system. Expect this, and decide in advance who's allowed to change the definition.
- **Numbers get optimised instead of outcomes.** Measure availability at the load balancer and you can hold a green dashboard while every checkout fails, because the load balancer is answering fine. The SLI is the whole ballgame; a target attached to the wrong measurement is worse than no target, because it's confidently wrong.
- **It slows early decisions.** Time spent deciding correctly is time not spent finding out whether the feature matters. For reversible decisions, building and learning usually beats deliberating.
- **Written decisions calcify.** An ADR gets cited two years later as authority, by someone who wasn't there, whose situation no longer matches the one in the document. Recording the assumptions is what makes this survivable — a decision with its premises attached can be shown to have expired.
- **False precision.** "99.95%" implies you can distinguish it from 99.9%. On 10,000 requests a month, the gap between those targets is five requests; the number is noise wearing a suit.
- **Unmeasured properties degrade.** This is the one that bites hardest. If latency has a target and security doesn't, controls get quietly removed to hit the target, and nobody records that a trade happened. Whatever you measure will be defended at the expense of whatever you don't.

## Alternatives

Other ways to make architectural decisions, and what each one gets you:

- **Adopt a reference architecture.** The framework's defaults, the Twelve-Factor App, the AWS Well-Architected Framework. Fast, uncontroversial, usually adequate — these encode a lot of hard-won experience. What you give up is fit: you inherit assumptions you can't see, and reference architectures are generally written for larger systems than yours.
- **Copy a company you admire.** Proven at *their* scale, under *their* constraints, with *their* team. This is the actual mechanism of cargo-cult architecture, and it's rarely a decision to copy a design so much as a decision not to examine one. The tell is that you can describe what they built but not what it cost them.
- **Prototype and measure.** Build both, load-test, pick. Strictly better than arguing whenever it's cheap, and it converts an unfalsifiable claim into an observation in a day. Not always cheap, and load tests lie in specific ways (uniform traffic, warm caches, synthetic data with no skew).
- **Defer and refactor when it hurts.** Build the simple thing; let real pain choose the next move. Excellent for reversible decisions and catastrophic for irreversible ones — which makes reversibility the variable that decides between this approach and the formal one. Amazon's framing of one-way versus two-way doors is the useful version.
- **Buy expertise.** A consultant or an experienced hire who has seen this failure before. Fast and often correct; the risk is that you get their previous employer's architecture, which lands you back at the second bullet.

Most teams should use the deferral approach for nearly everything and the formal conversion for the short list of one-way doors. The mistake is picking one mode and applying it everywhere.

## How to Achieve It

For a running system with none of this, in order. Each step has a signal telling you to take the next one.

**1. Write down what exists.** One page: what the system does, what its dependencies are, what breaks it, what it costs per month. No targets yet, no aspirations, just the current state. *Signal you need this:* nobody can answer "what happens if the database goes away" without a meeting.

**2. Measure one thing that maps to a customer experience.** Not CPU. Not the health check. One SLI on one journey that a customer would notice — logins that succeed, exports that complete, pages that render under a second. *Signal to move on:* you have the page, but arguments are still being settled by opinion.

**3. Set one target, with a budget and a consequence.** Pick the journey from step 2, choose a number you can defend, and agree in advance what happens when you're out of budget. One SLO that people actually respond to beats twelve that decorate a wiki. *Signal to move on:* the target is holding, and people are referring to it in decisions.

**4. Record decisions as they're made, with their numbers.** ADRs, three paragraphs each: what we chose, what the situation was, what we gave up. The number that justified it is the important part and the one that gets left out. *Signal to move on:* somebody cited a decision whose reasoning nobody could reconstruct.

**5. Give design points an explicit expiry.** Attach the assumed load to the decision — "this holds to roughly 500 req/s and 200GB" — and revisit when a real number approaches it. *Signal you needed this already:* you found a component sized for a load you passed a year ago.

Stop whenever the next step stops corresponding to real pain. This ladder has no prize at the top.

## Implementation

The most consequential code in this whole article is the code that decides whether the system is up. This is Go, but the idea is language-independent.

Here's the health check almost every service has:

```go
func health(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
    w.WriteHeader(http.StatusOK)
}
```

This returns 200 as long as the process is alive. It will keep returning 200 while the database is unreachable, the job queue is four hours behind, and every single checkout is failing. It's not useless — it's a fine liveness probe, and that's exactly what it should be used for — but it is not a measure of whether the service is doing its job, and a great many uptime dashboards are built on it.

An SLI measures real requests against a definition of "good" that you have to choose and defend:

```go
func measure(journey string, next http.Handler) http.Handler {
    return http.HandlerFunc(func(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
        start := time.Now()
        rec := &recorder{ResponseWriter: w, status: 200}

        next.ServeHTTP(rec, r)

        // "Good" = it worked, and it worked fast enough to be useful.
        good := rec.status < 500 && time.Since(start) < 400*time.Millisecond

        outcome := "bad"
        if good {
            outcome = "good"
        }
        requests.WithLabelValues(journey, outcome).Inc()
    })
}
```

Availability is then `good / (good + bad)` over the window, and the error budget for 99.9% across 30 days is one request in a thousand.

Two choices in that one boolean are worth arguing about, because they're where most SLIs go wrong:

**The latency clause.** Including it means a response that succeeds after eleven seconds counts as a failure, which matches how the customer experienced it. Leaving it out is how a service holds 99.99% availability while users describe it as unusable. If you take one thing from this section: a slow success is not a success.

**The `< 500` clause.** Excluding 4xx is conventional — a client sending malformed input isn't your outage. But it's also the standard way to hide a bad API. If a confusing endpoint causes half your integrators to send 400-producing requests, your SLI will look immaculate while your support queue fills up. Worth watching 4xx rates separately rather than letting the exclusion make them invisible.

Note what the middleware doesn't do: it doesn't decide anything about redundancy, regions, or replication. It just makes the availability conversation empirical. That's the entire move this article is about — the mechanism that turns "are we reliable?" from an opinion into a number you can disagree with.

## Further Reading

- **Google, _Site Reliability Engineering_ — "Embracing Risk" and "Service Level Objectives."** The origin of error budgets, and still the clearest explanation of why a reliability target below 100% is the correct engineering choice rather than a concession. Free online.
- **Google, _The Site Reliability Workbook_ — "Implementing SLOs."** The practical companion: choosing SLIs, picking windows, and the failure modes of measuring the wrong thing.
- **Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, chapter 1.** Reliability, scalability and maintainability treated as design goals, plus the best short explanation of why percentiles and not averages.
- **Kleppmann, "Please stop calling databases CP or AP" (2015).** Short, and corrects the version of CAP that circulates in design meetings.
- **Nygard, _Release It!_** Stability patterns and, more importantly, a catalogue of how production systems actually fail. Nygard's 2011 post "Documenting Architecture Decisions" is also the origin of the ADR format.
- **AWS Well-Architected Framework.** Six pillars, four of which are this article. Best used as a prompt list — a set of questions worth having been asked — rather than as a design method, since it has no opinion about your specific pressures.
- **Shostack, _Threat Modeling: Designing for Security_**, or the OWASP Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet for the fast version. Both make the point that the exercise is about noticing, not about documentation.
- **Amazon, 2015 shareholder letter (Jeff Bezos).** The one-way/two-way door distinction, stated in a page. It's the most useful single heuristic for deciding how much of this article to apply to a given decision.
- **Andreessen Horowitz, "The Cost of Cloud, a Trillion Dollar Paradox" (2021).** Contentious in its conclusions, but it made infrastructure-as-COGS a mainstream engineering concern, which is the frame the cost section above depends on.

A Java sibling for the Implementation section would show something the Go version hides. Spring Boot Actuator ships health endpoints, readiness and liveness probes, and Micrometer instrumentation out of the box, which means the lying-health-check problem arrives pre-installed and enabled — the framework has already made the choice this article says you should make deliberately. Walking through what Actuator measures by default, and which of those defaults correspond to a customer experience (almost none), would be a genuinely different article rather than a translation. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: **every architectural claim should be falsifiable.** When you read or write one, look for the two things that make it so — at what load, and paid for with what. A sentence that survives the removal of both was never an argument, and you'll now notice how many of them are.
