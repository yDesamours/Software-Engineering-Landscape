# What Makes a SaaS Application?

_Module 1.1 — SaaS Fundamentals & Business Models_

## The Problem

You've built an invoicing tool. It works well enough that four companies want it.

The traditional way to get it to them is to ship it: cut a release, produce an installer, hand it over. Each company runs it on hardware they control, against a database they control, configured the way they like.

Then the bug report arrives. "Tax totals are wrong on line items that have a discount."

Before you can start debugging, you need answers to questions that have nothing to do with tax:

- Which version are they on? (You've cut six.)
- Which OS, which database, which version of that database?
- Did they change the rounding mode from the default?
- Is this the customer running a patched build, because in March they needed a custom CSV export and you gave them a branch?

You eventually reproduce it, fix it, and cut 2.4.0. Now the fix has to travel. Release notes. A download link. Their change-management process. A maintenance window that their one part-time ops person has to schedule. Six months later, two of your four customers are still on the broken version, and the ticket is still open because you cannot verify that the bug is gone.

Meanwhile you are supporting four versions at once, and every new feature has to work on all of them.

Now change exactly one thing: instead of shipping the software to the customer, **you run it yourself.** One deployment, one version, one database — yours. Customers reach it over the network.

The same bug report arrives. You open the logs, find the request, see the rounding path, fix the code, deploy. Ten minutes later every customer on earth has the fix. Nobody scheduled anything. Nobody downloaded anything. There is no "which version."

That is an enormous improvement, and it is the whole reason this delivery model took over. But you did not get it for free — you traded. Look at what became true the moment you took over operations:

- Every customer's data now lives in your database, rows away from every other customer's data.
- Your downtime is their downtime. They have no local copy to fall back on.
- You are no longer paid once, at purchase. You are paid continuously, which means the software itself has to know who is currently entitled to what.
- You can now see everything: who logged in, what they clicked, who stopped coming back.

Each of those is a design consequence, and together they are most of what people mean when they say "SaaS architecture." The rest of this article is that bill, itemized.

## What It Is

This is Software as a Service: **the vendor operates the software and sells continuing access to it over a network, rather than selling copies of it.**

That's the whole definition, and it's worth separating from three things it gets confused with:

- **A web app is not automatically SaaS.** Your company's internal expense tool is a web app. There is no vendor, no customers, no subscription, no tenancy. None of the pressures in this article apply to it.
- **Running in the cloud is not SaaS.** Where you host it is your problem, not the customer's, and it says nothing about the commercial relationship.
- **Multi-tenancy is not SaaS**, though the words get used interchangeably. Multi-tenancy is the most common *implementation* of "one operator serving many customers." It is the leading answer, not the question. You can run a SaaS business with one fully isolated deployment per customer; you will just pay more per customer to do it.

One term to fix now, because everything else depends on it. A **tenant** is a customer organization, not a user. Acme Corp is a tenant; the fourteen people at Acme who log in are users. The tenant is the thing that has a subscription, a plan, a set of data, and its own administrators. Almost every design decision in this article is really a question about where tenants sit in your system.

## Recognizing the Problem

Signals that you are in this territory, whether or not you've called it that:

- Every new table needs an `organization_id` / `account_id` / `workspace_id` column, and you've started adding it reflexively.
- You have — or are about to have — a git branch named after a customer.
- A support question can't be answered until someone asks "which plan are they on?"
- Deploying affects everyone at once, and you've started to feel that before you hit the button.
- A customer asked to export all of their data and you wrote a one-off script.
- A customer stopped paying, and nobody could say what should happen to their data, or when.
- Someone asked how many accounts actually use the reports feature and the honest answer was that you'd have to guess.
- One customer's nightly bulk import makes the app slow for everybody.
- You're maintaining a config flag whose only purpose is to make one specific customer behave differently.

The last two are the ones that tend to escalate fastest: one is a shared-resource problem, the other is the beginning of a combinatorial explosion.

## How It Works

Four structural consequences follow from "one operator, many customers, continuing payment." Each one shows up as a concrete thing in your codebase.

### 1. Tenancy enters the data model

In shipped software, "which customer is this?" is answered by which machine the code is running on. There is no ambiguity, and no code needs to express it. When one deployment serves everyone, that question moves inside the process, and it has to be answered on every single request before any business question can be answered.

```
POST /invoices
      │
      ▼
┌──────────────┐   who is asking?        user:   u_88
│ authenticate │ ───────────────────►    tenant: acme
└──────┬───────┘
       ▼
┌──────────────┐   what may they do?     plan:     team
│ entitlement  │ ───────────────────►    features: bulk_export ✗
└──────┬───────┘
       ▼
┌──────────────┐   business logic runs here — and by this point
│   handler    │   "which tenant" is no longer a question it asks
└──────┬───────┘
       ▼
┌──────────────┐   every query carries tenant = acme, enforced in
│ data access  │   one place rather than remembered in 200 places
└──────────────┘
```

*How* tenant data is separated is a genuine architectural decision with several real answers — a shared schema with a tenant column on every table, a schema per tenant, a database per tenant, or a hybrid. Module 5 is entirely about that choice. What matters here is knowing the decision exists, that it is one of the hardest things to change later, and that the cheap hedge (put the tenant column in from the beginning) costs almost nothing today.

### 2. Money becomes live state inside the system

With shipped software, payment happens once, outside the code. A license key is about as deep as it gets. With SaaS, the subscription is a live object with a lifecycle, and your application has to react to every transition in it:

```
        signup
          │
          ▼
      ┌────────┐  converts   ┌────────┐  payment fails  ┌──────────┐
      │ trial  │────────────►│ active │────────────────►│ past_due │
      └───┬────┘             └───┬────┘◄────────────────└────┬─────┘
          │ expires              │ cancels  retry succeeds   │ dunning
          │                      │                           │ exhausted
          │                      ▼                           │
          │                 ┌──────────┐                     │
          └────────────────►│ canceled │◄────────────────────┘
                            └────┬─────┘
                                 │ retention window ends
                                 ▼
                            ┌─────────┐
                            │ purged  │
                            └─────────┘
```

Every arrow is a product decision that lands in your authorization layer. What does a `past_due` tenant see — full access, read-only, or a paywall? Does a `canceled` tenant keep read access to their history during the retention window? How long is that window, and who deletes the data at the end of it? You get to answer each question once, in code, instead of a hundred times in email.

The pricing model is not a marketing detail either; it determines what your system is obliged to measure.

- **Per-seat** means you must be able to count active seats accurately at any moment, and decide what happens when the fifteenth person is invited to a fourteen-seat plan.
- **Usage-based** puts metering on the critical path. The counter has to be durable and accurate enough to put on an invoice, which is a much higher bar than "accurate enough for a dashboard."
- **Tiered/feature-gated** means every gated feature needs an entitlement check, and those checks multiply.
- **Freemium** means your infrastructure cost scales with a population that pays you nothing, which makes per-user cost an engineering constraint rather than a finance one.

There's even an HTTP status code for the moment this all surfaces: `402 Payment Required`, which sat unused for twenty years and is now genuinely useful.

### 3. Operations becomes part of the product

The customer is not buying a binary. They are buying the fact that it is up, at 9am, on the day payroll runs. That turns several things that used to be someone else's job into features you own:

- **Availability.** Their tolerance for your downtime is low and their alternatives during it are zero.
- **Migrations without downtime.** You can't take everyone offline to alter a table, so schema changes become multi-step, backward-compatible sequences.
- **Gradual rollout.** Since one deploy reaches everyone, "ship to 2% first" becomes a safety mechanism rather than a nicety. This is where feature flags and canary releases come from.
- **Backups and recovery.** Their data is your responsibility, including the part where you prove you can restore it.

### 4. The feedback loop closes

When you operate the software, you can see how it's used. That's why SaaS comes with a vocabulary of metrics — not because the industry enjoys acronyms, but because recurring revenue changes the central question from "did we sell it?" to "are they still here next month?"

- **MRR / ARR** — monthly and annual recurring revenue. Contracted, not collected.
- **Churn** — the rate customers leave. Worth splitting: *logo churn* counts departed customers, *revenue churn* counts departed dollars. Losing ten tiny accounts and one large one are very different events that produce the same logo-churn number.
- **Net revenue retention** — what last year's cohort is worth today, including upgrades. Above 100% means existing customers grow faster than they leave, which is the closest thing to a load-bearing number in this whole list.
- **CAC** — cost to acquire a customer. **LTV** — total revenue expected from one before they leave. The ratio between them, and the months it takes to pay CAC back, is what determines whether growth is a good idea or a slow leak.

The engineering-relevant part: these are all lagging indicators. By the time churn moves, the cause is months old. The leading indicators live in product usage, which is why event tracking (Module 16) exists at all, and why "how many accounts used this feature last week" should be a query rather than a guess.

## When It's Appropriate

The delivery model fits well when:

- **The value accumulates in the data over time.** CRMs, analytics, monitoring, issue trackers. The longer a customer uses it the more it's worth to them, and the export is never as useful as the live system.
- **The customer doesn't want to run infrastructure.** For most companies under a few hundred people, "someone has to patch this server" is a real cost that easily exceeds your subscription price.
- **The software must change often because the world does.** Anything touching tax rules, payment rails, social platform APIs, or model providers. Shipped software in these domains is stale within a quarter.
- **Cross-customer data makes the product better.** Fraud scoring, spam detection, benchmarking. These are strictly impossible in a shipped model.
- **Per-customer marginal cost is small relative to price.** A $200/month tenant costing you $6/month in infrastructure leaves room for everything else.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **The customer legally or politically cannot hand you the data.** Defence, parts of healthcare and finance, several governments, anything with hard data-residency rules. Here the on-premise or customer-hosted model isn't a legacy choice, it's the only one.
- **There is exactly one customer, and there will be one.** Internal tools, a bespoke system for a single client. Multi-tenancy for one tenant is pure cost with no payoff.
- **The work is local and heavy.** Video editing, CAD, IDEs, large local datasets. The round trip is the product's worst feature. Hybrid models exist, but the compute stays near the user.
- **Low price, high per-user cost.** A $5/month product doing heavy inference per request is a business problem disguised as an architecture problem, and no amount of caching fixes a negative gross margin.

The most common overkill inside an otherwise-correct decision is **building the tenancy machinery before the second tenant exists**: tenant-resolution middleware, a per-tenant config service, a plan-entitlement framework, an admin impersonation console, all in month one, for one customer on a pilot.

But notice the asymmetry, because it's what makes this trap subtle. Retrofitting a tenant column into a schema that never had one is genuinely expensive: every table, every query, every index, every cached key, and a backfill on live data. Building a plan-entitlement framework later is cheap by comparison; it's mostly new code in new places. So the right hedge is narrow. Put the tenant identifier in the data model on day one and enforce it in one place. Leave everything else until a real second customer creates the pressure.

## Trade-offs

Nothing here is free once you have it.

- **You own operations forever.** The product is never "shipped." There's a pager. A release is now an event with a blast radius rather than an artifact on a download page.
- **That blast radius is everyone.** One bad deploy, one bad migration, one missing `WHERE` clause, and it's all customers at once. A cross-tenant data leak is not a bug, it's a disclosure event with legal consequences and an existential effect on trust.
- **Noisy neighbours.** Shared infrastructure means one customer's behaviour degrades another customer's latency. Nobody had this problem when each install had its own machine, and now it's a permanent category of incident.
- **Revenue is fragile and continuous.** A perpetual license is money in the bank; a subscription is money you have to keep earning every month. Cash comes in slowly, and it can leave at the speed of a cancel button.
- **Cost of goods sold is now an engineering number.** Infrastructure scales with usage, so gross margin depends on your query plans. An inefficient report you'd never have optimised in shipped software is a recurring line item here.
- **You are the data custodian.** GDPR deletion requests, breach notification, SOC 2, audit logs, data-residency commitments. These stop being legal's problem and turn into engineering backlog.
- **Customisation pressure never goes away — it changes shape.** In the shipped world it produced version sprawl: 2.3.1 at one customer, 1.9 at another. In SaaS you can't fork, so it comes back as plan tiers, feature flags, and per-tenant config. That's better, but it's not free: you've replaced a handful of versions with a combinatorial matrix of states, and every combination is theoretically reachable in production while only a few are ever tested.

That last point is the one worth internalising. The single-version promise from the Problem section is real, but it buys you less than it first appears. The diversity moved; it didn't vanish.

## Alternatives

Same problem — getting software into a customer's hands and getting paid — solved differently:

- **Perpetual license, on-premise.** The customer installs and operates it. You gain large up-front payments and zero operational burden; you give up the ability to fix anything quickly, and you take on version sprawl.
- **Single-tenant managed hosting.** One deployment per customer, operated by you. Isolation is strong and the "noisy neighbour" and data-leak categories mostly disappear. What you give up is leverage: a schema migration now runs N times, and a fleet of 300 deployments needs real automation before it needs anything else. This is commonly sold as the enterprise tier of a company that is otherwise multi-tenant.
- **Open core / self-host plus paid support.** GitLab, Sentry and Elastic all work this way. Adoption is frictionless and you reach customers who'd never send you their data, but monetisation is harder and you're back to supporting versions you don't control.
- **BYOC (bring your own cloud).** The control plane is yours; the data plane runs in the customer's cloud account, so their data never leaves it. Increasingly common for data-heavy and regulated products. It answers the residency objection directly, at the cost of operating software inside environments you can't fully see.
- **API-first / usage-billed services.** Arguably a SaaS variant with no UI. The same tenancy, entitlement and metering problems apply, often with metering mattering more than everything else.

Most successful companies end up running two of these at once. The mistake is running two of them from the same branch.

## How to Achieve It

There's a well-worn ladder here, and the useful part is that each rung has a signal telling you when to climb it. (The original four-level version of this is Carraro and Chong's 2006 paper, listed below.)

```
  L0  a fork per customer            ▲  isolation, blast-radius safety,
  L1  one codebase, one deploy/tenant│  per-tenant cost, migration pain
  L2  shared app tier, DB per tenant │
  L3  shared app tier, shared schema ▼  operational leverage, margin,
                                        ease of changing everything at once
```

**L0 → L1: kill the customer-specific branches.** One codebase; differences expressed as configuration. This is the highest-value step on the ladder and the one people skip because it feels like refactoring rather than progress. *Signal you need it:* a bug fix has to be applied more than once, or you've asked "which branch is that customer on?"

**L1 → L2: share the application tier, keep a database per tenant.** Compute is pooled, data stays physically separate. Isolation arguments with enterprise buyers get easy. *Signal:* your per-tenant infrastructure bill grows linearly with customers and you're provisioning servers for tenants with four users.

**L2 → L3: share the schema, discriminate by tenant.** Cheapest per tenant, simplest to operate, and every data-access path now has to be correct about tenancy or it leaks. *Signal:* running a migration across all tenant databases has become a multi-day project, or you're onboarding faster than provisioning can keep up.

You don't have to arrive at L3, and plenty of successful companies deliberately stop at L2 or run L2 and L3 side by side — shared schema for self-serve customers, dedicated databases for the enterprise tier that pays for it.

Billing has its own ladder, and it's shorter: **manual invoicing** (a spreadsheet and the Stripe dashboard) → **hosted checkout plus webhooks** (the provider owns the subscription state machine; you react to it) → **in-app plan management** (upgrade, downgrade, proration, self-serve cancel) → **metered usage** (you produce the numbers the invoice is built from). *Signal to move up:* a human is in the loop on a transition that now happens weekly.

When both ladders are in play, climb whichever one's manual version is currently costing you the most. It is almost never both at once.

## Implementation

The single most consequential piece of code in a multi-tenant system is the thing that decides which rows a request can see. Everything else is ordinary application code. This is TypeScript, but the shape is the same anywhere.

Resolve the tenant once, at the edge, into a context object that travels with the request:

```ts
type TenantContext = {
  tenantId: string;
  plan: "free" | "team" | "enterprise";
  seatsPurchased: number;
  status: "trialing" | "active" | "past_due" | "canceled";
};
```

Now here is the bug that defines this entire category of system:

```ts
// A perfectly ordinary-looking query.
async function listOverdueInvoices(db: Db) {
  return db.query(
    `select * from invoices where status = 'overdue' order by due_date`
  );
}
```

Nothing about this is wrong in a single-customer application. In a multi-tenant one it returns every customer's overdue invoices to whoever asked.

What makes it dangerous is how quiet it is. There is no exception, no slow query, no failing assertion — just a correct-looking result set that is too large. It will pass every test you write, because your test database has one tenant in it, and with one tenant the unfiltered query returns exactly the right answer. The highest-severity bug class in SaaS is produced by *forgetting to type something*, and the only reliable defence is making it impossible to forget.

So don't rely on remembering the filter. Make the unfiltered handle unavailable:

```ts
// Handlers receive this. They never see the raw `db`.
function forTenant(db: Db, ctx: TenantContext) {
  return {
    invoices: {
      findOverdue: () =>
        db.query(
          `select * from invoices
            where tenant_id = $1 and status = 'overdue'
            order by due_date`,
          [ctx.tenantId]
        ),
    },
  };
}
```

The improvement isn't the `WHERE` clause; it's that `db` is out of scope in handler code. Discipline doesn't survive a growing team and a deadline. Module boundaries and types do.

For anything holding data that would be genuinely damaging to leak, push the same rule down into the database, where it's enforced even if the application layer is wrong:

```sql
alter table invoices enable row level security;

create policy tenant_isolation on invoices
  using (tenant_id = current_setting('app.tenant_id')::uuid);
```

with `set local app.tenant_id = ...` issued at the start of each transaction. Postgres will then refuse to return the wrong rows regardless of what the query says. It isn't free — pooled connections need care so a setting doesn't leak between requests, migrations and superuser roles bypass policies, and the planner has extra work on every query — but it converts a silent correctness failure into an empty result set, which is a much better failure to have.

Notice that the same context object carries the plan and seat count. That's not incidental: in this model, "which rows may you see" and "which features have you paid for" are two questions asked at the same moment, about the same subject, and they belong together.

## Further Reading

- **Carraro & Chong, "Architecture Strategies for Catching the Long Tail" (Microsoft, 2006).** The origin of the SaaS maturity levels used above. Dated in its examples and still the clearest statement of the ladder.
- **Azure Architecture Center — "Architect multitenant solutions on Azure."** The most complete free treatment of tenancy models, noisy-neighbour mitigation, and per-tenant cost allocation. Vendor-flavoured but the concepts transfer.
- **AWS SaaS Factory — "SaaS Tenant Isolation Strategies" (whitepaper).** Goes deeper specifically on isolation enforcement: where it lives, and how to test that it holds.
- **PostgreSQL documentation — Row Security Policies.** Read this before adopting RLS, particularly the interaction with connection pooling and with roles that bypass policies.
- **Stripe Billing documentation — subscription lifecycle and dunning.** Even if you never use Stripe, the state machine they've settled on is a good model of the transitions your own system will need to handle.
- **David Skok, "SaaS Metrics 2.0" (forEntrepreneurs).** The canonical treatment of MRR, churn, CAC and LTV, and why the ratios matter more than the absolute numbers.
- **Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, chapter 1.** Reliability, scalability and maintainability as design goals — the frame that makes "operations is part of the product" concrete.
- **The Twelve-Factor App**, factors I (codebase) and III (config). A short, opinionated statement of the L0 → L1 step above.

A Java sibling to this article would be worth writing, and would not be a translation. Hibernate's `@TenantId` and `CurrentTenantIdentifierResolver` move tenant scoping into the persistence layer itself, so the "forgot the `WHERE` clause" failure mode is largely structural rather than something you defend against by hand — which is a genuinely different answer to the same problem, not the same answer in different syntax. It also brings its own failure modes around connection provisioning and second-level caching that the TypeScript version never has to think about. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying out of this article is smaller than the topic: **every query in a real system has an implicit scope, and the question is always where that scope is enforced.** Here it happened to be the tenant. Elsewhere it's the current user, a region, a time window, a permission level. When the scope lives in the developer's memory, it eventually goes missing. When it lives in a type, a module boundary, or the database, it doesn't.
