# When Red Stops Meaning Anything: Flaky Tests and the Suite in CI

_Module 21.5 — Testing Strategies_

## The Problem

An online grocer runs its delivery platform from one TypeScript repository: an API, a web app, and background workers. The pipeline runs about 5,200 tests on every pull request and every merge to `main`:

- 4,300 Vitest tests, most of them small.
- 700 API tests against Postgres in containers (21.3a).
- 200 Playwright journeys (21.3b).

A full run takes 26 minutes. Over one month:

- **29% of runs on `main` fail**, and nine in ten of those pass when re-run with no change.
- **Engineers press "Re-run failed jobs" about 150 times a week.** At 26 minutes a run, that's around 65 hours of waiting every week.
- **A broken migration turned `main` red at 09:40 on a Tuesday.** Nobody looked until 14:50. `main` had been red most mornings that month, and three people assumed it was the usual noise. For five hours, every merge landed on top of the broken migration.

A tech lead adds `retries: 2` to both the Vitest and Playwright configurations. Red runs on `main` drop to 4%. The re-run button goes quiet, and everyone is relieved.

Six weeks later, operations reports that **410 baskets were picked twice** over three Saturdays. The duplicates were thrown away at the end of each day, because chilled food can't go back on the shelf.

The cause was a race in order confirmation. When a customer pays, two things tell the API about it: the payment provider's webhook, and the customer's browser returning from the payment page. Both call `confirmPaid`, which reads the order, sees that it's still pending, marks it paid, and creates a picking task. When the two calls arrive a few milliseconds apart, which happens most often at the Saturday peak, both read "pending" and both create a task.

There was a test for exactly this: "webhook and redirect together confirm the order once." Since the day it was written, it had failed about one run in twenty. Two people had called it flaky. With two retries, it reports a failure only when all three attempts fail:

```
                  chance the test reports a failure
no retries        1 in 20        ████████████████████
retries: 1        1 in 400       █
retries: 2        1 in 8,000     ▏
```

When someone finally went through 90 days of results, 63 tests had both failed and passed on the same commit. The causes:

| Cause | Tests |
|---|---|
| Fixed sleeps, and promises nobody awaited | 21 |
| Time: fixtures for "tomorrow" broke between 23:00 and 00:00 UTC in summer, when London is already on the next day | 12 |
| Order dependence: a module-level slot cache that no test reset | 9 |
| Shared resources: a fixed port, one shared test customer | 8 |
| Timeouts on 2-vCPU runners running 4 browser workers | 7 |
| A real postcode-lookup API called from tests | 4 |
| **Real races in the product** | **2** |

Most of them failed about one run in 200. No single test was bad enough to worry anyone, but together they were enough:

```
0.995 ^ 60  ≈  0.74      about one run in four fails, and no one test is to blame
```

Retries made the build green by hiding all 63 in the same way. The two that were real bugs disappeared along with the rest.

## What It Is

A **flaky test** is one that can pass and fail on the same code, with the same configuration. "Flaky" names what you observed, not what caused it. Martin Fowler's word for the cause is **non-determinism**, and it can live in two places:

- **In the test or its environment:** a sleep, a fixture that depends on today's date, a shared port, a slow runner. The product is fine, and the test can't tell.
- **In the product:** a race, a missing lock, an ordering assumption. The test is doing its job, and the product only misbehaves sometimes. This is *flaky code*, and it's a bug.

The two look identical in a CI report. This article is about telling them apart, handling each one correctly, and running the suite in CI so that a red result keeps meaning something. The pipeline mechanics (stages, sharding, reports, what happens when `main` is red) are included because that's where flakiness is felt. Pipeline design in general belongs to Module 30.

Two neighbors are easy to confuse with flaky tests. A **broken** test fails every time, which makes it easy to find. A **brittle** test fails when someone makes a change that preserves behavior (21.2b). It's deterministic, just coupled to the wrong things.

## Recognizing the Problem

- "Re-run failed jobs" is a normal step in merging.
- Failures that nobody can reproduce locally.
- Tests that fail only at certain hours, on certain dates, or at month end.
- Tests that pass alone and fail in the full run, or the other way around.
- Failures that increase with more parallel workers or on smaller runners.
- A global retry setting, especially one that applies to small tests.
- A red `main` that nobody reacts to within the hour.
- `skip` annotations with "flaky" in the comment, older than a quarter.
- Merges made on red with an admin override.

## How It Works

### Where non-determinism comes from

Studies of real flaky-test fixes agree on the top three causes: waiting on asynchronous work, concurrency, and dependence on test order (Luo et al., 2014). The full list is short enough to check against:

| Source | What you see | Usual fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Waiting.** Fixed sleeps, promises nobody awaited, polling with a timeout | Timeouts, "element not found," assertions that run too early | Wait for a condition, not a duration. Await everything, and lint for floating promises. |
| **Time.** `new Date()`, time zones, DST, month and year ends | Failures at certain hours or dates | Inject the clock (21.2a). Pin `TZ` in CI, and also run once in an unusual zone on purpose. |
| **Randomness and ordering.** Random data, `Set` and map iteration, rows returned without `ORDER BY` | Assertions about order fail occasionally | Assert on sets, or sort first. Seed random generators and print the seed. |
| **Shared state.** Module-level caches, singletons, changed environment variables, leftover rows | Passes alone, fails in the suite (or the reverse) | Reset per test, or don't share. Run in random order to find dependencies. |
| **Shared resources.** Fixed ports, one shared account, a shared database or temp file | Fails only in parallel runs | Resources per worker: port `0`, a database per worker, data per test (21.3a). |
| **Resource contention.** CI runners smaller than laptops | Timeouts that rise with load | Match worker count to cores. Set timeouts for medium and large tests on CI hardware, not on laptops. |
| **The outside world.** Real third-party APIs, DNS, package registries | Fails when they do | Stub at your own adapter (21.2b). Use a registry mirror. |
| **Infrastructure startup.** Containers that aren't ready yet | "Connection refused" at the start of a run | Wait for readiness, not for an open port (21.3a). |
| **The product itself.** Check-then-act, missing locks, lost updates | A behavioral assertion fails occasionally | This isn't a flaky test. Fix the code. |

The last row is why the rest of the table matters. Every test problem you fix makes a product problem easier to notice.

### Flaky test or flaky code?

Triage each new flake before deciding what to do with it:

```
 test failed, then passed, on the same commit
                     │
                     ▼
 1. reproduce it: repeat 50–200×, retries off,
    also with more workers, less CPU, random order
                     │
                     ▼
 2. read the failing assertion
     ├── about scaffolding (timeout, setup, fixture, port) ──▶ test problem
     └── about behavior (two tasks, wrong total, lost update)
                     │
                     ▼
 3. could production reach that state?
    (two requests at once, a slow dependency, a retry)
     ├── yes ──▶ product bug: force the interleaving in a test, then fix the code
     └── no  ──▶ test problem: the test creates a situation production can't
```

**Step 1 matters most.** A flake you can reproduce 3 times in 100 is a problem you can work on. A flake you've seen once is a rumor. Running under stress, with more parallelism, fewer cores, or a CPU hog such as `stress-ng` on the runner, often turns a 1-in-200 failure into a 1-in-10 failure.

**Step 3 is where the grocer went wrong.** A webhook and a redirect arriving at the same moment isn't a situation a test invents. It's what the Saturday peak does.

When the answer is "product bug," the most useful next step is a test that **forces** the bad interleaving instead of waiting for it. Pause both callers after they've read and before either has written, then let them continue. A 1-in-20 failure becomes 20-in-20, and it stops being a flakiness question. The Implementation shows how.

### Retries: what they buy and what they hide

A retry answers one question: did this test pass at least once? That's a reasonable question for a large test, whose failures can come from an environment you don't control. It's the wrong question for a small test, which has no environment to blame.

A defensible retry policy has three parts:

- **Retries only for large tests** (browser journeys, deployed smoke tests). Small and medium tests get none. A small test that needs a retry is telling you about a sleep, a clock, or shared state.
- **A pass after a retry is recorded as flaky, not as a pass.** Playwright does this by default. Maven Surefire's `rerunFailingTestsCount` writes `flakyFailure` entries into its XML report. The Gradle test-retry plugin can be set to fail the build when a test passes only after a retry.
- **Flaky results are counted, and someone owns them.** A retry that nobody looks at is the grocer's `retries: 2` with extra steps.

### Measuring flakiness per test

The most reliable evidence is **a failure and a pass on the same commit**, whether from a retry, a manual re-run, or a scheduled repeat. Record that for each test, keep a rolling window of 30 to 90 days, and rank tests by impact:

```
impact  =  flake rate  ×  runs per day
```

A 1% flake in a test that runs on every pull request hurts far more than a 10% flake in a nightly job.

Some flakiness is normal at scale. Google has reported that about 16% of its tests showed some flakiness, and that about 1.5% of all test runs produced a flaky result (Micco, 2016). The goal isn't zero. The goal is a rate low enough that a red build is worth reading.

### Fix, quarantine, or delete

Each flaky test gets one of four outcomes:

- **Fix it now**, when the cause is cheap: a sleep, a clock, a port, a missing `await`. Once found, most causes in the table take about an hour.
- **Quarantine it**, when the fix is expensive and the test is still valuable.
- **Delete it**, when it doesn't earn its cost, or when a smaller test already covers the same class of bug (21.1a).
- **Rewrite it at a lower level**, when a large test is checking something a small one could.

**Quarantine** means the test still runs and its result is still recorded, but it can't fail the build. That's different from skipping it. A skipped test tells you nothing, so you can't see whether a fix worked or whether the test is failing more often. Fowler's rules keep quarantine honest, and a CI gate can enforce them:

- **Every entry has an owner, a ticket, and a date.**
- **Entries expire.** Past the limit, the test gets fixed or deleted. Renewing isn't an option.
- **The quarantine has a size limit.** When it's full, the next flake has to be fixed.
- **Product bugs are never quarantined.** An untriaged entry expires within days, so triage actually happens. If triage says "product," the result is a bug ticket and a failing test, not a quieter test.

Quarantine has a cost that's easy to forget: **a quarantined test protects nothing.** For as long as it's there, CI isn't testing the code it covers.

Some large organizations quarantine automatically when their tooling detects a flake. That removes friction and keeps `main` green. Sooner or later, it will also quarantine a real race. If you automate quarantine, have the tool open a ticket for the owner, and require triage before an entry can last more than a few days.

### Running the suite so the result is readable

A pipeline that runs tests well has a handful of properties. Several were set up in 21.1a:

- **Stages in cost order**, each within its time budget: lint and type checks, then small tests, then medium, then large. When a cheap stage fails, the expensive ones don't start.
- **Sharding balanced by duration.** `--shard=2/4` works in Vitest, Jest, and Playwright, but it splits by files. Without timing data from earlier runs, shards finish minutes apart and the slowest one sets the pace.
- **One merged report.** Each shard writes a machine-readable report (JUnit XML is the common format, and Playwright has its own blob format), and one step merges them. People should read failures on the pull request, not in a 40,000-line log.
- **Artifacts attached to failures**: traces, screenshots, container logs (21.3b).
- **New and changed tests run many times before they merge.** A flaky test is cheapest to fix while its author still remembers writing it. Repeating only the changed files 20 times costs little.
- **A red `main` is an incident.** Revert first and investigate second. A merge queue, which tests the merged result before it lands, keeps most breakage off `main` to begin with.
- **The environment is pinned**: runner image, lockfile, `TZ`, locale. A test that relies on the runner's defaults is one runner upgrade away from failing.

**Test impact analysis** runs only the tests affected by a change (Nx, Bazel, and similar tools). On a big repository, it saves a lot of time. It depends on a complete dependency graph, so run the full suite on `main` or nightly to catch what the graph misses.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Re-runs are routine.** If more than one pull request in ten needs a re-run, flakiness already costs more than fixing it would.
- **Hundreds of medium and large tests across several teams.** At that size, per-test flake history and an owned quarantine pay for themselves.
- **Retries on large tests only, with flaky results recorded.** This fits browser and deployed tests in any pipeline.
- **Repeat runs of new and changed tests**, in any suite with concurrency tests (21.3a) or browser tests (21.3b).
- **Sharding and merged reports**, once a stage exceeds its time budget.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A small suite with two flaky tests.** Fix them this afternoon. Dashboards and quarantine rules are for when there are too many to fix at once.
- **Building tooling before fixing the obvious causes.** A search for `sleep`, `waitForTimeout`, and `new Date()` in test code finds a large share of flakes in an hour.
- **Automatic quarantine for a team of five.** Everyone already knows which tests are flaky.
- **Test impact analysis for a ten-minute suite.** Maintaining the dependency graph costs more than the minutes it saves.
- **Retries on small tests "for stability."** They hide the flakes that are cheapest to fix.

## Trade-offs

- **Quarantined tests protect nothing** while they're quarantined, and the list grows unless something enforces its limits.
- **Retries cost time**, and each one can hide a real intermittent bug.
- **Tracking needs infrastructure**: a results store, a report parser, a dashboard, and someone who reads it.
- **Ownership causes friction.** Deciding which team owns a flaky test that touches several teams' code is a recurring argument.
- **Stress and repeat runs cost CI minutes**, and they can't prove a test is stable. They only show that it didn't fail this time.
- **Random order finds hidden dependencies** and makes failures harder to reproduce, unless the seed is printed and can be reused.
- **Sharding adds moving parts**: uneven shards, report merging, and a crashed shard that looks exactly like a shard with no failures unless the gate checks for it.
- **"A red `main` is an incident" only works once flakiness is low.** Before that, the policy just trains people to revert good changes.

## Alternatives

- **Hermetic builds** (Bazel and similar tools) remove environmental non-determinism by design: pinned tools, sandboxed tests, no network access. The cost is a large migration.
- **Deterministic simulation testing**, as practiced by FoundationDB and TigerBeetle and offered as a service by Antithesis. The system runs on a simulated clock, network, and scheduler, all driven by a seed, so a race found once can be replayed exactly. It's the strongest answer for systems with a lot of concurrency, and the system has to be designed for it.
- **Race detectors and model checkers** find some concurrency bugs without depending on lucky timing.
- **Deleting large tests and pushing checks down** (21.1a). With fewer large tests, there are fewer places for non-determinism to hide.
- **Synthetic monitoring in production** (Module 19), for journeys too unstable to be worth blocking merges on.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0: measure.** Count re-runs per week and the share of red runs on `main`. List the tests that failed and passed on the same commit in the last 30 days.
_Signal you're here:_ nobody knows how often `main` is red.

**Level 1: fix the cheap causes.** Remove fixed sleeps, inject clocks, pin `TZ`, give each worker its own ports and data, reset module state between tests, and stub third parties.
_Signal you need it:_ searching the tests for `sleep` and `new Date()` returns more than a handful of lines.

**Level 2: set a retry policy.** No retries for small and medium tests. Retries for large tests, with flaky results recorded.
_Signal you need it:_ a global retry setting, or failures nobody can explain.

**Level 3: quarantine with rules.** Keep a checked-in list with owners, tickets, dates, expiry, and a size limit, enforced by a gate. Triage every new entry.
_Signal you need it:_ more flaky tests than the team can fix this week.

**Level 4: add per-test history and stress runs.** Store results, rank tests by impact, repeat new and changed tests before merge, and run a nightly job with more workers and random order.
_Signal you need it:_ fixed flakes coming back, or new tests arriving already flaky.

**Level 5: automate at scale.** Detect and quarantine flakes automatically, with tickets. Add test impact analysis and a merge queue.
_Signal you need it:_ many teams, thousands of medium and large tests, and a quarantine list people can no longer keep current by hand.

## Implementation

This section uses TypeScript, for two reasons. The code that turns a quarantine policy into something enforced is a small script over JSON test reports, which most web CI tooling already produces. And JavaScript makes one point especially clearly: there are no threads here, and the grocer's race is still real.

First, the product bug, made deterministic. The fake store (21.2b) lets the test pause each caller right after it reads the order, until both callers have read:

```ts
// a promise that opens once `parties` callers have arrived
function barrier(parties: number) {
  let arrived = 0;
  let open!: () => void;
  const gate = new Promise<void>((resolve) => (open = resolve));
  return async () => {
    if (++arrived === parties) open();
    await gate;
  };
}

test("webhook and redirect arriving together create one picking task", async () => {
  const store = new MemOrderStore([{ id: "ord_1", status: "pending" }]);
  store.afterGet = barrier(2);                  // both callers read before either writes
  const picking = new PickingSpy();
  const orders = new OrderService(store, picking);

  await Promise.all([
    orders.confirmPaid("ord_1", "webhook"),
    orders.confirmPaid("ord_1", "redirect"),
  ]);

  expect(picking.tasksFor("ord_1")).toHaveLength(1);
});
```

The code the test caught, and the fix:

```ts
// before: check, then act. Every `await` is a point where the other request can run.
const order = await this.store.get(id);
if (order.status !== "pending") return;
await this.store.markPaid(id);
await this.picking.create(id);

// after: one conditional write decides which caller confirms.
const won = await this.store.transition(id, { from: "pending", to: "paid" });
if (!won) return;                               // the other caller got there first
await this.picking.create(id);
```

> **Aside: the forced version fails every time against the old code.** The version that relied on timing failed 1 run in 20. Once you can force the interleaving, there's nothing flaky left to discuss. The fixed code never calls `get`, so it never reaches the barrier and nothing waits.

> **Aside: the fake's `transition` is only as trustworthy as the real one.** In Postgres, it's `UPDATE orders SET status = 'paid' WHERE id = $1 AND status = 'pending'`, and "won" means exactly one row changed. Add "a transition from pending succeeds exactly once" to the store's contract suite, so the fake and Postgres are held to the same rule (21.2b).

Second, the quarantine as a checked-in file, `quarantine.json`, with a gate that enforces its rules. The shard jobs don't decide whether the build passes. The gate does.

```json
[
  { "id": "e2e/slots.spec.ts › shows tomorrow's slots", "owner": "team-slots",
    "ticket": "SLOT-812", "since": "2026-09-08", "kind": "test-bug" },
  { "id": "api/checkout.test.ts › applies voucher after retry", "owner": "team-checkout",
    "ticket": "CHK-340", "since": "2026-09-15", "kind": "untriaged" }
]
```

```ts
// ci/gate.ts
type Attempt = "passed" | "failed";
type TestRun = {
  id: string;
  attempts: Attempt[];   // one entry per try, in order
  isNew: boolean;        // not present in the base branch's last full run
};
type Entry = { id: string; owner: string; ticket: string; since: string; kind: "untriaged" | "test-bug" };

const MAX_DAYS = { untriaged: 3, "test-bug": 21 } as const;
const MAX_ENTRIES = 25;

export function gate(runs: TestRun[], quarantine: Entry[], today: Date, fullRun: boolean) {
  const quarantined = new Set(quarantine.map((e) => e.id));
  const blocking: string[] = [];
  const flaky: string[] = [];
  const problems: string[] = [];

  for (const run of runs) {
    if (!run.attempts.includes("failed")) continue;

    if (run.attempts.at(-1) === "passed") {
      flaky.push(run.id);                                       // recorded, whatever happens next
      if (run.isNew) blocking.push(`${run.id} (new test, already flaky)`);
    } else if (!quarantined.has(run.id)) {
      blocking.push(run.id);
    }
  }

  for (const e of quarantine) {
    const age = daysBetween(e.since, today);
    if (age > MAX_DAYS[e.kind]) {
      problems.push(`${e.id}: ${e.kind} for ${age} days (${e.owner}, ${e.ticket}). Fix it or delete it.`);
    }
    if (fullRun && !runs.some((r) => r.id === e.id)) {
      problems.push(`${e.id}: quarantined but didn't run. Renamed, or skipped?`);
    }
  }
  if (quarantine.length > MAX_ENTRIES) problems.push(`quarantine has ${quarantine.length} entries (limit ${MAX_ENTRIES})`);
  if (runs.length === 0) problems.push("no results at all: did a shard crash before writing its report?");

  return { ok: blocking.length === 0 && problems.length === 0, blocking, flaky, problems };
}
```

> **Aside: there is no `"product-bug"` kind, on purpose.** When triage finds a product race, the result is a bug ticket and a test that stays red until the fix lands (or the change is reverted). Because the type has no place for that kind of entry, getting around the policy takes a code review, not a quiet edit.

> **Aside: an already-flaky new test blocks the merge.** Everywhere else, a pass after retry is recorded and allowed through. A new test is the exception, because its author is still here and nobody depends on it yet. That makes it the cheapest point to fix it, and the point where the grocer's race test would have been examined instead of labeled.

> **Aside: the "didn't run" check only makes sense on full runs.** With sharding, a crashed shard looks exactly like a shard with no failures. The empty-results check catches the worst case. A stricter gate also compares the number of results with the previous full run on `main`.

Third, the pipeline around the gate: sharded browser tests, one merged report, the gate itself, and a repeat job for changed specs.

```yaml
# .github/workflows/test.yml (excerpt)
jobs:
  e2e:
    strategy: { fail-fast: false, matrix: { shard: [1, 2, 3, 4] } }
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    env: { TZ: Europe/London }                     # pinned: the stores' own time zone
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - run: npm ci && npx playwright install --with-deps chromium
      - run: npx playwright test --shard=${{ matrix.shard }}/4 --reporter=blob || true   # the gate decides
      - uses: actions/upload-artifact@v4
        if: always()
        with: { name: blob-${{ matrix.shard }}, path: blob-report }

  gate:
    needs: e2e
    if: always()
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - run: npm ci
      - uses: actions/download-artifact@v4
        with: { pattern: blob-*, path: all-blob-reports, merge-multiple: true }
      - run: PLAYWRIGHT_JSON_OUTPUT_NAME=results.json npx playwright merge-reports --reporter=json ./all-blob-reports
      - run: npx tsx ci/run-gate.ts results.json quarantine.json   # also sends flaky results to the history store

  repeat-changed:
    if: github.event_name == 'pull_request'
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
        with: { fetch-depth: 0 }
      - run: npm ci && npx playwright install --with-deps chromium
      - run: npx playwright test --only-changed=origin/${{ github.base_ref }} --repeat-each=20 --retries=0
```

> **Aside: `|| true` on the shard step is deliberate, and it's also a risk.** The shard can't know which failures are quarantined, so its exit code is ignored. That's why the gate has to treat missing results as a failure. Without that check, a shard that crashes before running anything reports nothing, and nothing looks like success.

> **Aside: pin the time zone, then break the pin on purpose.** `TZ=Europe/London` keeps the browser runs stable. A separate nightly job runs the small tests with `TZ=Pacific/Chatham` and `vitest run --sequence.shuffle`, using a seed taken from the run number and printed in the log. Chatham is UTC+12:45 (UTC+13:45 in its summer), so code that assumes whole-hour offsets or a UTC "today" fails there every night, instead of only between 23:00 and midnight in summer. The shuffle finds order-dependent tests the same way, and the printed seed lets anyone replay the failing order.

## Further Reading

- **Martin Fowler, "Eradicating Non-Determinism in Tests"** (martinfowler.com, 2011). Quarantine with limits, and the main causes with their fixes. Still the best single article on the subject.
- **John Micco, "Flaky Tests at Google and How We Mitigate Them"** (Google Testing Blog, 2016). The numbers at scale, and why flakiness is managed rather than eliminated.
- **Qingzhou Luo, Farah Hariri, Lamyaa Eloussi & Darko Marinov, "An Empirical Analysis of Flaky Tests"** (FSE 2014). The causes, ranked, from real fixes in open-source projects.
- **Owain Parry, Gregory Kapfhammer, Michael Hilton & Phil McMinn, "A Survey of Flaky Tests"** (ACM TOSEM, 2021). Detection, causes, and costs, with pointers into the research.
- **Wing Lam et al., "iDFlakies: A Framework for Detecting and Partially Classifying Flaky Tests"** (ICST 2019). How rerunning tests in different orders separates order-dependent tests from the rest.
- **Titus Winters, Tom Manshreck & Hyrum Wright (eds.), _Software Engineering at Google_ (2020), ch. 23, "Continuous Integration."** Presubmit versus post-submit testing, hermeticity, and what a CI system owes the people waiting on it.
- **Playwright documentation**: "Retries" (how flaky results are classified), "Sharding" (blob reports and merging), and the `--only-changed` and `--repeat-each` options.
- **Will Wilson, "Testing Distributed Systems w/ Deterministic Simulation"** (Strange Loop, 2014). The FoundationDB approach, and why a race you can replay is worth an architectural decision.

A Go sibling would show what a toolchain can catch on its own. `go test -race` instruments memory access and reports a data race whenever one occurs during the run, even if the timing didn't produce a wrong answer, so it can catch races a repeated assertion would miss. `-shuffle=on` randomizes test order and prints its seed, and `-count=N` repeats tests without using cached results. The sibling would also show the limit: the grocer's race is check-then-act across database calls, and no memory-level detector can see it. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: when a test fails and then passes, ask whether production could do what the test just did. If it could, you haven't found a flaky test. You've found a bug that only shows up sometimes, and a retry is the one tool guaranteed to hide it.
