# The Management Plane

_Module 17.2a — Webhooks & Integration APIs_

## The Problem

The pipeline from 17.1a delivers reliably. The payloads from 17.1b are designed. The signatures from 17.1c are correct. Six hundred accounts are integrated, and here is one ordinary week of support tickets.

**Monday. "Can you change our webhook URL? We're migrating hosts Saturday."** Endpoint URLs live in a table that only engineers can write to. The change is a one-line `UPDATE`, preceded by a ticket, a triage, an assignment, and a deploy window, and followed by a reply. Forty minutes of engineer time, five days of customer waiting. Integrations are what the sales team sells, and the rate at which they can be set up is capped by an engineering queue.

**Tuesday. "Did you send us the event for order 88421?"** You grep across twelve worker instances for twenty minutes and find it: delivered at 03:14, their endpoint returned `500`. You paste the log line. This exchange happens about six times a week, and every one is an argument in which exactly one party has evidence and it isn't the party being blamed.

**Wednesday. "We were down for maintenance Saturday morning — can you resend everything from that window?"** There is no resend. Somebody writes a script that inserts delivery rows directly, and it goes wrong in one of the two available ways: the `WHERE` clause is too broad and re-sends events already delivered, so the customer ships duplicate orders, or it's too narrow and the gap stays.

**Thursday. "Your new event type broke our integration."** They subscribe to nothing, because subscription isn't a concept — every endpoint receives all forty event types. They wanted `order.paid`; they discard 96% of what arrives; and their strictly-typed deserializer rejected the new `refund.partially_settled` you shipped on Tuesday, taking down their handler for everything.

**Friday, from your own dashboard.** Thirty percent of the delivery queue is attempts against endpoints that have never, in their entire history, returned a `2xx`. One belongs to an account that churned eight months ago. The system is spending a third of its capacity talking to the dead.

And behind all of it, the one that isn't a ticket at all: **a customer's TLS certificate expired on the 3rd, and they found out on the 19th** when their operations team asked why nothing had synced in two weeks. Your `deliveries` table contained a complete, precise, timestamped record of every failure the entire time. Nobody looked, because the failing thing belongs to them and the only place the evidence exists is with you.

That's the shape of the whole problem: **you hold the data and they hold the responsibility, and there is no surface joining the two.** Everything in this article is that surface.

## What It Is

Borrow the vocabulary from networking: what 17.1a built is the **data plane** — the machinery that moves events. What this article covers is the **management plane** — everything that configures, observes, and repairs it. Endpoint registration, subscriptions, the delivery log, replay, endpoint health and lifecycle, and the tooling an integrator needs to build against you.

The reframing that matters: **these are not internal admin tools.** They're product surface, for a user most teams don't think of as a user — the developer at your customer who is building the integration and who will be on call for it afterwards. Teams routinely build the delivery pipeline as a serious engineering project and the management plane as a pile of scripts, then discover that support volume, integration time-to-live, and how often customers notice their own breakage are all determined entirely by the second one.

One disambiguation: "webhook management" in the market usually refers to products for managing webhooks you *receive* — a gateway in front of your inbound endpoints. This article is the producer's side: the control surface you offer consumers over the events you send them.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Adding or changing an endpoint requires an engineer, a deploy, or a ticket.
- Answering "did you send it?" requires log access, and takes more than a minute.
- Your replay mechanism is a script in a repository, run by hand.
- Every endpoint receives every event type.
- A measurable share of delivery volume targets endpoints that have never succeeded.
- Customers learn their integration is broken from their own users, days late.
- Your integration guide says "you'll need a publicly reachable HTTPS endpoint" and offers no way to test one.
- You cannot answer "how many endpoints subscribe to `invoice.voided`?", which means you cannot deprecate anything.
- Nobody can state the success rate or p95 delivery latency for a given endpoint over the last hour.
- Support's first move on any webhook ticket is to ask the customer for *their* logs.

## How It Works

### The endpoint as a first-class resource

An endpoint is an object in your public API, created and managed by the customer:

```
POST /v1/webhook_endpoints
{
  "url": "https://hooks.acme.io/orders",
  "enabled_events": ["order.paid", "invoice.*"],
  "description": "Fulfilment service (owner: platform-team)"
}
→ 201
{
  "id": "we_01HXQ…",
  "status": "pending_verification",
  "secret": "whsec_…",          ← returned once, never again
  "api_version": "2026-03-01",  ← pinned here, per 17.1b
  "created_by": "usr_…"
}
```

The unglamorous fields do the most work. **`description`** is what tells someone in 2028 whether this endpoint can be deleted; without it you will accumulate endpoints nobody dares touch. **`created_by`** is who gets the email when it breaks. **`api_version`** pins at the endpoint, not the account, because one customer routinely has a 2021 integration and a new one.

A few operational decisions that are annoying to change later:

- **Normalize and validate the URL** at creation — scheme, no embedded credentials, no fragment — and run the 17.1c checks then as well as at delivery.
- **Detect duplicates.** The same URL registered twice means every event arrives twice, forever, and the customer will report it as your bug. Surface it at creation.
- **Cap endpoints per account.** A buggy Terraform provider creating four hundred endpoints is not hypothetical.
- **Disable rather than delete**, and never orphan the delivery log — "what did you send the endpoint we deleted last month?" is a real question with real money attached.
- **Restrict who can create one, and audit it.** This is the point worth stopping on: *a webhook endpoint is a standing data export.* Anyone who can create one can route a stream of account data to any address they choose. It belongs at an admin permission level, in the audit log (Module 24), and ideally with a notification to account owners when one is added.

### Subscriptions

Filtering is what makes the relationship sustainable in both directions: consumers stop receiving noise, and you stop being unable to add an event type without risking someone's parser.

**Filter at fan-out, not at delivery.** A delivery row you create and then discard costs a queue slot and a log entry, and it pollutes the customer's delivery log with events they never asked for. The matching belongs in the query that creates delivery rows.

**Wildcards** (`order.*`, `*`) are a convenience with a hazard attached: new event types silently start flowing to existing endpoints. That's the behaviour many consumers want and the last thing a strict deserializer wants. Offer them, document exactly what a `.` means in a pattern, and consider not auto-enrolling wildcard subscribers when you introduce a whole new resource.

**Attribute-level filtering** — only orders above $1,000, only this location id — gets requested eventually, and it's the place to be careful. You would be evaluating customer-authored predicates on the hot path of every event in the system. If you build it, keep it to equality matches on a small, declared set of fields. An expression language here is the same mistake as the rules engine in 14.5: enormous surface, evaluated in your critical path, authored by people you can't review.

**Scoping** is the decision that's hardest to reverse. Is an endpoint attached to an account (Stripe's model), or to a project, repository, or workspace (GitHub's model, where webhooks exist at repo, org, and app level)? Account-level is simpler and means a customer with two hundred projects gets one firehose. Resource-level matches how customers organize work and multiplies your endpoint count by a thousand. Pick deliberately; migrating between them means every consumer re-registers.

### The delivery log

One row per attempt: event id, endpoint id, attempt number, request time, response status, duration, a truncated response body, and the error if the request never completed.

It serves two different users with the same data and different access. Your support and engineering teams need to search across accounts by event id. The customer needs to see their own endpoint's history without asking anyone. Build the internal view first — it's the same query, it answers Tuesday's ticket in ten seconds instead of twenty minutes, and it's the highest-return artifact in this entire module.

Then size it honestly, because it will be your largest table:

```
 90,000 events/day
× 1.5 endpoints per event (avg fan-out)
× 1.2 attempts per delivery (avg)
≈ 162,000 attempts/day
× ~4 KB (payload + response snippet + metadata)
≈ 650 MB/day  →  ~58 GB at 90 days' retention
```

Which forces two decisions up front. **Partition by day and drop partitions** rather than issuing `DELETE`s against a table of that size. And **split retention by field**: bodies are the bulk and the most sensitive part, so keep request and response bodies for a short window (7–14 days covers all real debugging) while keeping status, timing, and event id for much longer, because the long-lived question is "did you send it" and not "what exactly did it say."

The bodies are also customer data, including whatever PII your payloads carry (17.1b). Access control and retention here are permanent obligations, not hygiene — Module 34.

### Replay

Replay means: create a **new delivery** for an **existing event**, with a new delivery id and the *same* event id and the *same* frozen body.

That sentence is the whole design. The consumer receives a byte-identical request with the same `Webhook-Id` they may already have processed, so their deduplication does exactly what it's supposed to. The 17.1b insistence on freezing the body is what makes replay safe to hand to a customer at all — a replay that re-rendered from current state would be a lie about the past, and would be silently discarded by every consumer who deduplicates correctly.

Offer it at three granularities: one delivery, all failed deliveries for an endpoint in a time range, and all events of certain types in a window. Then put guardrails on it, because a replay of 40,000 events into a small Rails app is a denial-of-service attack that you performed on request: cap the range, rate-limit the replayed deliveries, put them in a lower-priority lane so a backfill doesn't delay live events for the same endpoint, and state plainly in the UI that consumers may see duplicates.

### Health and the endpoint lifecycle

```
pending_verification ──▶ enabled ⇄ degraded ──▶ disabled ──▶ deleted
                                      │
                                      └── customer notified at each transition
```

**Degraded** is the useful middle state: consecutive failures past a threshold move the endpoint to a slower probe rate and trigger the first notification. Most breakage is fixed here, if anyone is told.

**The disable rule should be a time window, not a failure count.** A raw consecutive-failure threshold is unfair in both directions: an endpoint receiving 50 events a minute hits twenty consecutive failures during a sixty-second blip, while a genuinely dead endpoint receiving three events a week takes two months to get there. "No successful delivery in 72 hours, and at least N attempts in that period" treats both correctly, and a low-volume endpoint that never trips it is also one that isn't costing you anything.

**Notify the endpoint's creator or technical contact** — not the billing contact, who will delete the email. Email plus an in-app notice plus, if you want the circularity, an `endpoint.disabled` event delivered to the account's *other* endpoints.

**Don't auto-re-enable.** Probing a disabled endpoint occasionally and telling the customer when it looks healthy is good; resuming automatically produces flapping, and the flap coincides with a partly-fixed deploy.

**Decide the backlog question before a customer asks it**, because every recovered customer asks it within an hour: when an endpoint is re-enabled, do they receive the events that occurred while it was disabled? The defensible default is no — deliver forward from re-enable, and point them at replay or a log endpoint for the gap. That way the choice to absorb two weeks of backlog is theirs, made explicitly, rather than yours, made by a queue.

### Tooling for the developer building against you

This is where integration time-to-live is actually decided, and it is almost always underbuilt.

- **A "send test event" button**, producing a synthetic event with a clearly marked test flag so it can never be mistaken for a real one. It answers "is my endpoint reachable and is my signature check right?" in five seconds.
- **Payload examples for every event type**, generated from the same fixtures as your schema tests (17.1b) so the docs cannot drift from reality.
- **A way to trigger real events on demand in a sandbox.** The highest-value item in this list and the least commonly built. A developer cannot make `invoice.payment_failed` happen without a real card actually failing, so they either mock it from your docs and get the details subtly wrong, or they test against production. A CLI that puts a test account into the state that produces an event — Stripe's `stripe trigger` is the reference implementation — removes an entire class of integration bug.
- **Local forwarding.** A CLI that subscribes on behalf of the account and forwards to `localhost:3000`, or, at minimum, documentation pointing at a tunnelling tool. Without this, every iteration of their handler requires a deploy.
- **Test/live separation** that goes all the way down: separate endpoints, separate secrets, a `livemode`-style field in the payload, and a hard guarantee that test events never reach production endpoints. This has schema consequences, so decide it before you have customers rather than after.

### Metrics

Per endpoint, exposed to the customer: success rate, p95 delivery latency, pending backlog, attempts in the last hour. For yourself, the single best health metric is **the age of the oldest undelivered delivery** — queue depth tells you how much work exists, but oldest-age tells you whether anything is stuck, and it catches the per-endpoint starvation cases that averages hide.

And one alert that isn't about any endpoint: **a simultaneous failure-rate spike across many unrelated endpoints is a signal about you**, not them. Malformed payloads, a signing bug, an expired egress certificate. It's the only way you'll find out quickly that you broke every consumer at once.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A self-serve registration API:** once you have more than a handful of integrators, or the first time a URL change becomes a ticket.
- **An internal delivery-log search:** immediately. It's one query and a page, and it's the difference between a twenty-minute investigation and a ten-second answer.
- **A customer-facing delivery log:** once support is answering "did you send it?" more than once a week.
- **Replay:** as soon as any endpoint has ever been down, which is to say as soon as you have real consumers.
- **Subscription filtering:** when consumers discard the majority of what you send, or when adding an event type has become risky.
- **Auto-disable:** when dead endpoints are a measurable share of delivery volume — the 30% in the Problem isn't unusual.
- **Trigger and forwarding tooling:** when integrations are a sales dependency, or when "how do I test this?" is a recurring question.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Two partners with named contacts at each.** A config table and a shared Slack channel genuinely beats a management plane here. Build it when the coordination cost exceeds the build cost, not before.
- **Attribute-level filters before anyone has asked for event-type filters.**
- **A customer-facing dashboard before an internal one.** The internal view answers most of the same questions at a fraction of the cost, and it tells you what customers actually ask about.
- **Granular subscriptions for three event types.** Everyone wants all three.
- **Writing your own local-forwarding CLI** when documenting an existing tunnelling tool costs a paragraph.
- **Health scoring with weighted decay, anomaly detection, or automatic re-enablement.** Two thresholds and an email outperform all of it and can be explained to a customer in one sentence.

## Trade-offs

- **The management plane is usually more code than the delivery pipeline**, and it never feels like engineering while you're writing it. That mismatch is why it gets deferred and why deferring it is expensive.
- **The delivery log is your largest table and a concentration of customer data**, with retention, partitioning, and access-control obligations that don't go away.
- **Exposing the log exposes you.** Customers will see that an event was delivered forty minutes late, that it took nine attempts, that they received the same event twice. All of it is true and some of it is embarrassing. It is still net positive, because the alternative is them believing something worse and you having no way to correct it — but it's a real disclosure decision.
- **Replay is a load generator you hand to customers**, pointed at both systems.
- **Self-serve registration changes your threat model.** It is a self-service data-export capability, and it needs permissions and auditing in a way that a config table edited by engineers did not.
- **Filtering creates a new support category**: "why didn't we receive this event?" where the answer is their own subscription. Make the filter visible in the UI next to the delivery log, or you'll answer this weekly.
- **Auto-disable will eventually fire during an incident of your own making** and disable thousands of healthy integrations, which then need thousands of re-enables. The guard against this belongs in the rule itself.

## Alternatives

**A managed webhook provider** — Svix, Hookdeck, Convoy. The management plane *is* their product, including an embeddable portal your customers use directly. This is the strongest buy-versus-build case in the module, because the delivery pipeline is a week and the management plane is a quarter. The cost is a vendor holding your customer data and a customer-facing UX you don't control.

**A log API instead of a management plane.** Give consumers the cursor-paginated event log from 17.1a and let them heal themselves: replay becomes "read from an earlier cursor", backlog-on-re-enable stops being your decision, and "did you send it?" becomes a question they can answer without you. This removes a genuinely large fraction of everything above, and it's why so many mature APIs ship both mechanisms.

**Configuration as code.** Endpoints declared through your API by the customer's own Terraform or deployment pipeline rather than clicked into a UI. Complements the API rather than replacing it, and it's what your more sophisticated integrators want anyway.

**The installed-app model.** Instead of each customer registering a URL, a developer registers an *application* once with a single endpoint, and customers install it — GitHub Apps and Slack apps work this way. Endpoint count collapses from one-per-customer to one-per-integration, and registration, verification, and secret management happen once with a party you have a relationship with. It changes who the management plane serves, and it's where 17.3 and 17.4 pick up.

**Delivery into the customer's cloud account.** Management becomes IAM and their own console, which removes endpoint lifecycle, secrets, and health monitoring from your surface entirely — for the subset of customers who can use it.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — An internal page.** Endpoints in a table with a `description`, and a search-by-event-id view for support. One afternoon, and it removes the most expensive recurring ticket you have. *Move when:* customers ask to change their own URLs.

**L1 — Customer-facing CRUD and visibility.** Endpoint create/update/disable, secret reveal and roll, event-type subscriptions, and a delivery log for the last 7 days. *Move when:* the first "can you resend?" arrives.

**L2 — Repair and lifecycle.** Replay (single and filtered), auto-disable with notification to the creator, a test-event button, and published payload examples generated from fixtures. *Move when:* a customer's breakage goes unnoticed for more than a day, or dead endpoints show up in your queue metrics.

**L3 — Developer experience and scale.** Trigger tooling and local forwarding, test/live separation, per-endpoint metrics exposed to customers, partitioned retention on the log. *Move when:* integration setup time is a sales blocker, or the log becomes a database capacity question.

**L4 — Data-driven contract management.** Subscription data feeding deprecation decisions, attribute filters if genuinely demanded, or a serious evaluation of buying the whole layer. *Move when:* you need to retire an event type and want to know exactly who to email.

## Implementation

TypeScript. Three pieces: the matcher that turns subscriptions into fan-out, the disable decision, and replay.

**Matching, with the fan-out query it implies:**

```ts
// Stored per endpoint: ["order.paid", "invoice.*", "*"]
export function matches(patterns: string[], type: string): boolean {
  return patterns.some((p) => {
    if (p === "*") return true;
    if (p.endsWith(".*")) return type.startsWith(p.slice(0, -1));
    return p === type;
  });
}
```

Three rules and no regular expressions, deliberately. The moment patterns become regexes, a customer writes one that backtracks catastrophically and it executes on your fan-out path for every event in the system. Note also the decision hidden in line three: `invoice.*` matches `invoice.payment.failed` here, because the check is a prefix test. Whether a dot is a boundary is a semantic choice — make it, document it, and never change it, because consumers build subscription lists on the answer.

In the database, the same rule is an array overlap rather than a scan — expand the event type into every pattern that could match it, and let a GIN index do the work:

```sql
-- replaces `$3 = ANY(ep.event_types)` from 17.1a
WHERE ep.status = 'enabled'
  AND ep.event_types && ARRAY[$3, split_part($3, '.', 1) || '.*', '*']
```

**The disable decision, as a pure function:**

```ts
export function shouldDisable(h: EndpointHealth, now: Date, fleet: FleetStats): boolean {
  // If most endpoints are failing, the broken thing is us.
  if (fleet.failureRateLastHour > 0.5) return false;

  const lastGood = h.lastSuccessAt ?? h.firstAttemptAt;
  const hoursDown = (now.getTime() - lastGood.getTime()) / 3_600_000;

  return hoursDown >= 72 && h.attemptsSinceLastSuccess >= 20;
}
```

Both conditions are load-bearing, and so is the first line. The time window plus an attempt minimum is what makes the rule fair across volumes — twenty consecutive failures is sixty seconds for a busy endpoint and two months for a quiet one, and neither number means what it appears to. The fleet guard exists because of a specific incident that happens to everyone eventually: you ship a malformed payload, four fifths of your consumers return `400`, and your automation disables three thousand integrations in ten minutes. The payload bug takes twenty minutes to fix. Re-enabling three thousand endpoints and explaining it takes a week.

**Replay:**

```ts
export async function replay(endpointId: string, eventIds: string[], actor: Actor) {
  if (eventIds.length > MAX_REPLAY) throw new ApiError("replay_too_large");

  await db.tx(async (tx) => {
    await tx.query(
      `INSERT INTO deliveries (id, event_id, endpoint_id, state, next_attempt_at, priority, replay_of)
       SELECT gen_random_uuid(), e.id, $1, 'pending', now(), 'low', $3
         FROM events e
        WHERE e.id = ANY($2) AND e.tenant_id = $4`,
      [endpointId, eventIds, actor.requestId, actor.tenantId],
    );
    await audit(tx, actor, "webhook.replay", { endpointId, count: eventIds.length });
  });
}
```

New delivery rows, existing event rows. The consumer receives a byte-identical body with the `Webhook-Id` they may already have seen, which is precisely what makes their deduplication work and what makes this button safe to expose. `priority: 'low'` keeps a ten-thousand-event backfill from starving live events on the same endpoint — the leasing model from 17.1a serializes per endpoint, so without a priority lane a replay is a queue of ten thousand things standing in front of today's orders. And the `audit` call is not decoration: replay is a bulk data-transmission action performed by a user, and when someone asks in nine months why a customer received four thousand duplicate events on a Tuesday, that row is the entire answer.

---

The pattern generalizes well beyond webhooks: **anything a customer configures once and that then runs without them watching accumulates a management plane, whether you build one or not.** API keys, SSO connections, scheduled exports, sync integrations, alerting rules — each is a thing that was correct when it was set up, fails silently later, and whose failure is visible to you and consequential for them. The questions are always the same four. Can they change it themselves? Can they see what it did? Can they make it run again? And when it breaks, who finds out, from what record, and how quickly? A feature that can't answer those isn't finished; it's just not failing yet.

## Further Reading

- **Stripe's webhook endpoint API and dashboard documentation**, plus the `stripe listen` and `stripe trigger` CLI commands — the clearest worked example of the developer tooling described above, and the reason so many integrators expect it elsewhere.
- **GitHub's webhook deliveries interface and redelivery API** — a public, mature implementation of a customer-facing delivery log with replay, and worth studying for what they chose to expose.
- **Shopify's documented policy on removing webhook subscriptions after sustained failures** — a concrete, published auto-disable rule and the customer communication around it.
- **Svix's and Hookdeck's documentation on embedded app portals** — useful for scoping the build-versus-buy decision, since they enumerate what a complete management plane contains.
- Documentation for **ngrok** or **cloudflared** tunnels — the thing to link from your integration guide today if you never build local forwarding.
- Within this map: delivery and leasing in **17.1a**, frozen bodies and event ids in **17.1b**, registration-time security checks in **17.1c**, admin tooling patterns in **26**, audit logging in **24**, metrics and alerting in **19**, deprecation in **33**, and retention obligations in **34**.

A Go sibling would earn its place on the replay path specifically. Backfilling a million deliveries without starving live traffic is a scheduling problem — priority lanes interacting with per-endpoint leases, a token bucket per destination, and cancellation halfway through — and it's much easier to see when the worker loop is in front of you rather than behind a queue library's configuration. Not yet written.
