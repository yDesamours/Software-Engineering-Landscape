# Event Payload Design

_Module 17.1b — Webhooks & Integration APIs_

## The Problem

The delivery pipeline from 17.1a is built and working. Events fan out, retries back off, dead endpoints get disabled. The thing being delivered was decided in about forty seconds, because there was an obvious answer sitting right there:

```ts
await emit(order.tenantId, JSON.stringify(order));
```

Which produces this, on every order change:

```json
{
  "id": 8841,
  "tenant_id": 42,
  "status": "paid",
  "total_cents": 12900,
  "currency": "usd",
  "customer_email": "dana@acme.io",
  "internal_risk_score": 0.82,
  "fulfillment_hold_reason": null,
  "line_items": [ … 3 objects … ],
  "updated_at": "2026-03-04T11:32:04.881Z"
}
```

It's fine for a year. Then the bills come due, and they come due all at once.

**"Is this a new event or a retry?"** There is no event id, only an order id. A consumer receiving two identical bodies cannot tell whether you delivered twice or the order changed twice in a way that didn't alter any visible field. They pick one interpretation. Whichever they pick is wrong some of the time.

**"What changed?"** There is no event type either. The consumer's only option is to keep a local copy of every order and diff it, which is a database they didn't want, kept in sync by the very mechanism they're trying to use. The ones who don't do that just re-run their entire order workflow on every delivery, which is how a customer gets four shipping labels.

**`internal_risk_score` is now load-bearing for someone else.** Nobody meant to publish it. Three integrators found it, two built alerts on it, and one has it in a quarterly report. You cannot remove it and you cannot change how it's computed, because the field's meaning is now a contract you never signed.

**The mirror goes backwards.** Two updates 200 milliseconds apart deliver in parallel, and the `pending` body arrives after the `paid` body. The consumer applies last-write-wins, because that's the obvious thing to do, and their order sits in `pending` forever. This is not rare. It is the default behaviour of any at-least-once system with more than one worker.

**Attempt #9 is not attempt #1.** The body is serialized when the delivery worker picks the job up. The first attempt failed; six hours later the retry succeeds and carries `status: "cancelled"` under an event that means *paid*. The same event has now had two different truths, and your delivery log shows only the second one.

**`"id": 8841`.** When you shard and move to prefixed string identifiers, every consumer who stored that as an integer breaks on the same day.

None of these are delivery bugs. The pipeline did exactly what it was told. They're all the same bug: **the thing being delivered was never designed** — it's a database row that escaped.

## What It Is

Two things are being designed here, and separating them is most of the work.

The **envelope** is the metadata every event carries regardless of what happened: an id, a type, a timestamp, a version. It's the part your delivery infrastructure, your documentation, and your consumers' plumbing all depend on, and it's identical across your entire catalogue.

The **payload** — conventionally a `data` object inside the envelope — is what happened, in domain terms. Its design comes down to one axis: how much state it carries. At one end, a bare reference (*order `ord_88421` was paid; go look*). At the other, a full copy of the object. The first shape is usually called a **notification event**; the second is **event-carried state transfer**, Martin Fowler's term and a useful one, because naming it makes clear that you are choosing to replicate data into someone else's system.

One framing that resolves a surprising number of arguments: **an event is a fact about the past, not a row and not a command.** `order.paid`, not `order` and not `send_receipt`. Facts are immutable, which is why the body gets frozen; facts are things someone else may reasonably act on, which is what decides whether a state change deserves an event at all.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Consumers issue a `GET` for the same resource immediately after every webhook — either your payload is missing what they need, or they don't trust it.
- Your payload is the output of an ORM serializer, or is assembled by spreading an entity.
- Support has been asked whether a delivery was a duplicate, and couldn't answer.
- A consumer keeps a shadow copy of your objects purely to diff them and work out what changed.
- You know of a field you'd like to remove and can't, because you don't know who reads it.
- Somebody's replica of your data is wrong in a way that goes away if they refetch — a reordering symptom.
- The retry of an event carries different data than the first attempt.
- Your payload contains something your own UI doesn't show.
- A consumer's parser broke when you added a field, or when you added a value to an enum.
- Identifiers in your payloads are integers, or are the primary keys from your tables.

## How It Works

### The envelope

Six fields, each earning its place:

```json
{
  "id":          "evt_01HXQ7M3F2K8",
  "type":        "order.paid",
  "api_version": "2026-03-01",
  "occurred_at": "2026-03-04T11:32:04Z",
  "data":        { … }
}
```

- **`id`** — unique per event, **stable across retries**. This is the consumer's idempotency key and your support handle. It is the single most unretrofittable field in the system: without it, deduplication is impossible and no conversation about a specific delivery can be grounded.
- **`type`** — what happened, as a string the consumer can switch on and subscribe to. `resource.action_past_tense`, namespaced.
- **`occurred_at`** — when the fact became true, not when the request was sent. Those differ by milliseconds normally and by days after an outage, and the consumer needs the first one. Send time and attempt number belong in headers, because they legitimately differ per attempt.
- **`api_version`** — the schema the body was rendered in, so a consumer can tell when something changed underneath them.
- **`data`** — the payload.
- Optionally **`sequence`** — a monotonic counter per subscription, which is what lets a consumer detect that they missed something rather than discovering it later.

Everything that varies per delivery attempt goes in headers (`Webhook-Id`, `Webhook-Timestamp`, `Webhook-Attempt`), never in the body. The body has to be byte-identical across attempts, for two reasons: the signature covers the raw bytes (17.1c), and an event that means different things on attempt one and attempt nine isn't a fact.

### The body is frozen at creation

Render the JSON when the event is recorded, store those exact bytes, and re-send them unchanged forever. Not a JSONB blob re-serialized at send time; not a reference to a live entity.

This is a small implementation decision with three consequences that are hard to get back later. The signature stays valid. The delivery log shows precisely what the consumer received, which is the whole point of having one. And the semantics stay honest: `order.paid` describes the order as it was when it was paid, which is what the event claims.

It also forces the staleness question into the open rather than letting it be discovered: **the payload is a snapshot of a past moment, and the retry horizon from 17.1a is measured in days.** Design accordingly, and say so in your documentation.

### Thin, fat, and the shape almost everyone lands on

**Thin** — the envelope plus a reference:

```json
{ "type": "order.paid", "data": { "object": "order", "id": "ord_88421" } }
```

Can't go stale, because there's nothing to go stale. Authorization happens at fetch time, so a payload can never leak something the consumer's token wouldn't get them. The schema is trivially stable. But every event becomes a callback to your API, and those callbacks are *spiky* in exactly the wrong way: a bulk update fans out to forty integrators who all call you back within the same second. It also fails outright for terminal events — `user.deleted` followed by a fetch that returns `404` tells the consumer nothing about what was deleted.

**Fat** — the full object:

Self-contained, no callback, survives deletion, and lets a consumer maintain a replica without ever calling your API. In exchange: it's stale on arrival by up to the retry horizon, it corrupts replicas under reordering unless you solve that separately, it publishes your schema, and the data lands permanently in systems outside your control — their logs, their backups, their warehouse.

**What mature APIs actually ship** is a designed middle: the envelope, a deliberately chosen set of fields that define *this event type*, and a resource reference for anything more. `invoice.payment_failed` carries the amount, the currency, the failure code, and the invoice id — not the full invoice with its line items. The selection is the design work, and it's per event type, not global.

### The version guard

The single highest-value field in a payload that consumers replicate is a monotonic version on the object.

```
producer emits:    v2 (pending)  ─┐
                   v3 (paid)     ─┤ 200ms apart
                                  │
consumer receives: v3 (paid)      ← 11:32:04.180   apply:  3 > 0  ✓
                   v2 (pending)   ← 11:32:04.260   ignore: 2 < 3  ✗
```

A consumer who stores the version alongside the object and applies an event only when its version is higher gets a convergent replica out of an unordered, at-least-once, duplicate-prone stream. One integer does what serialized delivery, ordering guarantees, and exactly-once semantics were all supposed to do — and unlike those, it costs you nothing at runtime and survives your consumers doing things in parallel.

Any monotonic value works: a row version column, a per-entity counter, even `occurred_at` if your resolution is fine enough and you can tolerate ties. What matters is that it is *per object*, it never goes backwards, and it appears in the payload. Document what consumers should do with it, because most of them won't derive the rule themselves.

### Naming and granularity

Past tense, dot-namespaced, resource first: `order.paid`, `invoice.payment_failed`, `document.signature_requested`. No commands (`send_receipt` is an instruction, not a fact), no verbs in the present tense.

Granularity is the judgment call. A single `order.updated` forces every consumer to diff. Forty fine-grained types make your catalogue a maintenance surface and your consumers' subscription lists unmanageable. The working rule: **emit a type when someone outside would react to it differently.** `order.paid` and `order.cancelled` lead to different code paths in every consumer; `order.notes_edited` leads to none. When you genuinely can't tell, a coarse `resource.updated` carrying a list of changed field names is a reasonable escape hatch — Stripe's `previous_attributes` is this idea — but it's expensive to compute correctly and permanent once shipped.

Your event types are a catalogue and a commitment. Adding one is safe. Removing one is a breaking change to invisible consumers. A type you emit "just in case" is a type you support forever. And wildcard subscriptions (`order.*`) mean new types silently start reaching existing endpoints — convenient for consumers, and a way to ship a surprise into production for someone who never asked for it.

### Compatibility rules

What's safe and what isn't, given that you cannot see your consumers' code:

| Change | Safe? |
|---|---|
| Add an optional field | Yes — *if* consumers ignore unknown fields |
| Add a new event type | Yes, unless consumers use wildcards and switch exhaustively |
| Remove or rename a field | No |
| Change a field's type (`8841` → `"ord_8841"`) | No |
| Add a value to an enum | No, in practice |
| Change a field's *meaning* without changing its shape | No, and undetectable |
| Send `null` where the field was previously absent | Usually, but test it |

Two of those deserve emphasis because teams get them wrong confidently.

"Consumers ignore unknown fields" is a thing you have to *tell them*, prominently, and even then it isn't universally true — several popular deserializers reject unknown properties by default, and typed languages with generated models are where this bites. Adding a field is the safest change you can make and it still warrants a changelog entry.

Adding an enum value breaks exhaustive switches, which is to say it breaks the consumers who wrote the most careful code. Publish enums as open from day one — document that new values may appear and name the fallback behaviour you expect — and you convert a breaking change into a normal one.

Beyond that, a few conventions that are free at the start and expensive later: **identifiers are opaque strings**, never integers and never your primary keys. **Money is an integer in minor units plus a currency code**, never a float and never a bare number. **Timestamps are RFC 3339 with an explicit offset**, in UTC.

### Versioning, when additive stops being enough

Three strategies, in increasing order of cost:

1. **Additive only.** Never remove, never re-type; new concepts get new fields or new event types. Costs nothing, works far longer than people expect, and accumulates cruft that you document as deprecated and keep sending.
2. **Version in the type**: `order.paid` and `order.paid.v2` emitted in parallel during a migration window, consumers subscribe explicitly. Simple, explicit, self-documenting. Doubles delivery volume for the overlap and puts a migration deadline in front of every consumer.
3. **Pinned versions per endpoint.** Each registered endpoint records the schema version it was created against; events are rendered into that version at send time. This is the model large API companies converge on, and it is genuinely expensive: you maintain a transformer per version, forever, and those transformers must run over *stored* events, which means a bug in one is a delivery bug for a customer who changed nothing.

Note *per endpoint*, not per account. One customer routinely has an old integration built in 2021 and a new one built last month, and pinning at the account level forces them to migrate both at once.

### Size, and what you're allowed to put in someone else's database

Cap the payload, and never embed an unbounded collection. An order with 400 line items produces a body that will be rejected somewhere — body-parser defaults, gateway limits, serverless request caps — and the failure looks like a delivery problem for one specific customer on one specific day. Reference the collection and let them page it.

And treat the payload as data you are permanently depositing in systems you do not control. It lands in their application logs, their error tracker, their warehouse, their backups. Redaction is not retractable. The sharpest case is the one people ship without noticing: **a `user.deleted` event containing the user's full record distributes the data at the exact moment you were obliged to destroy it.** Terminal events carry identifiers and a timestamp. Module 34 covers the obligations this touches.

## When It's Appropriate

Which shape to reach for, in concrete terms:

- **Fat payloads** when the consumer maintains a mirror or a warehouse; when the event is terminal and there will be nothing to fetch; when the object is immutable after the event (a finalized invoice); when your API is rate-limited tightly enough that a callback per event is a real cost to them.
- **Thin payloads** when the resource is large or contains unbounded collections; when authorization is fine-grained and changes over time; when the data is sensitive enough that you'd rather it be fetched under a live token than copied; when objects change many times a second and the consumer only ever wants the latest.
- **A version field** as soon as any consumer stores your objects and more than one event per object can be in flight — which in practice means any object that changes more than once a minute.
- **Sequence numbers per subscription** when consumers need to detect gaps, which usually means they're doing reconciliation or compliance work rather than reacting to individual events.
- **Pinned per-endpoint versioning** when you have hundreds of integrators on multi-year contracts and have already been burned by a breaking change — not before.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **One internal consumer.** Share a types package and skip all of this; you can change both sides in one pull request, which is the only real advantage of owning both ends and it's worth taking.
- **A single event type.** "Your export is ready" needs `id`, `type`, `occurred_at`, and a download reference. No catalogue, no versioning mechanism, no changed-fields.
- **Version-transformation machinery before your second breaking change.** Additive-only is not a stopgap; it's the strategy, until evidence says otherwise.
- **`previous_attributes` or changed-field lists on speculation.** Computing them correctly is fiddly, and supporting them is forever.
- **A schema registry, Avro, or Protobuf** for JSON-over-HTTP to strangers. Registries work because both sides are in one ecosystem with one toolchain; your consumers are a PHP shop, a Zapier action, and an intern with `requests`. Publishing JSON Schema gets you the reviewable artifact without the ceremony.
- **Sequence numbers when nobody replicates.** They imply a guarantee you then have to keep.

## Trade-offs

- **Every published field is permanent.** Designing the payload doesn't reduce that commitment; it makes it explicit and reviewable, which is the actual benefit. You're trading a vague permanent obligation for a deliberate one.
- **A frozen body is stale by design.** You've traded "arrives wrong under retry" for "arrives old under retry", which is better but must be documented, and it pushes consumers toward a refetch you were trying to avoid.
- **Thin payloads move load back onto your API**, in bursts shaped by your own fan-out.
- **Fat payloads make you a data processor** in someone else's system, with the retention and deletion consequences that carries.
- **Per-endpoint versioning means transformers over stored events** — code that runs long after it was written, against data rendered under assumptions the author has forgotten.
- **The catalogue is documentation work.** Every type needs a description, an example payload, and a test. Teams underestimate this by an order of magnitude and end up with fourteen event types documented by three of them.
- **A richer envelope invites dependence on fields you consider incidental.** Anything you send is something someone can build on, including `occurred_at` resolution and key ordering.

## Alternatives

**CloudEvents** — a CNCF standard envelope with defined attributes and HTTP bindings. Worth adopting when your events cross into infrastructure that already speaks it (Knative, EventBridge, Azure Event Grid), or as a checklist even if you don't. The cost is an attribute vocabulary (`specversion`, `source`, `subject`) that reads like infrastructure rather than product API, which most integrators find odd in a payload about orders.

**Standard Webhooks** — narrower, covering the headers and signature rather than the body, and complementary rather than competing.

**Publishing JSON Schema for every event type** — the practical middle ground. You get a machine-readable contract, generated types for consumers who want them, and a CI check that the code and the published schema haven't drifted, without asking anyone to adopt a registry.

**No payload at all** — "something changed on resource X, go look." The degenerate thin case, and a legitimate design when your data is sensitive or your objects are large. WebSub works roughly this way. The consumer still polls, but only when there's something to find.

**A log API as the contract, webhooks as a hint.** If the sync endpoint is the source of truth, payload design stops being load-bearing — consumers use the webhook as a trigger and read state from an interface that already has versioning, pagination, and authorization solved. This is the cheapest escape from most of this article, and it's the same recommendation 17.1a arrived at from the delivery side.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — The four unretrofittable fields.** `id`, `type`, `occurred_at`, and an opaque resource reference. You can ship these with no payload at all and add data later; you cannot add them later without breaking everyone. *Do this before the first external consumer exists.*

**L1 — A designed `data` object per type.** Hand-written projections, not serialized entities. Documented, with an example payload per type and an explicit "ignore fields you don't recognize; enums are open." *Move when:* consumers start calling back for the same three fields every time.

**L2 — Version guard and a published schema.** A monotonic `version` in every payload that represents an object, a JSON Schema per event type in your docs, and a test that validates rendered payloads against it. *Move when:* the first consumer reports state that's wrong until they refetch.

**L3 — Catalogue features.** Subscription filters, wildcards, changed-field lists, a machine-readable event catalogue. *Move when:* consumers are receiving and discarding the majority of what you send.

**L4 — A versioning mechanism.** Pinned per endpoint, with transformers. *Move when:* you have a change you genuinely cannot make additively and more consumers than you can email.

The migration pattern that works at every level: **emit both, then deprecate the type.** Add the new event type alongside the old, announce a sunset date, watch your own delivery log to see who's still subscribed to the old one — you have that data, which is more than Module 33 can usually say about a REST endpoint — and contact them individually. It's slow and it works.

## Implementation

TypeScript, because the event catalogue *is* a discriminated union, and the compiler enforces the part that matters: that every published field was written down on purpose.

```ts
type Envelope<T extends string, D> = {
  id: string;                      // evt_…, stable across retries
  type: T;
  api_version: "2026-03-01";
  occurred_at: string;             // RFC 3339, UTC
  data: D;
};

type Ref = { object: "order"; id: string; version: number };

export type OrderPaid = Envelope<"order.paid", Ref & {
  currency: string;
  amount_total: number;            // minor units, integer
  paid_at: string;
  customer: { id: string; email: string };
}>;

export type OrderCancelled = Envelope<"order.cancelled", Ref & {
  cancelled_at: string;
  reason: "requested_by_customer" | "payment_failed" | "fraud" | (string & {});
}>;

export type PublishedEvent = OrderPaid | OrderCancelled;
```

That `(string & {})` on `reason` is deliberate. It keeps autocomplete for the known values while declaring the enum *open*, which is the shape you want consumers to code against — and it's a reminder to whoever adds `reason: "chargeback"` next year that they're changing something consumers switch on.

```ts
function projectOrderPaid(o: Order, p: Payment): OrderPaid["data"] {
  return {
    object: "order",
    id: publicId(o.id),                    // "ord_88421", not 8841
    version: o.version,                    // monotonic; consumers discard older
    currency: o.currency,
    amount_total: o.totalMinorUnits,
    paid_at: p.capturedAt.toISOString(),
    customer: { id: publicId(o.customerId), email: o.customerEmail },
  };
}
```

Seven fields typed out by hand where `JSON.stringify(order)` would have been shorter. The tedium is the feature: this function is where `internal_risk_score` doesn't get published, and it's where the column somebody adds next Tuesday doesn't silently reach forty integrators. A projection is a review surface; a serializer is a leak with a schedule.

```ts
async function emit(tx: Tx, ev: PublishedEvent): Promise<void> {
  const body = JSON.stringify(ev);                 // rendered once, here
  await tx.insert("events", {
    id: ev.id, type: ev.type, body,                // stored as text, not jsonb
  });
  await fanOut(tx, ev);                            // 17.1a
}
```

Storing `body` as text rather than `jsonb` looks like a small thing and isn't. `jsonb` normalizes whitespace and reorders keys, so the bytes that come back out are not the bytes that went in — which invalidates a signature computed over the payload and means attempt #1 and attempt #9 are cryptographically different messages. If you want to query events by content, store the text for sending and a separate indexed column for querying.

```ts
it("order.paid payload matches the published schema", () => {
  const body = projectOrderPaid(fixtureOrder(), fixturePayment());
  expect(validate(publishedSchema("order.paid"), body)).toBe(true);
  expect(body).toMatchSnapshot();
});
```

The snapshot is the point of this test, more than the validation. A pull request that changes a projection shows a reviewer one line in a mapper; the snapshot diff shows them the bytes an integrator will receive tomorrow. That diff is the only moment in a normal development process where a human is confronted with a change to a public contract in the units the public sees it in.

---

The habit worth carrying: **anything leaving your system for somebody else's is a published interface, whether or not you wrote it down.** The test to run on any outbound payload, event or otherwise, is to pick a field and ask who breaks if you delete it tomorrow. If the answer is "no idea", the interface exists and is simply undocumented — which is the same position, minus the ability to change anything.

## Further Reading

- **Martin Fowler, "What do you mean by 'Event-Driven'?"** — the source of the notification / event-carried state transfer / event sourcing distinction, and the shortest correct treatment of why the first two are different design decisions.
- **Stripe's event object and API versioning documentation** — the reference implementation of per-endpoint pinned versions and `previous_attributes`, and worth studying specifically for what it cost them.
- **GitHub's webhook event catalogue** — a large, mature, publicly documented set of event types; useful for calibrating granularity before inventing your own.
- **CloudEvents specification** (CNCF), particularly the HTTP protocol binding and the required-attributes list.
- **Standard Webhooks** (standardwebhooks.com) — the envelope and header conventions assumed by the code in 17.1a.
- **Confluent's schema compatibility documentation** — backward, forward, and full compatibility as defined vocabulary. The mechanism doesn't transfer to JSON-over-HTTP, but the definitions are the clearest statement of what "safe change" means and are worth borrowing wholesale.
- **RFC 3339** for timestamps, and **JSON Schema** for the published contract.
- Within this map: delivery and retries in **17.1a**, signatures over raw bytes in **17.1c**, deprecation process in **33**, and erasure obligations in **34**.

A Java sibling would reveal something this article can only assert: `FAIL_ON_UNKNOWN_PROPERTIES` defaults to enabled in Jackson, which means the "adding a field is safe" rule is false for a large fraction of enterprise consumers unless they've configured otherwise, and `@JsonEnumDefaultValue` is the open-enum fix. Watching an additive change break a strongly-typed consumer is more convincing than being told it might. Not yet written.
