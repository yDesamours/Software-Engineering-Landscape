# Calling Other People's APIs

_Module 17.3b — Webhooks & Integration APIs_

## The Problem

Your product syncs contacts with whatever CRM the customer already uses. The first integration took a week and looked like this:

```java
@Value("${hubspot.api.key}") String apiKey;
```

One key, in the environment, for the design partner who asked for it. Everything after that is the bill for that line.

**The second customer breaks the model entirely.** Credentials are now per tenant, which means encrypted at rest, refreshable, revocable, and attached to a connection object that didn't exist. The refactor touches the scheduler, the job payloads, the error handling, and every call site.

**Then the connection dies for no visible reason.** A customer's sync stops. The logs show `invalid_grant`. What happened: the access token expired while thirty jobs for that tenant were in flight, all thirty noticed at once, and all thirty called the refresh endpoint. The provider rotates refresh tokens on use — exactly the scheme 17.3a recommended you implement — so the first response invalidated the token the other twenty-nine were still holding. The connection is unrecoverable and the customer has to re-authorize. Nobody can tell them why.

**One customer's backfill stops everyone's sync.** The provider's rate limit is per *application*, not per customer. A new enterprise account imports 400,000 contacts, consumes the hourly quota in eleven minutes, and every other customer's integration returns `429` for the rest of the hour. Their limit has no concept of your tenants. Fairness among them is entirely your problem, and you don't currently have any.

**Their bad afternoon is your outage.** The contact panel calls their API synchronously during page render. Their p99 goes to eight seconds. Your dashboard times out, your error rate alarms fire, and your status page conversation is about an incident you didn't have.

**Your retries make it worse.** They return `503`; your client retries five times; you run forty pods; you are now generating a sustained load spike against a partner who is already down. Eventually they block your application, which affects every tenant.

**And the quiet one.** A customer revoked your app in the provider's admin console nine weeks ago. Every sync since has failed with a `401`. No alert existed, because the failure is per-tenant and nobody set a threshold on it. You find out when they ask why their data looks old.

Underneath all six: **you have taken a hard dependency whose availability, latency, quotas, semantics, and lifecycle are controlled by an organization that has no contract with you, no awareness of your incidents, and no obligation to tell you anything before it changes.** Everything below is about containing that.

## What It Is

The outbound side of integration: your system as an API *client*, at multi-tenant scale. Three concerns that teams merge into one class and shouldn't, because they fail independently and are fixed in different places:

- **Connection management** — one credential per tenant per provider, with a lifecycle: granted, refreshing, expired, revoked, needs re-authorization.
- **Call discipline** — timeouts, retries, quota, isolation. How you behave as a guest in someone else's system, and how their bad day is prevented from becoming yours.
- **Synchronization** — getting their data into a form you can use, and keeping it correct as it changes.

Before any of those, there's a fork that determines nearly everything else: **do you proxy or do you sync?** Read through to them on demand, or replicate into your own store. That decision is made early, usually implicitly, and it's expensive to reverse.

This is the mirror of Module 7 (you as the server) and the outbound counterpart to 17.2b (their events arriving at you). In practice the three combine: their webhook tells you something changed, their API tells you what it is, and your store is where it lands.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Provider credentials are in environment variables and you have more than one customer.
- Token refresh happens inline, in whichever request happened to notice the expiry.
- A connection has died and the only remedy you could offer was "please reconnect," with no explanation.
- One customer's activity has visibly degraded another customer's integration.
- Third-party calls happen inside HTTP handlers, or worse, inside database transactions.
- You cannot state your current consumption against a provider's quota without triggering a `429`.
- A provider's incident has been discussed internally as your outage.
- No alert exists for "this integration has produced nothing in six hours."
- Your sync uses an `updated_since` timestamp, and records have gone missing.
- You can't attribute the provider's bill to tenants.
- Retry counts are configured in two layers, by two people, and nobody has multiplied them.

## How It Works

### Fork one: proxy or sync

**Proxy** — fetch on demand, render, discard. Always fresh. No storage, no conflict resolution, no deletion problem, no obligation to hold their customer's data. In exchange: their latency is in your path, their availability is your availability, their rate limit meets your traffic pattern directly, and you can never search, join, or aggregate across what you can't see.

**Sync** — copy into your schema. Enables search, cross-provider joins, analytics, and functioning while they're down. In exchange you take on staleness, conflict resolution, deletion detection, storage, reconciliation, and custody of data that belongs to your customer's customers.

The deciding question is narrow: **do you need to search, join, or aggregate?** If a user only ever views one record at a time in direct response to an action, proxy and cache. If your product's value is a view across everything, sync. Most real systems end up hybrid — sync the index, fetch the detail, write through on mutation — and that's a legitimate destination rather than a failure to decide.

### Connection management

A connection is a first-class object: *(tenant, provider, external account id, encrypted credentials, granted scopes, status, connected_by, last_success_at, last_error)*.

**Encrypt credentials** with envelope encryption and a key you can rotate, and make sure they can't leak sideways — HTTP client debug logging and error reporters both capture request headers by default, and a bearer token in an exception payload is a credential in your error tracker.

**Refresh under a lock that works across processes.** This is the fix for the stampede in the Problem section, and the important word is *across*. An in-process mutex or a `ConcurrentHashMap` of locks looks like a solution, passes review, works perfectly on one machine, and fails the moment you run two pods — with a symptom (dead connection, customer must re-authorize) that nobody traces back to concurrency. With rotating refresh tokens, losing the race isn't a wasted call, it's data loss.

**Refresh proactively**, at something like 75% of the token's lifetime, rather than reactively on the first `401`. Keep the `401` path as a fallback, because providers expire tokens early for their own reasons.

**Model revocation as a status, not an error.** The customer can disconnect your app in the provider's console at any time, and their admin can do it on their behalf. You find out as a specific error code on the next call. Map it to `needs_reauth`, stop retrying, and surface it in your UI with a reconnect button and a plain-language explanation. This single piece of product does more for integration support load than any amount of retry tuning.

**Expect several connections per tenant** — two Slack workspaces, three mailboxes — and record which external account each one is, or you'll discover later that you can't tell them apart.

### Call discipline

Conceptually, a stack around every outbound call:

```
  fairness limiter      per (tenant, provider)   ← yours; their quota can't see your tenants
  global budget         per provider, fleet-wide ← matches their documented quota
  circuit breaker       per provider             ← fail fast when they're down
  bulkhead              concurrency cap          ← their slowness ≠ your outage
  timeout + retry       idempotent calls only
```

**Their quota is per application, so fairness is your invention.** The global budget has to be shared across your fleet — a token bucket in Redis, not a counter per process — and it should have lanes: reserve headroom for interactive calls so a background backfill can't starve a user waiting on a page. Same idea as the replay priority in 17.2a.

**Back off globally, not per call.** When one worker sees a `429` with a `Retry-After`, that's information about the whole application's standing with that provider. If each of forty pods has to discover the limit independently, you spend the recovery window continuing to hit it.

**A circuit breaker is about your resources, not theirs.** When a provider is down, the cost you're avoiding is ten thousand queued jobs each holding a worker for a thirty-second timeout — the same occupancy arithmetic as 10.1a and the head-of-line problem in 17.1a. Fail fast, and have a defined fallback: serve synced data with a staleness label, or degrade the feature visibly.

**Keep third-party calls out of the request path** by default, and absolutely out of database transactions. When a user-facing call is unavoidable, give it a hard one-to-two-second timeout and design the degraded state deliberately.

**Retry only what's safe.** Idempotent reads, freely. Writes only with the provider's idempotency key if they offer one — and if they don't, a timeout on a create is ambiguous (17.1a's lesson from the other side) and needs a read-back to resolve rather than a blind retry.

### Synchronization mechanics

**Use their webhooks for latency and their API for completeness.** This is 17.2b's conclusion applied outbound, and the reconciliation job is the part that makes the webhooks safe to depend on.

**Cursors beat timestamps, and when you only get timestamps, overlap.** An `updated_since` filter has exactly the commit-visibility bug from 17.1a — a transaction that stamps a row before it commits leaves that row permanently behind your cursor. You're the victim of it now rather than the cause. The cheap fix is to re-read the last few minutes on every pass and rely on idempotent upserts to make the duplication free.

**Pagination shifts under you.** Offset pagination over a list ordered by `updated_at`, while records are being updated, silently skips rows as items move between pages. Prefer keyset or an opaque cursor; if only offsets exist, the overlap window is again the mitigation.

**Deletions are usually invisible.** A list endpoint returns what exists; nothing announces what stopped existing, so deleted records live in your database forever. Three options in order of preference: a deletions or changes endpoint if they publish one, delete events via webhook, or a periodic full pass that marks everything it saw and treats anything unseen as gone. The full pass is expensive, so it runs weekly rather than hourly — and it needs to be aware of failures, because a full pass that died halfway through must not delete the half it didn't reach.

**Keep the raw response.** Store their payload alongside your mapped columns, exactly as 17.2b argued for webhook bodies. The payoff is that a mapping bug becomes a re-run over local data rather than a re-fetch of two million records through a rate-limited API.

**Be careful with two-way sync.** Your write triggers their webhook, which triggers your write, which triggers their webhook. Echo loops are the default outcome, and last-write-wins quietly destroys data when both sides change. Mark the changes you originated — a source tag, or comparing their revision id against the one you just wrote — and prefer one-way sync unless the product genuinely requires otherwise.

### Observability

Per *(tenant, provider)*: last successful sync, error rate, records processed, quota consumed. Then the two that catch what thresholds don't:

**Absence alerting**, again. A connection producing nothing is indistinguishable from a quiet week unless someone declares what quiet means.

**A customer-facing integration health view** — last sync time, current status, the last error in language they can act on. It does for outbound integrations what the delivery log did for webhooks in 17.2a: it converts a support conversation into something the customer can resolve alone.

## When It's Appropriate

- **All of the connection machinery:** as soon as you call a provider on behalf of more than one customer. The cost scales with tenants, not with providers.
- **Sync rather than proxy:** when you need to search, join, or aggregate, or when your product must work while the provider doesn't.
- **Circuit breakers and bulkheads:** when the provider shares a worker pool with anything else you care about, which is nearly always.
- **Fairness limiting:** as soon as a single tenant's volume can plausibly consume a meaningful share of the application-wide quota — one enterprise backfill is usually enough.
- **Deletion reconciliation:** when a stale record is wrong rather than merely old. Contacts, permissions, and inventory all qualify.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **One provider, called a few times a day by a batch job.** A timeout, a retry, and an alert on failures is the entire correct solution.
- **A one-time migration.** Don't build a sync engine to import once; write the import.
- **Circuit breakers on something you call hourly.** The breaker never accumulates enough signal to be meaningful; a timeout and an alert do more.
- **Full bidirectional sync when the customer only reads.** Half the machinery, none of the echo loops.
- **A provider-agnostic abstraction before the third provider.** The first two will teach you which differences matter; guessing produces an abstraction shaped like provider number one.
- **Syncing data you never query.** Storage plus obligations plus reconciliation, in exchange for nothing.

## Trade-offs

- **Every provider is a permanent operational surface.** Their deprecations, quota changes, auth migrations, and outages become items on your roadmap, scheduled by them.
- **Synced data is your customer's customers' data, in your database** — with residency, retention, and deletion obligations (Module 34), including deleting it when they disconnect.
- **Fairness limiting means deliberately slowing one customer to protect others**, which looks like your product being slow unless you say otherwise in the UI.
- **A circuit breaker serving stale data serves confident wrong answers.** Staleness has to be visible, or you've traded an obvious failure for a silent one.
- **Keeping raw responses costs storage** and adds another table with a retention policy.
- **Per-tenant credentials mean key management** — encryption, rotation, access control — that a single environment variable didn't.
- **Debugging is three-party again**, and unlike the webhook case you have no access at all to the other side's logs.
- **Maintenance grows faster than the integration count.** N providers times M capabilities, each with its own pagination quirk, rate-limit shape, and deletion semantics. This is precisely the pressure that makes 17.4's build-versus-partner-versus-platform question unavoidable.

## Alternatives

**Unified API vendors** — Merge, Nango, Apideck, Paragon and similar — give you one normalized interface across many providers in a category, plus credential handling and the sync loop. The gain in time-to-market is genuine and large. The costs are a lowest-common-denominator data model, another party holding your customers' data, per-connection pricing, and inheriting their coverage gaps at the worst possible moment: the enterprise deal that depends on one custom field their mapping doesn't expose.

**Managed ETL** — Fivetran, Airbyte and the like — when the destination is a warehouse and the purpose is analytics rather than a product feature. Wrong tool for anything in a request path.

**Build inside their ecosystem.** A Slack app, a Salesforce managed package, a Shopify app. The data stays where it is, the platform handles auth and distribution, and you inherit their review process and their limits.

**Let the customer push to you.** Their scheduled export, a file drop, a call to your API. Shifts the operational burden to the party that actually controls the source system, and is what larger customers frequently prefer anyway.

**Push it to the customer's automation platform** — a Zapier or Make connector instead of a native integration, which is 17.4's subject.

**Don't integrate.** CSV import and export covers more real customer need than teams expect, at a small fraction of the cost, and it works with every system in the world including the ones with no API.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — One provider, done properly.** Credentials per tenant and encrypted, timeouts on every call, bounded retries, an alert on the aggregate failure rate. *Move when:* a connection dies and you can't explain why.

**L1 — Connections as objects.** Status including `needs_reauth`, surfaced in the customer's UI with a reconnect action; refresh behind a cross-process lock; all provider calls in background jobs rather than request handlers. *Move when:* one tenant's volume affects another's.

**L2 — Isolation and fairness.** A fleet-wide quota budget with per-tenant fairness and reserved interactive headroom, a circuit breaker and concurrency cap per provider, raw responses stored, incremental sync with an overlap window and idempotent upserts. *Move when:* stale records start being wrong rather than old.

**L3 — Correctness and visibility.** Deletion reconciliation, resumable backfills on a low-priority lane with progress the customer can see, per-tenant integration health, cost attribution. *Move when:* the third provider arrives.

**L4 — Generalize or buy.** A provider abstraction extracted from three working integrations, or a unified API vendor adopted with clear eyes about the data model.

Two things are painful to retrofit: **the per-tenant credential model** and **storing raw responses**, because neither can be reconstructed from what you didn't keep.

## Implementation

Java, because the failure isolation primitives have names here — Resilience4j's bulkhead, circuit breaker, and rate limiter — and seeing them named separately is most of the concept.

**Refreshing without racing:**

```java
@Transactional
public AccessToken accessTokenFor(UUID connectionId) {
    Connection c = repo.lockForUpdate(connectionId);          // SELECT … FOR UPDATE

    if (c.expiresAt().isAfter(Instant.now().plus(SKEW))) {
        return c.accessToken();                               // refreshed while we waited
    }

    TokenPair fresh = provider.refresh(c.refreshToken());     // rotates the refresh token
    repo.save(c.withTokens(fresh));
    return fresh.access();
}
```

The re-check after acquiring the lock is the entire point: thirty threads queue, one refreshes, twenty-nine read the new token and proceed. Without it you've serialized the stampede rather than prevented it.

Two notes on the shape. An in-process lock keyed by connection id is the natural-looking alternative and it is wrong as soon as there are two instances — and the symptom isn't a performance problem, it's a permanently broken connection, because the losing refresh invalidates the winner's token. And yes, this holds a database row lock across a network call, which is normally a serious mistake: it's deliberate here, it must be paired with a short client timeout, and it's the lesser evil compared to losing a refresh-token race.

**Wrapping the call:**

```java
Supplier<Response> call = Decorators.ofSupplier(() -> http.get(url, token))
    .withBulkhead(bulkheads.bulkhead("hubspot"))                       // concurrency cap
    .withCircuitBreaker(breakers.circuitBreaker("hubspot"))            // fail fast when down
    .withRateLimiter(limiters.rateLimiter("hubspot:" + tenantId))      // local fairness
    .withRetry(retries.retry("hubspot-read"))                          // GETs only
    .decorate();
```

Read this as a chain with one structural requirement: the concurrency cap and the breaker sit outside the retry, so a retry storm can't multiply through them — five retries behind an unbounded concurrency limit is five times the load on a provider that's already failing.

And note what this snippet gets wrong on purpose, because it's the version teams ship: the rate limiter is in-process. It enforces fairness within one JVM and nothing across the fleet. The application-wide budget has to live in shared state — a Redis token bucket keyed by provider, with the `Retry-After` from any `429` written back into it so the whole fleet slows at once.

**Incremental sync:**

```java
Instant since = state.cursor().minus(OVERLAP);   // re-read the last few minutes, deliberately
String page = null;
do {
    var res = provider.listContacts(since, page);
    for (var ext : res.items()) {
        jdbc.update("""
            INSERT INTO contacts (tenant_id, provider, external_id, external_revision, raw, synced_at)
            VALUES (?, ?, ?, ?, ?::jsonb, now())
            ON CONFLICT (tenant_id, provider, external_id) DO UPDATE
               SET external_revision = EXCLUDED.external_revision,
                   raw               = EXCLUDED.raw,
                   synced_at         = now()
             WHERE contacts.external_revision < EXCLUDED.external_revision
            """, tenantId, PROVIDER, ext.id(), ext.revision(), ext.rawJson());
    }
    page = res.nextPage();
} while (page != null);

state.setCursor(res.serverTime());   // their clock, never yours
```

Four decisions in twenty lines. The overlap window costs a few duplicate rows per pass and covers the commit-visibility gap that would otherwise drop records permanently. The `WHERE external_revision <` predicate is 17.1b's version guard pointed at somebody else's data, so pages arriving out of order or a retried job can't move a record backwards. The cursor comes from the provider's own reported time, because your clock and theirs are unrelated and a few seconds of skew is a silent data-loss window. And `raw` is stored so that the next mapping bug is a local re-run rather than another million API calls.

---

The habit worth carrying: **integrations look like features and behave like infrastructure.** They're scoped like a feature, estimated like a feature, and demoed like a feature — and then they need quotas, isolation, health monitoring, reconciliation, and an on-call story, forever, per provider. Before adding one, the five questions to have answers for are what happens when it's slow, when it's down, when it's rate-limited, when it returns something wrong, and when it silently stops. The fifth is the one that gets discovered by a customer.

## Further Reading

- **Michael Nygard, _Release It!_** — the canonical source for circuit breakers, bulkheads, timeouts, and the failure cascades they exist to stop. Most of the call-discipline section is this book applied to a multi-tenant client.
- **Resilience4j documentation** — useful even outside Java as a well-named catalogue of the primitives and how they compose.
- **AWS Architecture Blog, "Exponential Backoff and Jitter"** — the standard reference for why synchronized retries are the problem and jitter is the fix, referenced here from 10.2.
- **RFC 6585** for `429`, and the rate-limit documentation of any large API — Stripe, Shopify, and Google all publish their quota models, and comparing three of them is the fastest way to learn which shapes exist.
- **Documentation from a unified-API vendor** (Merge or Nango) — worth reading specifically for their list of per-provider quirks, which is an honest inventory of what you're taking on per integration.
- Within this map: retries and dead-lettering in **10.2**, caching in **13**, receiving their events in **17.2b**, obtaining the credentials in **17.3a**, inbound rate limiting in **18**, observability in **19**, data obligations in **34**, and circuit breakers in depth in **36.3**.

A TypeScript sibling would show something the Java version structurally hides. With a thread pool, a slow provider exhausts threads and the failure is a starved pool you can see in a dump; on Node there's no pool to exhaust, so the same slow provider instead accumulates sockets, timers, and pending promises until memory or file descriptors run out — a bulkhead there is a semaphore you have to add, not a pool you have to size. The concepts are identical; the symptom and the reason you need the limit are not. Not yet written.
