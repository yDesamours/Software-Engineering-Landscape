# Delegated Access for Third-Party Apps

_Module 17.3a — Webhooks & Integration APIs_

## The Problem

Your product has an API and API keys. A customer generates one in Settings, and it works: `Authorization: Bearer ak_live_7f2c…`, full access, no expiry.

Then other people's software starts wanting in. A reporting vendor. An agency that manages twelve of your customers. An automation platform. Each one has an onboarding screen that says *paste your Acme API key here*, and your customers paste it, because that's the only mechanism you offer.

Look at what each of those pastes actually did.

**It granted everything.** The customer wanted their orders synced to a spreadsheet tool. The key they handed over can read every customer record, delete invoices, change billing details, and mint more keys. There is no way to express "only orders, only reading" because the key has exactly one scope, which is *all of it*.

**It moved a credential to a place neither of you controls.** The key is now in the vendor's database, their logs, their engineers' laptops during debugging, and the support ticket where their CS agent asked the customer to re-send it. You told the customer that the key is a secret and then built a workflow whose only step is giving the secret away.

**It destroyed attribution.** Your audit log says `ak_live_7f2c` deleted four thousand records at 02:14. Which of the three vendors? Which of the vendor's employees? Their sync job, or their support tool? The key is a single identity worn by everyone who has a copy of it.

**It made revocation all-or-nothing.** Six months later, one vendor has a breach. Revoking the key breaks the other two vendors and the customer's own CI pipeline, so the customer schedules it for "after the quarter," and it doesn't happen.

**It outlived everyone.** The employee who created the key left in March. The vendor's contract ended in June. The key works fine. It will work fine in 2031.

And from your side, the aggregate is worse than any individual case. **You cannot see your own ecosystem.** How many third parties integrate with your API? Which ones? You can't answer, because they're indistinguishable from customers' own scripts. When one vendor's retry loop generates forty percent of your API traffic, it appears as five hundred unrelated customers. When you deprecate an endpoint (Module 33), you email your customers — not the developer who actually has to change the code, whose existence you can't confirm. And when that vendor ships a bug, the resulting tickets arrive at your support desk describing an outage you don't have.

The thing the customer was trying to do, in plain language, is: *this specific third party may read my orders, nothing else, until I say stop.* An API key cannot express a single clause of that sentence. Not "this specific third party," not "only orders," not "until I say stop."

## What It Is

The problem is **delegation**: letting software act on a user's behalf, with less authority than the user has, revocably, and identifiably. OAuth 2.0 is the protocol built for it, and the roles are worth naming explicitly because this is the reverse of how Module 4 used the same word.

There, you were the **client** — "Log in with Google" made you the party receiving delegated access. Here, you are the **authorization server** (you issue tokens and run the consent screen) and the **resource server** (your API enforces them). The third-party app is the client. Your customer is the **resource owner**, and that name carries the whole idea: the data is theirs, and only they can grant access to it.

Three distinctions that get conflated constantly:

**OAuth is not authentication.** It grants access to resources. OpenID Connect is the identity layer built on top of it, and it's what "Sign in with…" means. A vendor who needs both needs both; using an access token as proof of identity is a well-documented way to get it wrong.

**Delegation is not credential sharing.** The point of the flow below is that the customer's password, and any credential of theirs, never reaches the vendor at all.

**API keys are not obsolete.** They remain the right answer for a customer's own scripts and server-to-server automation. The two mechanisms answer different questions: *an API key answers "is this the account's own automation?" and OAuth answers "is this a third party my user chose to trust, for these specific operations, revocably?"* Most mature APIs offer both, forever.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Customers paste API keys into third-party websites, and your documentation tells them to.
- Your key model has one scope, and that scope is everything.
- Your audit log cannot say which vendor performed an action.
- Somebody has asked for read-only keys.
- A customer asked to cut off one vendor and the answer was "rotate the key," which breaks the others.
- You cannot state how many third-party integrations exist against your API.
- A single vendor's traffic spike is indistinguishable from organic growth.
- You have no way to email integration developers before a breaking change.
- A partner asked for a sandbox and you had nothing to offer but a real account.
- Security questionnaires ask how customers scope and revoke third-party access.
- A breach at one vendor would expose full-access credentials for every customer they share with you.

## How It Works

### The authorization code flow

```
 user, in the vendor's app        clicks "Connect Acme"
        │
        ▼
 GET  acme.com/oauth/authorize?client_id=…&redirect_uri=…&scope=orders:read
                               &state=…&code_challenge=…
        │   ← the user authenticates on YOUR domain, with YOUR login
        │   ← consent screen: which app, which account, which permissions
        ▼
 302 back to the vendor:  ?code=…&state=…
        │
        ▼
 POST acme.com/oauth/token   (code + client_secret + code_verifier)
        │
        ▼
 access_token  (minutes)   refresh_token  (long-lived)
        │
        ▼
 GET acme.com/v1/orders   Authorization: Bearer …
```

The essential move is in the middle: **the user authenticates on your domain and the vendor never sees a credential.** Everything else in the protocol exists to make that exchange safe.

The details that are not optional:

- **Authorization code, never the implicit grant.** Tokens in URL fragments leak through browser history, referrers, and logs. The implicit grant is deprecated; don't implement it.
- **PKCE for every client**, confidential ones included. It costs a hash and closes code interception.
- **`state`**, checked on return, for CSRF on the redirect.
- **Exact redirect URI matching.** No wildcards, no prefix matching, no "starts with the registered origin." Loose redirect matching combined with any open redirect anywhere on your domain is the classic path to stolen authorization codes, and it's the most exploited registration weakness there is.
- **A consent screen that names four things**: the app, who publishes it, which account or workspace is being connected, and what the app will be able to do, in a sentence a non-engineer can evaluate.

Two other grants are worth knowing without dwelling on: **client credentials** for a vendor acting as itself with no user involved, and **device code** for CLIs and anything without a browser.

### Scopes

Scopes are how "less authority than the user has" gets expressed, and they're a public API — you can add them, but renaming or removing one breaks live integrations, and tokens already issued keep whatever they were granted.

Design them as capabilities a person can evaluate, not as endpoint lists: `orders:read`, `orders:write`, `customers:read`. Coarse enough that the consent screen is readable, fine enough that it isn't a lie. Two hundred scopes means nobody reads the screen; two scopes means the screen says "this app can do everything" while pretending otherwise. Read/write separation per resource is the minimum that's worth having.

**Incremental authorization** — asking for more scopes later, when the feature that needs them is used — beats requesting everything at install time. Better conversion, and it keeps the initial consent honest.

And then the rule people ship without: **a scope is a ceiling on authority, not a source of it.**

```
effective authority = granted scopes  ∩  the granting user's CURRENT permissions
```

Scopes were agreed once, possibly two years ago. Permissions change. An admin connects an app with `invoices:write`, is later demoted to read-only, and the app keeps writing invoices because nothing re-evaluated the grant. Both checks have to run on every request, and the second one has to read live state.

Related: bind the token to a specific account or workspace, not merely to a user who belongs to several. "Which tenant is this token for" must never be inferable from context.

### Tokens

**Short access tokens, long refresh tokens.** Minutes to an hour for the former; the latter is what makes revocation meaningful, because it's the thing that can be destroyed.

**Opaque or JWT** is a real decision. A JWT needs no database lookup, which is attractive at volume — but it also means revocation is a promise you can't keep until expiry, because a self-contained token is valid as long as the signature checks out. For third-party access, where "revoke this vendor now" is a feature customers will exercise during incidents, the default should be opaque tokens with a cached lookup, or JWTs with a TTL short enough that the lie is brief.

**Store tokens hashed**, with SHA-256 rather than bcrypt. This looks wrong to anyone coming from password storage, and the reasoning is that password hashing is deliberately slow because passwords carry perhaps thirty bits of entropy. A token is 256 bits of CSPRNG output; nobody is brute-forcing it. The threat being defended against is a database dump being *directly usable*, and a fast hash handles that while remaining cheap enough to run on every API request.

**Prefix your tokens** — `acme_at_…`, `acme_rt_…`. It costs nothing and buys two things: secret-scanning services can recognize and report leaked credentials, and support can identify a token's type from a screenshot without the screenshot being a usable credential.

**Rotate refresh tokens, and detect reuse.** Every refresh issues a new refresh token and invalidates the old one. If an already-used token is presented, either it was stolen or the legitimate client is replaying after a theft — you can't tell which, so you assume compromise, revoke the entire token family, and notify the account owner. This turns a stolen refresh token from permanent access into a detectable event.

**Expire idle grants.** A refresh token unused for ninety days should die. The trade-off is real: an integration that runs quarterly will break, so the policy needs a notification before the axe falls.

Finally, implement **revocation** (RFC 7009) and, if partners need it, **introspection** (RFC 7662), and bind refresh tokens to the client that received them.

### App registration, which is the thing you actually gained

The record you create for each third-party app — name, publisher, contact email, redirect URIs, requested scopes, privacy policy — is the answer to half the problems in the Problem section. It's how you email developers before a breaking change, how you attribute traffic, how you rate-limit a misbehaving integration without punishing its customers, and how you know your ecosystem exists at all.

Around it: a developer account distinct from a customer account, a sandbox where an app can be built before any customer has installed it, client secret rotation, and — once apps request sensitive scopes — a review process. Unverified apps limited to a handful of installations is the standard mechanism, and it's what keeps consent phishing from scaling.

### Installations are first-class objects

A grant is a relationship: *(app, account, scopes, granting user, created, last used)*. Expose it on both sides.

Customers get a "Connected apps" page showing what each app can do, when it last did anything, and a revoke button. The last-used timestamp does more work than it looks like — it's what lets a customer confidently remove the eleven integrations nobody remembers approving.

You get install counts per app, which is your ecosystem metric and your deprecation mailing list.

And one detail that generates support tickets everywhere it's missed: **the grant usually belongs to the user who authorized it**, so when that employee leaves and their account is deactivated, the company's integration dies. Either support transferring a grant to another user, or let apps install against an account-level identity rather than a personal one.

### Rate limits across an ecosystem

Limit per *(app, account)* pair, not per account and not per app alone. A buggy app must not exhaust a customer's entire quota, and a popular app must not be able to take down your API across all of its customers simultaneously. Module 18 has the mechanics; what's new here is that you now have a developer to actually contact when their retry loop goes wrong, which is the real control.

### What this adds to your attack surface

Your authorization server becomes a high-value target. Exact redirect matching, no open redirects anywhere on the domain, CSRF protection and clickjacking defences on the consent screen, and hard rate limits on the token endpoint.

The contemporary attack isn't protocol-level, though — it's **consent phishing**. A malicious app with a plausible name runs a completely legitimate OAuth flow, and the user clicks Allow. The protocol works perfectly and the outcome is a breach. Defences are product decisions, not cryptographic ones: app verification, scopes described in blunt language, install caps for unverified apps, anomaly detection on sudden install spikes, and — the one enterprise customers will insist on — **admin controls letting an organization decide which apps its users may install at all.**

## When It's Appropriate

- Third parties will act on behalf of your customers: partners, agencies, automation platforms, a marketplace.
- Customers have asked for scoped or independently revocable access.
- A typical customer has more than one integration, which is what makes per-vendor revocation meaningful.
- Enterprise buyers require admin control over which apps their staff can connect.
- You need to be able to identify and contact the developers building against your API.
- You're planning a marketplace or app directory (17.4), which cannot exist without this.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Only your own frontend and your customers' own scripts use the API.** Scoped, labelled, expiring API keys are the right tool and a fraction of the work.
- **One partner, under contract, server-to-server.** Client credentials, or mTLS and a key. A consent flow for a relationship with a signed agreement behind it is ceremony.
- **Internal service-to-service calls**, which your mesh or workload identity already handles.
- **Building the authorization server yourself.** The protocol is a solved problem with conformant implementations in every ecosystem; the parts that carry your product's value are scope design, consent UX, and enforcement, and none of those come from writing your own token endpoint.
- **A marketplace before two integrations exist.** Build the first two by hand, then generalize what they had in common.

## Trade-offs

- **You are now operating an authorization server** — security-critical, standards-conformant, always-up, permanently interesting to attackers, and subject to audit.
- **The consent screen is where security outcomes are actually decided**, which means a copywriting decision is a security control. Users click through anything that looks routine.
- **Scope names are permanent.** They're in other people's source code.
- **Token validation is on the hot path** of every API request, which pushes you toward caching and gives cache invalidation a security consequence.
- **Developer relations becomes a function.** App review queues, sandbox support, and documentation for people who are not your customers and cannot be handled by your customer support process.
- **Every issue now has three parties.** Customer, vendor, you — and two of them will conclude it's the third's fault. Delivery logs and per-app request logs are what settle it.
- **Refresh rotation breaks naive clients.** Two of the vendor's processes refreshing concurrently looks exactly like token theft, and without a grace window you will revoke legitimate integrations daily until someone disables the feature.
- **You still cannot stop a user from authorizing a bad app.** You can only make the decision informed, and give admins a way to constrain it.

## Alternatives

**Scoped, expiring API keys with labels and per-key audit.** Most of the operational benefit for a tenth of the work: revoke one integration without breaking the others, see which key did what, force expiry. What it can't do is prevent the credential from being handed over, which means it's a complement rather than a replacement — and it's the right first step regardless.

**Personal access tokens**, the GitHub model — user-owned, scoped, expiring, obviously not a shared account credential. A genuinely useful intermediate step that many APIs never outgrow.

**The app/installation model.** GitHub Apps are the reference: the app has its own identity and its own permission set, is installed on an account rather than authorized by a person, and receives short-lived installation tokens. It survives the granting employee leaving, has clearer attribution, and fits server-to-server integrations better than user-delegated OAuth does. GitHub offers both because they answer different questions — user-delegated for "act as me", installation for "act as this integration" — and if most of your integrations are the second kind, this may be the better primary model.

**SAML and SCIM.** Frequently confused with this, and a different problem: identity federation and user provisioning, not resource delegation. If the request is "manage our users from our IdP," OAuth is the wrong answer.

**mTLS or signed requests** for a small number of high-trust partners, where certificate management is acceptable and consent is a contract rather than a screen.

**An identity vendor** — Auth0, Okta, Keycloak, Ory Hydra, Spring Authorization Server. The default recommendation for the protocol layer. You still own client registration, scope design, the consent experience, and enforcement in your API, which is where the work and the risk actually live.

**Build the integrations yourself instead.** Sometimes correct, and it's the question 17.4 opens with.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Scoped API keys.** Labelled, revocable individually, with expiry and an audit trail that records which key acted. *Move when:* someone pastes one into a third party's website, which has probably already happened.

**L1 — Authorization code with PKCE, by hand, for one or two partners.** A handful of scopes, opaque short-lived access tokens, rotating refresh tokens, a connected-apps page with a revoke button, apps registered manually by you. *Move when:* a third developer asks for access.

**L2 — Self-serve developer surface.** App registration, sandbox credentials, installation objects with last-used timestamps, per-*(app, account)* rate limits. *Move when:* you can't name every integration from memory.

**L3 — Governance.** App verification for sensitive scopes, install caps for unverified apps, admin allowlisting for enterprise accounts, revocation and introspection endpoints, incremental authorization. *Move when:* the first enterprise security review asks who can connect what.

**L4 — Ecosystem.** Marketplace, install analytics, a developer communication channel, and the partner programme that comes with it — 17.4.

Two things are much easier to loosen than to tighten, so start strict: **scope names** and **exact redirect URI matching**.

## Implementation

Go. Not the protocol plumbing — use a conformant implementation for that — but the three pieces you own regardless of which one you use.

```go
// acme_at_… / acme_rt_… : the prefix makes leaked tokens findable by secret
// scanners and identifiable in a screenshot without being usable.
func newToken(prefix string) (plain string, hash []byte) {
	buf := make([]byte, 32)
	if _, err := rand.Read(buf); err != nil {
		panic(err) // a CSPRNG failure is not a recoverable condition
	}
	plain = prefix + base64.RawURLEncoding.EncodeToString(buf)
	sum := sha256.Sum256([]byte(plain))
	return plain, sum[:]
}
```

SHA-256, not bcrypt, and the reasoning is worth keeping straight because the instinct from password storage points the other way. Slow hashing exists to make guessing a low-entropy secret expensive. This secret is 256 bits of randomness — guessing is not the threat. The threat is a leaked database being immediately usable, which a single fast hash prevents, at a cost you can afford on every request.

```go
func Refresh(ctx context.Context, tx *sql.Tx, presented string) (*TokenPair, error) {
	h := sha256.Sum256([]byte(presented))

	var t RefreshToken
	err := tx.QueryRowContext(ctx,
		`SELECT id, grant_id, family_id, used_at, expires_at
		   FROM refresh_tokens WHERE token_hash = $1 FOR UPDATE`, h[:],
	).Scan(&t.ID, &t.GrantID, &t.FamilyID, &t.UsedAt, &t.ExpiresAt)
	if errors.Is(err, sql.ErrNoRows) || t.Expired() {
		return nil, errInvalidGrant
	}

	if t.UsedAt.Valid {
		if time.Since(t.UsedAt.Time) < refreshGrace {
			return reissueFor(ctx, tx, t) // concurrent refresh by the same client
		}
		// An old token, presented long after it was exchanged. Either it was
		// stolen, or the legitimate client is replaying after someone else used
		// it. You cannot distinguish these, so assume the worse one.
		revokeFamily(ctx, tx, t.FamilyID)
		notifyGrantOwner(ctx, t.GrantID)
		return nil, errInvalidGrant
	}

	return rotate(ctx, tx, t) // mark used, issue a new pair in the same family
}
```

`refreshGrace` — thirty seconds or so — is what decides whether this feature survives contact with real clients. Two worker processes at the vendor refreshing the same token at the same instant is completely normal and, without the grace window, looks identical to theft. Teams that skip it revoke legitimate integrations every day for a week and then turn rotation off, which is the worst of both outcomes.

```go
func Authorize(r *http.Request, need Scope) error {
	t := tokenFrom(r.Context())

	if !t.Scopes.Has(need) {
		return errInsufficientScope // what the user agreed to, once
	}
	perms, err := permissions(r.Context(), t.AccountID, t.UserID)
	if err != nil {
		return err
	}
	if !perms.Allows(need) {
		return errForbidden // what that user is allowed to do right now
	}
	return nil
}
```

Two checks that look redundant and aren't. The first reads a promise made at consent time, possibly years ago and frozen since. The second reads live state. The gap between them is where the bug lives: the admin who authorized an app, was later demoted, and whose app kept writing because the token remembered an authority the user no longer had.

---

The habit worth extracting: **whenever one party acts on behalf of another, four questions need answers — who is acting, on whose behalf, with exactly what authority, and how does it stop.** API keys answer none of them, which is why the whole apparatus above exists. But the same four questions apply well outside third-party integrations: to service accounts, to admin impersonation features (Module 26), to CI credentials, and — most sharply and least resolved — to LLM agents calling tools with a user's authority (39.5), where the acting party is a model, the authority is whatever was in the token, and "how does it stop" is frequently unanswered.

## Further Reading

- **OAuth 2.0 Security Best Current Practice** (RFC 9700) — implement from this rather than from RFC 6749 alone; it consolidates two decades of attacks and removes the options you shouldn't use. **RFC 6749** remains the base specification, with **RFC 7636** (PKCE), **RFC 7009** (revocation), **RFC 7662** (introspection), and **RFC 8252** (native apps).
- **oauth.net** and Aaron Parecki's *OAuth 2.0 Simplified* — the clearest available orientation before reading the specifications, and the best summary of where OAuth 2.1 consolidates things.
- **GitHub's documentation comparing GitHub Apps and OAuth apps** — the most useful public treatment of the installation model versus user-delegated access, including why they kept both.
- **Slack's app scopes and admin-approval documentation** — a worked example of consent design plus enterprise allowlisting.
- **Google's OAuth app verification and sensitive-scope review process** — what app review looks like at scale, and a realistic picture of what you're signing up for at L3.
- Within this map: you as an OAuth *client* in **4.1**, rate limiting in **18**, the OWASP surface in **20**, audit logging in **24**, admin impersonation in **26**, deprecation in **33**, and the marketplace question in **17.4**.

A Java sibling would make a point this article can only assert. With Spring Authorization Server, the entire protocol — endpoints, PKCE, rotation, introspection — is configuration, and what remains to be written is exactly the part that matters: scope design, the consent screen, the effective-permission check, and the installation model. Seeing how little application code is left is the most convincing available argument against implementing the protocol yourself. Not yet written.
