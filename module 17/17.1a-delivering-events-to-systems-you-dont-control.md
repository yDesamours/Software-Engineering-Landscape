# Delivering Events to Systems You Don't Control

_Module 17.1a — Webhooks & Integration APIs_

## The Problem

A shipping-label API. Customers create shipments through it; hours or days later a carrier scan changes a shipment's status. Customers need to know, because a status change is what triggers the rest of their own workflow — email the buyer, release the next order, close the ticket.

The API is REST, so they poll.

```
GET /shipments?updated_since=2026-03-04T11:32:00Z
```

Here is what that costs, with real numbers.

```
4,000 integrating accounts
× 1 request / 30s
= 8,000 requests/min  ≈  133 req/s  ≈  11.5M requests/day

actual status changes: ~90,000/day  ≈  1/s
```

At most one poll in a hundred and thirty carries anything at all. The other 99.2% are the question *anything yet?* answered *no* — eleven million times a day, every one of them authenticated, tenant-resolved, and served by an indexed range scan on your busiest table. Your read load is a function of how anxious your customers are. Nothing in that number is related to how often your business does anything.

Then the second failure, which is the one customers actually complain about. **Average detection delay is half the poll interval.** Fifteen seconds at a 30-second cadence; five minutes at the cadence you'll eventually ask them to use. An account whose warehouse doors are gated on delivery confirmation finds that unacceptable and drops to every 5 seconds. They're right to. If every account did the same, you'd be serving 800 req/s of nothing.

So you rate-limit them. Now the customers who care most about freshness get the worst freshness, and you've built a system where the correct behaviour for a consumer and the interests of the producer point in opposite directions. Every conversation about it is adversarial, and neither side is wrong.

And then the third failure, which is quieter and worse: **most polling integrations silently lose events.** `updated_since` compares against `updated_at`, which is set when a transaction *starts writing*, while visibility arrives when it *commits*. A slow transaction stamps a row `11:31:58`, commits at `11:32:04`, and the consumer who already moved their cursor to `11:32:00` will never see that row again. The bug shows up as one missing shipment a month, is unreproducible, and gets attributed to your API. Correct polling needs a monotonic cursor over an append-only log, not a timestamp on a mutable row — which means the "simple" option requires the same event log as the complicated one.

Now look at the same moment from your side. The carrier scan lands on your webhook from UPS, and 40 milliseconds later your code is executing with the shipment id in a local variable. The fact the customer is asking for 2,880 times a day is in your process's memory, once, for free.

So send it to them.

```
POST https://customer.example.com/hooks/shipping
{ "id": "evt_01HX…", "type": "shipment.delivered", "shipment_id": "shp_88421" }
```

That's a five-line change to the handler, and it deletes every problem above. It also creates every problem in the rest of this article, because four things became true the moment that line landed:

1. **You are now an HTTP client to an address a customer typed into a form.** You have inherited their uptime, their TLS renewal process, their 30-second GC pause, their staging environment that returns `200` for everything and does nothing, and their reverse proxy that holds the connection open for four minutes.
2. **If that call is in the request handler, your latency now includes theirs** — and your availability multiplies with theirs, for a feature that has nothing to do with whether the scan was recorded.
3. **If it's before the commit you announce things that didn't happen; if it's after, you lose events** when the process dies in between. Two writes to two systems, no transaction across them.
4. **Fire-and-forget means a failed POST is an event nobody will ever see again** — and neither party knows it existed. The polling version had one genuinely good property: the consumer controlled completeness. You just took that away and put it on your side of the wall, where it is now your job.

## What It Is

The mechanism is called a **webhook**: an HTTP request the producer makes to a URL the consumer registered in advance. Push instead of pull. You'll also see *HTTP callback*, *reverse API*, and *event notification* for the same thing.

The word covers both directions, which causes real confusion in design discussions. *Receiving* webhooks — the Stripe integration in 15.2a — is a different job with different failure modes, and it gets its own article (17.2b). This one is about **sending** them, which is where nearly all the engineering lives, because the sender is the party that has to absorb the other side's failures.

The one-line version worth carrying: **a webhook system is a message queue whose consumers you do not operate, whose downtime you must survive, and whose delivery guarantee is at-least-once.** Every design decision below follows from one of those three clauses.

## Recognizing the Problem

You need outbound webhooks when:

- List endpoints with a `since` parameter dominate your traffic, and the overwhelming majority return empty arrays.
- Somebody has asked "how fast are we allowed to poll?", or you've raised a rate limit for an account that "needs fresher data".
- You have events whose value decays in seconds — payment result, build finished, moderation decision, incoming message — behind an interface whose floor is the poll interval.
- A support ticket says "we missed a status change", and the cause turns out to be a cursor over `updated_at`.
- An integrator has resorted to scraping your UI, parsing your notification emails, or asking for read replica access — all three mean your API has no way to say *something happened*.
- Read-replica load scales with the number of integrations rather than with product usage.

You've outgrown the version you have when:

- One customer's broken endpoint visibly delays deliveries to everybody else.
- You cannot answer "did you send us the event at 14:02?" without grepping application logs.
- Outbound sends still happen inside request handlers, and you've had an incident where a customer's slow endpoint consumed your web tier.
- The retry backlog is dominated by endpoints that have been dead for weeks.

## How It Works

```
  domain change
        │
        ▼
   event  (id, type, payload, occurred_at)        ← durable, written once
        │
        ├── match subscriptions ──┬── endpoint A
        │                         ├── endpoint B
        ▼                         └── endpoint C
   delivery rows  (event × endpoint)              ← one fate each
        │
   [ queue, partitioned by endpoint ]
        │
        ▼
   POST + hard timeout ──────────▶ customer endpoint
        │
   2xx ─┴─ non-2xx / timeout
    │            │
 delivered   backoff ──▶ exhausted ──▶ dead-lettered, endpoint disabled, human told
```

**An event is a record before it is a request.** Write it down, with an id you generate, and put that id in the payload. Every support conversation you will ever have about this system starts with somebody quoting an id at somebody else; if the id lives only in a log line, the conversation has no shared evidence and takes three days.

**An event is not a delivery.** One event fans out to however many endpoints subscribe to it, and each of those has an independent outcome: delivered on the first try, delivered on the ninth, or never. The unit of retry, of state, and of the delivery log is the *(event, endpoint)* pair. Systems that get this wrong store a `sent` boolean on the event and then cannot express "delivered to two of three subscribers".

**Getting it out of the transaction is a known problem with a known name.** Write the event row inside the same transaction as the state change, and deliver from that table asynchronously. That's the transactional outbox, and it belongs to Module 37 — the only thing to carry here is that the send must not be the thing that makes the event durable.

**Delivery is asynchronous, always.** A worker, never a request handler. Two reasons: the latency and availability coupling from the Problem, and the fact that retries need somewhere to live that outlives the request that caused them.

**You set the timeout, and it should be short.** Five to ten seconds, total — connect, TLS, write, read. The receiver's job is to acknowledge, not to work. A receiver that does its processing inline before returning `200` is the single most common cause of "your webhooks are unreliable", and the fix is on their side, which is why 17.2b exists and why your documentation should say it in the first paragraph.

**Classify the response, and document the classification.** `2xx` is delivered. `410 Gone` is the one status worth treating as a permanent instruction to stop. `429` and `5xx` are retryable, and if a `Retry-After` comes back, a number the receiver chose beats a formula you invented. Timeouts are the interesting case: a timeout is not a failure, it's an *absence of information*, and the request may well have been processed. That ambiguity is the entire reason duplicates exist, and no amount of engineering on your side removes it.

**Retries here have a horizon of days, not minutes.** Module 10.2 covers exponential backoff and jitter; what's specific to webhooks is the length. The failure being absorbed is a customer's bad deploy, an expired certificate discovered on Monday, an outage at their hosting provider. A dozen attempts spread over 24 to 72 hours is the industry-typical shape. That has a consequence for payload design that catches people out: **an event delivered eighteen hours late must still be interpretable**, which pushes you toward "here is what happened, at this time, to this id" and away from "here is the current state of the object". That's 17.1b's subject.

**Isolation is the design decision people skip.** One customer's endpoint starts black-holing — accepting connections, never responding. Their timeout is your 10 seconds. They have 200 events queued.

```
shared FIFO queue, 20 workers
  200 deliveries × 10s ÷ 20 workers = 100 seconds
  during which every other customer's events wait behind a company
  that isn't even receiving them
```

The fix is to partition the queue by endpoint: a concurrency limit per endpoint, a queue per endpoint, or a claim that leases one endpoint at a time. Whichever form, the property you need is that *the cost of a dead consumer is bounded and paid only by that consumer*.

**Ordering is a promise you probably shouldn't make.** Parallel delivery reorders by definition. Even strictly serial delivery reorders the moment anything is retried — event #4 fails and lands three minutes after #5. Three honest positions: don't promise ordering and tell consumers to reconcile against your API; serialize per endpoint or per entity, accepting that one stuck delivery blocks that entire stream; or attach a per-subscription sequence number so consumers can *detect* gaps and reordering themselves. Most established providers take the first and are right to — but say so explicitly, because a consumer who assumes ordering will build something that works in testing and corrupts data under load.

**Fan-out amplifies spikes.** A bulk import that touches 50,000 records produces 50,000 events, times every subscribed endpoint, arriving at a small business's Rails app in ninety seconds. You are now the aggressive client in someone else's rate-limiting article. Per-endpoint send rates, and coalescing where the domain allows it (`invoice.updated` ×40 in one minute is rarely forty pieces of news), are the two levers.

**Endpoints that are dead must eventually be declared dead.** After the retry budget has been exhausted repeatedly over days, disable the endpoint, stop generating deliveries for it, and tell a human by email — the integration's owner, not the account's billing contact. Without this, dead endpoints accumulate forever and their retry traffic eventually dominates the queue, which means the least valuable work in the system crowds out the most valuable.

**The delivery log is not observability, it's the product.** For every attempt: timestamp, request body, response status, a truncated response body, duration, attempt number. Exposed to the customer, with a replay button. The justification isn't debugging, it's support economics — without shared evidence, every integration bug becomes a two-party investigation where each side is confident and neither can prove anything.

Security gets its own article (17.1c), but note the shape now, because both halves are easy to leave until too late: the payload needs a signature over the raw body with a timestamp, and you are making HTTP requests to URLs that arbitrary users supply, which is a server-side request forgery primitive pointed at your own network.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Many independent consumers you don't operate.** This is the defining case. Two hundred integrators, each with their own deploy schedule and error handling, is a situation webhooks were designed for and a message broker is not.
- **The event rate is far below the aggregate poll rate.** A few events per account per day against hundreds of polls per account per day is a 100× reduction in request volume, and it's typical.
- **Freshness matters in seconds**: payment captured, build finished, document signed, alert fired, message received.
- **Consumers are internet-reachable servers.** SaaS integrating with SaaS.
- **The events are discrete and meaningful in the consumer's vocabulary** — `shipment.delivered`, not `shipments_row_updated`.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **One consumer, and you operate both sides.** Use a real message broker, or just call their API. You get ordering, backpressure, replay, and a consumer you can page — none of which a webhook gives you.
- **The consumer is a browser or a mobile app.** There is no URL to POST to. That's Module 9 (SSE, WebSockets) and 14.3 (push notifications).
- **Very high volume per consumer.** One HTTP request, with TLS, per event stops being efficient somewhere in the millions per day. Offer a streaming endpoint, a batch export, or a shared topic.
- **The consumer needs completeness more than latency.** Financial reconciliation, ledger mirroring, anything audited. A cursor-paginated log endpoint they pull is *stronger* here, because they can detect their own gaps and replay at will instead of trusting your retries. The best answer is frequently both: the webhook is a hint that says *look now*, the log API is the truth.
- **Your event vocabulary isn't stable yet.** Shipping webhooks publishes your internal model as a contract held by parties you cannot enumerate. If the domain is still being renamed every few weeks, wait — one released event type is harder to retire than fifty internal ones.
- **Cache invalidation between your own services.** Don't route internal concerns through the customer-facing delivery system. It's slower, weaker, and now a customer-visible incident.

## Trade-offs

What it costs you once you have it:

- **An outbound attack surface.** Your servers now make requests to arbitrary user-supplied URLs, from inside your network. That's SSRF by design, and it needs deliberate handling rather than a note in the backlog.
- **Support load moves onto you.** "Your webhooks are down" arrives as your incident, at your on-call. Nine times in ten it's their endpoint, and the only way to demonstrate that is infrastructure you have to build first.
- **The payload becomes a public API** — with no version negotiation at call time, and no way to know who is parsing which field. Deprecation is Module 33's subject and is meaningfully harder here, because you cannot see your callers' code and they will not read your changelog.
- **Storage is dominated by attempts, not events.** One event × 20 subscribers × up to a dozen attempts is 240 rows, each with a request body and a response snippet. The delivery log becomes one of your largest tables within a year, and it needs a retention policy chosen deliberately rather than discovered during a disk alert.
- **Duplicates and reordering are pushed onto consumers** — including the ones who didn't read the documentation. Their idempotency bugs become your support tickets.
- **Your throughput is coupled to strangers' p99.** Worker capacity is consumed by endpoints whose performance you can't influence, measure in advance, or fix.
- **Debugging is asymmetric.** You observe a `200`. You cannot observe that their handler caught an exception and returned it anyway. "Delivered" means the bytes were accepted, not that anything happened — the same lie SMTP's `250 OK` tells in 14.1.

## Alternatives

**Polling with a real cursor.** Underrated, and the fair comparison isn't against the naive `updated_at` version. Give consumers an append-only event log with a monotonic cursor: `GET /events?after=evt_01HX…`. They get completeness they can verify, no duplicates by construction (the cursor is idempotent), replay for free, and it works from behind a firewall with no inbound endpoint and no public TLS certificate. You give up latency and still pay for the requests. Many mature APIs ship both, and the log endpoint is what makes the webhook system's occasional failures survivable.

**Long polling.** The consumer's request hangs until an event arrives or a timeout fires. Removes most of the empty responses, keeps the pull model and the consumer's control over pacing. Costs a held connection per consumer and awkward interactions with every proxy between you.

**A streaming API** — SSE, a WebSocket, or a gRPC server stream with a resume token. The right answer when a small number of consumers each take a large volume. More infrastructure on both sides, and consumers now need long-lived connection handling, which many integrators are not equipped to write.

**Publish into the consumer's cloud account.** An EventBridge partner event source, a Pub/Sub topic, a Kafka topic with per-tenant ACLs. Delivery, retention, and replay become the cloud provider's problem, and large enterprise customers often prefer it. Requires them to be on that cloud, and gives you a second integration surface to operate.

**Managed webhook infrastructure** — Svix, Hookdeck, Convoy and similar. Everything in the *How to Achieve It* ladder below, bought. A legitimate decision, especially at L3; the cost is that the component your customers blame you for is now a vendor, plus whatever your data-residency commitments have to say about event payloads leaving your infrastructure.

**Bulk export or a data share.** Nightly S3 drops, a warehouse share. For the analytics consumer who is only polling because you offered nothing else and doesn't need events at all. Frequently the correct answer for your single loudest poller.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Fire-and-forget after commit, one URL in a config row.** A single event type, one design partner, no retries. This is a defensible starting point and its purpose is to find out whether anybody actually integrates. Accept that events are silently lost. *Move when:* the first "we never got it."

**L1 — Durable events, async delivery, retries.** An `events` table written in the same transaction as the state change, a worker, backoff with jitter, attempts recorded. Still possibly one URL per account. *Move when:* a second event type appears, or a customer asks for a second endpoint, or you need to prove you sent something.

**L2 — Subscriptions and a contract.** An endpoint registry (url, secret, subscribed types, enabled), signatures over the raw body, a documented envelope with a stable event id and type, and a delivery log the customer can read. *Move when:* support asks you to run a SQL query to answer a customer question, or one endpoint's latency shows up in another customer's delivery times.

**L3 — Isolation and lifecycle.** Per-endpoint concurrency and circuit breaking, per-endpoint send rates, automatic disabling with notification, customer-triggered replay, retention on the attempt log. *Move when:* the deliveries table becomes a database capacity question, or you have had more than one incident caused by a single consumer.

**L4 — A platform.** Self-serve endpoint management, test events, endpoint verification at registration time, a published event catalogue, versioning. Or the decision to buy it, which most teams should at least cost out here rather than at L1.

One ordering constraint cuts across the ladder: **signatures and stable event ids have to exist by L2 at the latest**, because both are unretrofittable. Adding a signature later means every existing consumer starts rejecting or ignoring it, and introducing an id later means the events already delivered can never be referenced.

## Implementation

Go, because the delivery worker is fundamentally a concurrency and timeout problem, and because the isolation mechanism is easiest to see when the claim, the attempt, and the failure handling are in one place.

**Recording and fanning out, inside the transaction:**

```go
// Called with the same *sql.Tx that wrote the state change.
func RecordEvent(ctx context.Context, tx *sql.Tx, e Event) error {
	if _, err := tx.ExecContext(ctx,
		`INSERT INTO events (id, tenant_id, type, payload, occurred_at)
		 VALUES ($1, $2, $3, $4, now())`,
		e.ID, e.TenantID, e.Type, e.Payload,
	); err != nil {
		return err
	}
	// One delivery row per subscribed endpoint. This is the fan-out.
	_, err := tx.ExecContext(ctx,
		`INSERT INTO deliveries (id, event_id, endpoint_id, state, next_attempt_at)
		 SELECT gen_random_uuid(), $1, ep.id, 'pending', now()
		   FROM endpoints ep
		  WHERE ep.tenant_id = $2 AND ep.enabled AND $3 = ANY(ep.event_types)`,
		e.ID, e.TenantID, e.Type,
	)
	return err
}
```

The point of this function is what it doesn't do: touch the network. If it called `http.Post`, two things would break simultaneously — the transaction would stay open for the duration of a stranger's p99, holding locks the rest of your application wants, and a rollback after a successful POST would announce an event that never happened. The transaction's only job is to make the *intent* durable.

**One attempt:**

```go
var client = &http.Client{
	Timeout: 8 * time.Second, // total: connect + TLS + write + read
	Transport: &http.Transport{
		DialContext:         guardedDialer.DialContext, // refuses private IPs — 17.1c
		MaxIdleConnsPerHost: 4,
	},
}

type outcome int

const (
	delivered outcome = iota
	retryable
	permanent
)

func attempt(ctx context.Context, ep Endpoint, d Delivery) (outcome, int, string) {
	req, _ := http.NewRequestWithContext(ctx, http.MethodPost, ep.URL, bytes.NewReader(d.Body))
	ts := time.Now().Unix()
	req.Header.Set("Content-Type", "application/json")
	req.Header.Set("Webhook-Id", d.EventID) // the consumer's idempotency key
	req.Header.Set("Webhook-Timestamp", strconv.FormatInt(ts, 10))
	req.Header.Set("Webhook-Signature", sign(ep.Secret, d.EventID, ts, d.Body))

	resp, err := client.Do(req)
	if err != nil {
		// Includes timeout, which is ambiguous: they may have processed it.
		return retryable, 0, err.Error()
	}
	defer resp.Body.Close()

	// Cap what comes back. A receiver that streams a 40MB stack-trace page
	// is not hypothetical, and it is your memory, not theirs.
	snippet, _ := io.ReadAll(io.LimitReader(resp.Body, 2048))
	io.Copy(io.Discard, io.LimitReader(resp.Body, 1<<20)) // drain so the conn is reusable

	switch {
	case resp.StatusCode/100 == 2:
		return delivered, resp.StatusCode, ""
	case resp.StatusCode == http.StatusGone:
		return permanent, resp.StatusCode, string(snippet)
	default:
		return retryable, resp.StatusCode, string(snippet)
	}
}
```

That `default` is a judgment call, not an omission. By the book, `400` is permanent — the same bytes will fail identically, so retrying is pure waste. In practice a large share of `400`s and `401`s come from a receiver that is misconfigured *this hour*: a rotated secret, a body parser that chokes during a redeploy, a proxy answering while the app restarts. Most providers retry everything except an explicit `410`. Either policy is defensible; what isn't defensible is leaving it undocumented, because your consumers' error handling is built on top of the answer.

**Isolation, by leasing the endpoint rather than the delivery:**

```sql
UPDATE endpoints SET leased_until = now() + interval '60 seconds'
 WHERE id = (
   SELECT ep.id FROM endpoints ep
    WHERE ep.enabled AND ep.leased_until < now()
      AND EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM deliveries d
                   WHERE d.endpoint_id = ep.id
                     AND d.state = 'pending'
                     AND d.next_attempt_at <= now())
    ORDER BY ep.leased_until
      FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED
    LIMIT 1)
RETURNING id, url, secret;
```

Claiming the *endpoint* instead of the *delivery* is what buys the isolation. At most one worker is ever pointed at a given consumer, so a black-holing endpoint costs exactly one worker for one lease, no matter how much work is queued behind it. Per-endpoint ordering falls out for free as a side effect.

```go
func drain(ctx context.Context, ep Endpoint) {
	for _, d := range dueDeliveries(ctx, ep.ID, 50) { // oldest first
		out, code, body := attempt(ctx, ep, d)
		recordAttempt(ctx, d, out, code, body)

		switch out {
		case delivered:
			markDelivered(ctx, d)
		case permanent:
			markDead(ctx, d)
		case retryable:
			scheduleRetry(ctx, d) // backoff + jitter, Module 10.2
			noteEndpointFailure(ctx, ep)
			return // ← stop draining this endpoint
		}
	}
}
```

The `return` is the load-bearing line. Without it, an endpoint that's been down since Tuesday burns eight seconds per queued delivery every cycle, forever, and fills the attempts table with identical timeouts. With it, one failed attempt acts as the probe for the whole endpoint, and the backoff on the oldest delivery becomes the backoff for the endpoint — a circuit breaker assembled out of parts you already had. It also preserves ordering: you never deliver #5 after #4 has failed.

---

The habit worth taking from this: the instant a piece of your system starts talking to an address somebody else controls, *delivery becomes a first-class concern with its own state, its own storage, and its own failure budget* — not a function call with a retry wrapped around it. That's true of webhooks, of payment gateways, of push notification services, and of every integration in Module 17 that follows. The question to start asking of any outbound call in a codebase is simply: if this fails at 3am, who finds out, and from what record?

## Further Reading

- **Standard Webhooks** (standardwebhooks.com) — a specification for the envelope, headers, and signature scheme, produced by the Svix team. Worth reading even if you don't adopt it; it's the closest thing to a consensus on the header names used in the code above.
- **Stripe's webhook documentation**, particularly the pages on event delivery behaviour and best practices. The de facto reference for retry horizons, endpoint disabling, and the "acknowledge fast, process later" instruction to receivers.
- **GitHub's webhooks documentation** — the best public example of a delivery log with redelivery exposed directly to the consumer, which is worth looking at before designing your own.
- **CloudEvents** (CNCF specification) — a standard envelope for event metadata across transports. Useful mostly as a checklist of the fields an event needs before you design your own payload in 17.1b.
- **RFC 8935** (Push-Based Security Event Token Delivery over HTTP) and **RFC 8936** (the poll-based variant) — a rare formal treatment of exactly the push-versus-poll choice this article opens with, including why a standards body felt both were necessary.
- **Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_**, chapter 11 — for the vocabulary of at-least-once delivery, ordering, and replay that the rest of this module assumes.
- Within this map: retry scheduling and dead-lettering in **10.2**, the transactional outbox in **37**, rate limiting turned outward in **18**, and payload deprecation in **33**.

A TypeScript sibling would earn its place here, because per-endpoint isolation looks completely different when the queue provides it: with BullMQ's grouped queues the entire leasing mechanism above collapses into a configuration option, which both removes a hundred lines and hides the mechanism from anyone who then has to debug it. Not yet written.
