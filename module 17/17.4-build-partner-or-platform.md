# Build, Partner, or Platform

_Module 17.4 — Webhooks & Integration APIs_

## The Problem

A project-management product, two years in. There is a recurring meeting about integrations, and it has the same shape every time. Someone reads the list:

```
Jira            3 open deals   $180k    "blocking"
Salesforce      1 open deal    $400k    "blocking"
Slack           everyone       —        "table stakes"
Linear          2 deals
Asana, ClickUp, Monday, Notion, Trello, Basecamp …   1 deal each
"our internal tool"            1 deal   $250k
```

Engineering's estimate is six to ten weeks each, plus permanent maintenance — which, after 17.3b, everyone now understands means quota management, credential lifecycle, reconciliation, and an on-call story per provider. The list grows faster than the team does. It has grown between meetings, every meeting, for a year.

Meanwhile the integrations that exist are not behaving like finished work.

**The first one is a liability.** Jira shipped eighteen months ago. Atlassian deprecated the API version it was written against, the engineer who wrote it has left, it breaks every few weeks, and support treats it as its own product area.

**Nothing can be removed.** The Basecamp integration has four active customers. Deleting it is four churn risks and a public changelog entry explaining that you took something away. So it stays, forever, and its maintenance cost is now a fixed subscription you pay in engineering time.

**The tail doesn't end.** The tenth-most-requested integration has two customers. The fiftieth has one. Each of those customers considers theirs essential and is entirely correct from where they sit.

**Three customers already built their own** against your public API. One of them is better than what you would have shipped. You found out about it when your deprecation notice broke it, because — 17.3a's point — there was no record that they existed.

**"Can't we just add a Zapier connector?"** is asked in the meeting, agreed to in principle, and then takes four months, because your list endpoints sort by `updated_at`, your payloads have no stable ids, and there's no programmatic way to register a webhook endpoint. None of which anyone knew was a prerequisite.

**And someone proposes a marketplace**, on the theory that then other people would build these. Nobody in the room can say what would be in it on launch day.

The meeting keeps producing the wrong output because it keeps asking the wrong question. It asks *should we integrate with X*, one X at a time, forever. The question that resolves the list is: **who builds and maintains each of these, and what do we have to build once so that it doesn't have to be us?**

## What It Is

Three postures toward any given integration:

**Build.** You write it, own it, and maintain it. A native or first-party integration.

**Partner.** Somebody else writes it — the other vendor, a systems integrator, an automation platform like Zapier or Make, or the customer's own engineers. You supply the surface they build against.

**Platform.** You build the capability for anyone to build, plus distribution and governance: developer registration, a directory, a review process.

The important correction to the usual framing: **these are not stages you graduate through.** They're a portfolio you hold simultaneously. A mature company builds five integrations natively, maintains one connector on an automation platform, publishes an app in two other vendors' marketplaces, and runs a directory of third-party apps — all at once. The work is deciding which bucket each request goes into, not picking a strategy.

The second correction: **you have already built the platform.** OAuth for third-party access (17.3a), outbound webhooks with a registration API (17.1a, 17.2a), a documented API, and event payloads that are a stable contract (17.1b) — that *is* the platform layer. The marketplace is distribution and trust sitting on top of it. This article is about what to do with what the rest of the module built.

A few terms, since they get used interchangeably and aren't: a **native integration** is code you own; a **connector** is a declarative definition of triggers and actions hosted and executed by an automation platform; an **embedded iPaaS** is that platform's machinery running inside your own product's UI; and a **marketplace** or **app directory** is a listing, a review process, and an install flow.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Integration requirements appear in deal terms, and nobody can forecast them.
- A majority of your roadmap is integration work you didn't plan a year ago.
- An integration you shipped has no current owner.
- You have lost a deal over a missing integration, or won one by promising a date engineering hadn't seen.
- The same shape — fetch records, map fields, push to us — is being written for the fifth time with no shared code.
- Customers are building against your API and you learn about it only when something breaks.
- Somebody proposed a marketplace and the room couldn't name its launch contents.
- Sales asked whether a connector platform would work and the answer required a week of investigation.
- You maintain an integration with a vendor whose own customers never asked for it.

## How It Works

### The triage

Two axes decide most cases: how many customers want it, and how deep it has to be.

```
                  SHALLOW                        DEEP
            (notify, create, simple      (two-way sync, object mapping,
             field mapping, triggers)     conflict resolution, backfill)
          ┌────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────┐
  HIGH    │ native quick win, or a     │ BUILD IT.                    │
  DEMAND  │ connector — both are cheap │ Nothing else can express it. │
          ├────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────┤
  LOW     │ connector platform.        │ API + docs. The customer or  │
  DEMAND  │ One build, many targets.   │ an agency builds it, or you  │
          │                            │ charge for it as services.   │
          └────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┘
```

**Build it yourself** when demand is concentrated — many customers, or a few very large ones — or when the integration *is* the product for a segment. A code-review tool without a deep GitHub integration isn't a product with a missing feature; it's not a product. Depth is the other trigger: bidirectional sync, custom field mapping, and conflict resolution cannot be expressed declaratively in anyone's connector format.

**Partner** when the request is trigger-and-action shaped — *when X happens in your product, do Y somewhere else* — or when demand is one or two customers per target. A single connector on a large automation platform reaches thousands of destinations for the cost of one native integration, and the tail is where nearly all of the list in the Problem section lives.

There's a third partnering move that's consistently underrated: **build in their marketplace instead of waiting for them to integrate with you.** A Slack app, a Shopify app, a Salesforce package, an Atlassian Forge app. You get their distribution, their auth, their install flow, and their users' existing trust. It's the strongest option when your customers spend their working day inside that other tool, and it's frequently cheaper than the equivalent native integration because the platform supplies half of it.

**Platform** when third parties are already integrating without your help, when the demand is both high-volume and heterogeneous, and when the breadth of the integration surface is itself competitive in your category. It is the most expensive posture and the one most often chosen for prestige.

### What a connector actually requires

The decision to "add a Zapier connector" is usually made without knowing what the platform needs from your API. The requirements are specific and they're the reason the four-month surprise happens.

**Polling triggers** — the platform calls a list endpoint every few minutes and keeps whatever it hasn't seen before. That demands a list sorted newest-first by *creation*, a stable unique id on every item, and a page size large enough that a burst between polls isn't lost. Sorting by `updated_at` makes an edited two-year-old record appear as new; missing or unstable ids break deduplication entirely.

**REST hooks** — webhook-driven triggers — turn a five-minute poll into a two-second push, and they require a programmatic subscribe and unsubscribe. That's the endpoint registration API from 17.2a, called by a machine rather than a human.

**Dynamic fields.** A dropdown listing the user's projects needs an endpoint returning id/label pairs. Without it, the connector asks the user to type `prj_01HXQ7M3F2K8` into a text box, which means it technically works and practically doesn't.

**Sample payloads** for every trigger, because the platform's field-mapping UI is built from a sample.

**OAuth**, strongly preferred over API keys, for the reasons in 17.3a — and because the platform's users will otherwise paste credentials into a form on a third site.

Budget a few weeks to build, plus the platform's review process, plus ongoing maintenance, plus supporting people who use your connector without being your customers, inside a UI you don't control. And note the economics: these platforms price per task or per operation, which makes them the wrong shape for bulk synchronization no matter how convenient they are for events.

### Embedded iPaaS

The variant worth knowing: the automation platform runs *inside* your product. Customers configure their integrations in your UI, using connectors the vendor maintains. You ship a hundred integrations without writing any of them, the experience stays native, and the customer never leaves.

The costs are per-connection pricing that scales with your success, a deep dependency on a vendor whose connector quality is now indistinguishable from your quality, and a support path where the failure is in someone else's code but the screenshot is of your interface.

### What "done" means for a native integration

The API work is often forty percent of it. The rest is product, and it's what 17.3b enumerated: a connection UI, a health surface, a reconnect flow when authorization lapses, field mapping, sync status the customer can read, error messages translated out of the provider's vocabulary, a backfill with visible progress, and documentation.

Two additional obligations that decide whether it's still working in two years: **a named owner**, because an integration without one becomes everyone's problem and therefore nobody's, and **a watch on the provider's deprecation channel**, because their schedule is now an input to your planning.

### Marketplaces

A marketplace is **distribution and trust**, not technology. The technology is 17.1 through 17.3. What a directory adds is a listing with categories and search, a review process covering security and quality, an install flow that lands the user in the right place, support routing that answers whose ticket this is, and — if you go there — billing and revenue share, which is Module 15 again for someone else's money.

The bootstrap problem is universal and has one known solution. Developers build where the users are; users look where the apps are. **You build the first five yourself and recruit the next ten by hand.** A directory launched with four apps, three of which you wrote, is worse than no directory: it's a public advertisement that nobody builds on you.

And the governance is not optional once it exists. A third-party app's outage is your brand's outage in the customer's mind. You need the revocation path from 17.3a as a genuine kill switch, a review queue with a stated bar, a policy about what apps may do with customer data, and enough app-level telemetry to know which integration is responsible when something goes wrong.

Revenue share is mostly a distraction early. The return on a marketplace is retention and switching cost, not the percentage.

### The escape hatches

A surprising share of the list in the Problem section is resolved by things that aren't integrations at all: CSV import and export, which interoperates with every system ever built including the ones with no API; an email-in address; an embeddable widget; and a public API with webhooks and good documentation, which is the complete answer for any customer who employs engineers.

One of these deserves special mention because it's already built. **A generic outbound webhook to a customer-chosen URL, plus a documented inbound API, is an integration with everything that has a webhook endpoint** — including every automation platform. It's the cheapest coverage of the long tail available, and by this point in the module you have both halves.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Build natively:** the top three to five by revenue-weighted demand, and anything the product is defined by. Roughly: deep integration shape, plus either several customers or a single deal that pays for the next three years of maintenance.
- **Connector platform:** any request shaped like *when X happens, do Y*, and any target with three or fewer interested customers.
- **Their marketplace:** when your users are already in that tool all day and it has a real distribution channel.
- **API and docs only:** when the requesting customer has engineers, which is most enterprise requests and almost no SMB ones.
- **Directory or marketplace:** when third parties are already building without your help — ten or so unprompted integrations is the honest threshold.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A marketplace before roughly ten third-party integrations exist.** You'd be building distribution for nothing to distribute.
- **Connectors on five automation platforms.** Pick the largest, learn what it costs, then decide about the second.
- **A native integration for one customer, unfunded.** Either it's paid services work with a maintenance agreement, or it's a no.
- **An internal integration framework before the third one exists.** The first two teach you which differences matter; guessing produces an abstraction shaped exactly like provider number one.
- **Revenue share mechanics before there is revenue** to share.
- **Integrating with a vendor whose API is in beta**, or whose company is eight people. The maintenance risk is asymmetric and lands entirely on you.

## Trade-offs

- **Every integration is a permanent liability**, priced as a project and paid as a subscription. Someone else's deprecation calendar is now on your roadmap.
- **Adding one is close to irreversible.** Removal is a churn event, which means the decision to build is effectively permanent and should be made with that in mind.
- **Partnering trades control for reach.** The connector's error messages, limits, and UX are not yours, and the user blames you for all three.
- **Platform trades speed for leverage.** Once people build on your API and your event payloads, you can't move quickly anymore — that's 33's deprecation problem and 17.1b's contract problem, both now with external holders.
- **Marketplace quality is reputational.** You're accountable for software you didn't write and can only review at the margin.
- **The incentives are misaligned by default.** Integrations win deals for a team that doesn't carry the maintenance. Without a written rule, every case becomes an argument decided by whoever is most senior in the room.
- **Support ownership is ambiguous in every non-native model**, and ambiguity resolves toward whoever the customer is already paying.

## Alternatives

**Say no, consistently, and publish an excellent API.** Some companies do exactly this and it works — no native integrations at all, superb documentation, and an ecosystem or the customer's own team fills the gap. It buys enormous focus and it costs you every customer who doesn't employ engineers.

**Charge for it.** Bespoke integrations as professional services, with a maintenance agreement attached. This funds the work and, more usefully, filters demand: a request that survives a price quote is a real one.

**Adopt a unified API vendor** for a whole category at once (17.3b), converting five integrations into one with a data model you don't control.

**Acquire or partner commercially** with whoever already built the integration your customers want — frequently cheaper than building it.

**Invert the model.** If connecting systems is your product's actual value proposition, none of this is overhead — integrations are the roadmap, breadth is the moat, and the correct posture is to industrialize integration-building as a core competency rather than triaging it.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — A list and a published API.** Every integration request in one place with the revenue attached, and public docs. *Move when:* the list has entries you keep re-litigating.

**L1 — Self-serve for customers with engineers.** Outbound webhooks and OAuth — both already built, in 17.1a and 17.3a — plus documentation good enough that a stranger can finish without talking to you. This alone removes a meaningful fraction of the list.

**L2 — One connector, and two or three natives.** The largest automation platform, plus the natives your revenue-weighted list actually justifies, each with a named owner. *Move when:* third parties start asking for credentials unprompted.

**L3 — A developer surface.** App registration, sandbox, an integrations page on your site that lists third-party integrations alongside yours. *Move when:* you can no longer name every integration from memory.

**L4 — Directory and governance.** Listings, review, install flow, app-level telemetry, a kill switch. Revenue share only if there's revenue.

The highest-leverage artifact at any level is not code: it's **a one-page integration policy** stating the bar for building natively, who owns what gets built, and what the answer is when the bar isn't met. It converts a recurring argument into a rule, which is the only way the meeting in the Problem section ever stops.

## Implementation

TypeScript, and only two fragments — because the interesting thing here isn't the connector code, it's what the connector code silently requires from you.

```ts
// A polling trigger, roughly Zapier-shaped.
export const newTask = {
  key: "new_task",
  noun: "Task",
  operation: {
    inputFields: [
      { key: "project_id", label: "Project", dynamic: "project_list.id.name", required: true },
    ],
    perform: async (z, bundle) => {
      const res = await z.request({
        url: "https://api.acme.io/v1/tasks",
        params: { project_id: bundle.inputData.project_id, order: "-created_at", limit: 100 },
      });
      return res.data.data; // newest first; every item carries a stable `id`
    },
    sample: { id: "tsk_01HXQ7M3F2K8", title: "Draft the brief", created_at: "2026-03-04T11:32:04Z" },
  },
};
```

Fifteen lines of their code, three hard requirements on yours. `order=-created_at` exists because the platform polls, keeps the ids it hasn't seen, and discards the rest — sort by `updated_at` and an edited two-year-old task arrives as brand new. The stable `id` is what deduplication runs on, and it has to be the same id forever, which is 17.1b's opaque-identifier rule cashing out in a system you'll never see. And `dynamic` is a promise that a second endpoint exists returning projects as id/label pairs; without it the field is a text box asking a marketing manager to type `prj_01HX…`.

```ts
performSubscribe: async (z, bundle) =>
  (await z.request({
    method: "POST",
    url: "https://api.acme.io/v1/webhook_endpoints",
    body: { url: bundle.targetUrl, enabled_events: ["task.created"] },
  })).data,

performUnsubscribe: async (z, bundle) =>
  z.request({
    method: "DELETE",
    url: `https://api.acme.io/v1/webhook_endpoints/${bundle.subscribeData.id}`,
  }),
```

This is the article in miniature. Those two calls are the registration API from 17.2a, invoked by a machine instead of a person — and they're the difference between a connector that polls every five minutes and one that fires in two seconds. If endpoint registration had been built as an internal admin tool, as it usually is, this trigger could not exist and nobody would be able to explain why the connector felt sluggish.

---

The habit worth taking from the module's last article: **integration demand is unbounded and integration capacity is not, so the only durable answer is leverage rather than effort.** Every hour spent making it possible for someone else to build the integration — a webhook that registers itself, an event payload with a stable id, an OAuth flow with meaningful scopes, a list endpoint sorted the way a poller needs — pays out across every integration that will ever exist, including the ones nobody has thought of. Every hour spent building the twelfth native integration pays out once, and then bills you monthly. The first kind of work looks like infrastructure and gets deprioritized; the second looks like progress and fills the roadmap. Knowing which one you're doing is the whole skill.

## Further Reading

- **Zapier's platform documentation**, particularly on polling triggers versus REST hooks and on deduplication — the clearest public statement anywhere of what an API must provide to be automatable, and worth reading as an API design review even if you never build a connector.
- **Embedded iPaaS vendor documentation** (Paragon, Prismatic, Workato Embedded) — read the pricing model alongside the features, since per-connection pricing is the decisive fact.
- **A marketplace's app review guidelines** — Slack's, Shopify's, or Atlassian's — before anyone proposes building one. The review criteria are an inventory of the governance obligations that come with a directory.
- **Parker, Van Alstyne and Choudary, _Platform Revolution_** — for the chicken-and-egg problem and the seeding strategies, which are older and better understood than most product teams assume.
- Within this map: the webhook pipeline in **17.1a**, endpoint registration in **17.2a**, OAuth and app registration in **17.3a**, the cost of maintaining an outbound integration in **17.3b**, deprecation in **33**, and revenue share as a billing problem in **15**.
