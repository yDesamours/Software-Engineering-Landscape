# After Launch: Monitoring, Drift, Retraining and Bias in Classical Models

_Module 39.2b — Machine Learning & LLM-Powered Systems_

## The Problem

Suppose you run accounting software for small businesses, and you've started offering working-capital loans inside the product: $5,000 to $50,000, repaid over six months. The advantage over a bank is data. You can see each business's cash balance, how fast its customers pay, how steady its revenue is, and what it spends on payroll, through its connected bank feeds. A model trained on two years of history predicts the probability that a business defaults within six months. Applications scoring above 6% are declined.

It launches in January. The offline evaluation was careful: the data was split by time, there was no leakage (39.1a), the model runs in shadow mode first, and it's rolled out with a canary (39.2a). For three months, everything looks fine. Latency is low, there are no errors, and the dashboards are green.

**March: the model is quietly fed broken data.** One of the two bank-feed aggregators the product uses splits its "Payroll" category into "Payroll – Wages" and "Payroll – Taxes." The feature `payroll_share_of_outflows` looks for the old name, so it drops to zero for the 30% of businesses on that aggregator. Nothing errors. Approvals rise from 41% to 52% over a week, and finance notices three weeks later, when the month's loan volume is far above plan.

**May: a new kind of customer arrives.** A partnership with a restaurant point-of-sale company brings in 2,000 restaurants. Restaurants were 4% of the training data. They're now 22% of applications. Their scores look reasonable, but the model learned very little about restaurants, and nobody can tell yet whether it's right about them.

**September: the truth arrives for January.** Six months after funding, the January loans have matured. The model predicted 4.1% of them would default. The actual figure is 7.8%. Over the spring, many small businesses' own customers started paying more slowly, and the pattern that separated good borrowers from bad ones in the training years changed. By the time the labels confirm it, the model has been approving loans with outdated judgment for eight months.

**October: an audit finds a gap.** A review of approvals by neighborhood shows that businesses in ZIP codes where most residents are Black or Hispanic are approved at 23%, against 44% elsewhere. The model never sees race. But two of its features are `zip_median_business_revenue` and `bank_name`, and both carry information about where a business is and which banks serve that neighborhood, on top of anything about the business's own finances. Two businesses with identical books get different scores depending on their address.

None of these showed up in the offline evaluation, and none of them triggered an error. Every one was a model working exactly as built, in a world that had moved away from its training data, or on data that no longer meant what it used to.

## What It Is

**Model monitoring** is watching a deployed model's inputs, outputs and eventual outcomes, so you notice when it stops being right. 39.2a set up the raw material: a prediction log with features, a version and a prediction ID for every decision. This article is about what to do with that log.

What monitoring looks for is usually called **drift**, a catch-all for "production no longer looks like training." It has four meaningfully different causes, and the Problem hit each of them:

- **Data-quality breaks** (March). The world didn't change; the pipeline did. A renamed category, a unit change or a new null makes a feature mean something different. This is the most common cause of "drift" in practice, and it isn't drift at all. It's a bug.
- **Covariate shift** (May). The mix of inputs changes: more restaurants, more businesses from one region. Each type of business may still behave as the model expects, but the model is now spending more of its time where it knows least.
- **Label shift** (also called prior shift). The overall base rate changes, for example when defaults rise across the board in a recession, while the relationship between each feature and the outcome stays the same.
- **Concept drift** (September). The relationship itself changes. The signals that separated good borrowers from bad ones in the training years no longer separate them in the same way.

Around monitoring sit two responses. **Retraining** fits a new model on newer data, usually through an automated pipeline with checks before a new model is promoted. **Bias detection** checks whether the model's decisions or errors differ systematically between groups of people, which is what the October audit found.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A model has been in production for months, and the only things monitored are latency and errors.
- The fraction of approvals, flags or positive predictions has moved, and nobody can say why.
- Nobody knows the model's current accuracy, because the outcomes take months to arrive.
- A new customer segment, market or partner has grown quickly since the model was trained.
- An upstream data source (a vendor feed, an event schema, another team's table) changed without the model owners hearing about it.
- The model is retrained on a schedule, and nobody checks the new one before it's promoted.
- The team's answer to "is the model fair?" is "it doesn't use protected attributes."
- The model makes decisions about people's access to credit, jobs, housing, insurance or services.

## How It Works

### Four layers of signals, arriving at different speeds

The difficulty in monitoring is that the thing you care about, whether the model was right, arrives last. For these loans, it takes six months. So monitoring is built from faster signals that stand in for it:

```
 decision    seconds       hours–days          60 days             6 months
    ●───────────┼──────────────┼──────────────────┼───────────────────┼──────▶
            service       data quality,       early outcome       the real label
            health        input & score       (30 days late on    (default or
            (39.2a)       drift               a payment)          repaid)
            "is it up?"   "does traffic look  "is this cohort     "was the model
                          like training?"     souring early?"      right?"
```

- **Service health**: latency, errors and fallback rate. 39.2a covered these. They catch outages, not a model that's wrong.
- **Data quality**: checks on each input before it reaches the model, such as the schema, the null rate, the value range and the set of category values. March's failure would have been caught here within an hour: the share of zeros in `payroll_share_of_outflows` went from 3% to 31% for one aggregator. These checks are cheap, precise and the highest-value monitoring you can add.
- **Drift in inputs and outputs**: comparing the distribution of each feature, and of the model's scores and decisions, with a reference period. This catches May, and it would have flagged March too.
- **Outcomes**: once labels arrive, measure the model's actual accuracy. When labels are slow, look for an **early outcome** that predicts the real one. A borrower who's 30 days late on a payment within the first 60 days is much more likely to default, so a rising early-delinquency rate is a warning four months ahead of the true label.

### Measuring drift without drowning in it

A drift check compares a **current window** (for example, the last seven days of applications) with a **reference window** (for example, the model's first stable month in production, or its training data). For each feature and for the score, it asks how different the two distributions are.

A common measure, from credit scoring, is the **Population Stability Index (PSI)**. Split the reference values into ten bins with equal counts. For each bin, compare the share of current values that fall in it (`cur`) with the reference share (`ref`), and add up `(cur − ref) × ln(cur / ref)` over the bins. By convention, below 0.1 is stable, 0.1–0.25 is worth a look, and above 0.25 is a significant shift. Other measures (Jensen–Shannon distance, Wasserstein distance, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic) work similarly.

Three habits keep drift monitoring useful:

- **Use a measure of how big the difference is, not a significance test.** With thousands of applications a week, a statistical test finds a "significant" difference in almost every feature almost every week. PSI and similar measures report size, and size is what matters.
- **Watch the score and the decision first.** Forty features produce forty alerts. The score distribution and the approval rate summarize what the model is actually doing. When they move, look at the features, ranked by how much the model relies on each one, to find out why.
- **Compute everything by segment.** March affected 30% of applicants, so the overall approval rate moved less than it did for the affected slice. Computing drift separately by aggregator, industry, region and new versus returning customers finds problems that averages hide.

Drift is not an error on its own. The restaurants arriving in May is good news for the business. The question drift raises is whether the model is still right *for the segment that grew*, and only outcomes can answer that.

### Outcomes that arrive late: cohorts

When the label takes six months, the most useful view is a **cohort** (or **vintage**) table: group loans by the month they were funded, and track what fraction have gone wrong at each age:

```
 funded     loans   30+ days late after…          expected at that age
                     1 mo    2 mo    3 mo    4 mo   (from training data)
 Jan        1,840    0.8%    2.9%    4.4%    5.6%    0.7%  1.5%  2.2%  2.8%
 Feb        1,910    0.9%    3.1%    4.9%     –
 Mar        2,660    1.4%    3.8%     –       –      ← broken payroll feature
 Apr        2,230    0.7%     –       –       –
```

At two months, the January cohort had already reached nearly twice the expected early-delinquency rate. That was a clear signal in March, six months before September's confirmation. The dashes matter as much as the numbers: a cohort is only compared at ages it has actually reached. The Implementation section includes this query.

**Performance estimation** goes one step further. If the model's probabilities are well calibrated, the expected default rate of a group of loans is simply the average of their predicted probabilities. The confidence-based performance estimation (CBPE) method in the NannyML library builds on this to estimate accuracy under covariate shift before any labels arrive. It relies on the relationship between features and outcome staying the same, so it can't detect concept drift. September's problem would have looked fine to it. It's a useful early signal, not a substitute for outcomes.

### Retraining, and when it isn't the fix

Retraining means fitting the model again on more recent data. Each of the four drift causes calls for a different response, and retraining is the right one for only some of them:

```
 cause                          what to do
 data-quality break (March)     fix the pipeline. Retraining on broken data
                                teaches the model the broken feature.
 covariate shift, into a        check the model's accuracy on that segment;
 segment with training data     retraining adds examples but may change little
 covariate shift, into a        collect outcomes first: limited offers, smaller
 segment without data (May)     amounts, or a separate policy for the segment
 label shift                    recalibrate: adjust the probabilities to the new
                                base rate on recent data, without a full retrain
 concept drift (September)      retrain on recent matured data, weighting recent
                                cohorts more; accept that the lag is the label delay
```

A **retraining pipeline** makes the last row routine:

1. **Assemble data** from the prediction log and outcomes, with point-in-time features (39.2a).
2. **Keep only matured labels.** A loan funded two months ago that hasn't defaulted is not a "good" loan. It just hasn't had time to default. Counting it as good teaches the model that recent loans are safe, and the model then approves more of exactly the loans it knows least about. This mistake is easy to make and hard to spot, because the new model looks *better* on the flawed data.
3. **Train** on a chosen window: all history, a sliding window of recent cohorts, or all history with recent cohorts weighted more. For concept drift, recent data matters more; for rare events, you need enough history to see them.
4. **Evaluate the challenger against the current champion** on the most recent matured cohort that neither was trained on, overall and per segment and per group (see bias below).
5. **Pass the gates, then roll out** through shadow and canary (39.2a). For high-stakes models, promotion needs a human's approval, not only passing gates.

What sets retraining off is a choice:
- **A schedule** (monthly, weekly) is predictable and easy to operate. It's the right default.
- **A drift trigger** retrains when inputs move, which responds faster but can chase noise or a pipeline bug.
- **A performance trigger** retrains when measured accuracy drops, which is precise but arrives as late as the labels.

Pipelines are usually run by a workflow orchestrator such as Airflow, Dagster or Kubeflow Pipelines, or the equivalent in a cloud ML platform, with runs recorded in an experiment tracker such as MLflow.

Retraining can't fix one thing. The model only learns from loans it approved, because declined businesses never produce an outcome. That's the selective-labels problem from 39.1a, and each retrain inherits it. The exploration slice described there is what gives retraining a view of the decisions the model refused.

### Bias: where it comes from and how to measure it

A model is biased, in the sense that matters here, when its decisions or errors differ systematically between groups of people in ways that aren't justified by what it's supposed to predict. It enters through a few routes:

- **Proxies.** A feature that isn't a protected attribute carries information about one: ZIP code, bank, first name, device type, school. In October, `zip_median_business_revenue` scored the neighborhood, not the business.
- **Historical labels.** If the outcome being predicted was shaped by past unfair treatment (for example, businesses in some areas were offered worse terms and defaulted more often because of it), the model learns that history as if it were a fact about the applicants.
- **Unequal data.** Groups with fewer examples get less accurate predictions. The restaurants in May are the non-demographic version of this.
- **Feedback loops.** A group that's declined more often produces fewer outcomes, so the model stays less accurate about it (39.1a).

"We don't use protected attributes" doesn't prevent any of these. And to *measure* bias, you usually need the attribute, or an estimate of it. For lending in the US, a common method is Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding (BISG), which estimates race and ethnicity from a surname and location, and which the CFPB uses in its own analyses. Keep group data like this separate from the model's features, with restricted access, and use it only for measurement.

There are several fairness metrics, and each makes a different claim:

- **Selection-rate parity (demographic parity):** each group is approved at the same rate. A common rough screen, borrowed from US employment guidance, flags a group whose rate is under four-fifths of the highest group's. October's 23% against 44% is a ratio of 0.52.
- **Equal opportunity:** among applicants who *would* repay, each group is approved at the same rate. **Equalized odds** also requires equal rates of approving applicants who would default. Both need outcomes for declined applicants, which lending never observes directly, so they're estimated from exploration slices or reconstructed with statistical methods.
- **Calibration within groups:** a score of 5% means a 5% default rate in every group. This is the one that's directly observable from matured loans.

These metrics can't all be satisfied at once. When groups have different base rates, a model can't be calibrated within each group and also have equal error rates across groups, unless it predicts perfectly (Kleinberg et al., and Chouldechova, both 2017). So **choosing a fairness metric is a policy decision, not an engineering one**. It belongs to the product, legal and risk owners, and engineering's job is to measure it reliably, put it on the dashboard and enforce it in the promotion gates.

Once a gap is measured, the fixes include:
- **Removing or replacing proxies.** A quick test is to train a small model to predict group membership from the features. Features that make that easy are proxies. Another is counterfactual: re-score real applications with only the ZIP code changed, and see how much the score moves.
- **Collecting better data** for groups where the model is least accurate.
- **Reweighting** the training data so underrepresented groups count more.
- **Adjusting decisions by group.** This is legally restricted in some domains. In US lending, for example, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act forbids using protected characteristics in the credit decision, even to correct for bias. Decide with legal advice, not by default.

Regulation increasingly requires this work. US credit law requires specific reasons for declining an application, which means per-decision explanations of the model's score. The EU AI Act classifies credit scoring of individuals as high-risk. After the Digital Omnibus amendment took effect in July 2026, the high-risk obligations (risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight) apply from 2 December 2027. This article describes engineering practice, not legal advice. When a model makes decisions like these, the legal requirements need a lawyer's reading.

## When It's Appropriate

Every production model needs some monitoring. The question is how much, and that depends on how fast labels arrive and what mistakes cost.

- **Data-quality checks and score/decision drift: always.** They're cheap, and they catch the most common failures (pipeline breaks) within hours.
- **Segmented monitoring:** when traffic comes from sources that can change separately, such as two data vendors, several markets, or partner channels.
- **Cohort tables and early outcomes:** when the real label takes weeks or months, as in lending (six months), insurance claims or subscription churn.
- **Frequent automated retraining:** when labels are fast and the world changes steadily. A delivery-time model (39.2a) gets its labels within the hour and can retrain weekly on fresh data.
- **Scheduled retraining with human approval:** when labels are slow and mistakes are expensive, like the loans here.
- **Fairness measurement and gates:** whenever the model makes or informs decisions about people's access to credit, work, housing, insurance, education or public services, and in any product where a segment of users could be systematically served worse.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A monitoring platform for one batch model.** A scheduled SQL query that computes PSI and an approval rate, with an alert, covers a lot.
- **Drift alerts on every feature.** They produce more alerts than anyone reads. Alert on the score, the decisions and data quality, and investigate features when those move.
- **Automatic retraining with automatic promotion for high-stakes, slow-label models.** The newest data is the least matured, and a bad retrain can go unnoticed for months. Have a person approve promotions.
- **Frequent retraining when the world is stable.** A model that reads invoice PDFs doesn't need weekly retraining. Every retrain carries risk, and a stable domain gains little from it.
- **Continuous (online) learning for most business models.** Updating the model with every new example makes it very hard to reproduce, audit or roll back, and it's easy to poison.
- **Formal fairness audits for models that don't affect people.** Predicting disk usage for autoscaling doesn't need them. Segment monitoring is still worth it.

## Trade-offs

- **Monitoring is always late for the thing that matters most.** Faster signals are only proxies for correctness, and the true answer takes as long as the label does.
- **Alerts compete for attention.** Too sensitive, and people learn to ignore them. Not sensitive enough, and March happens. Thresholds and reference windows need tuning and maintenance.
- **Reference windows age.** A reference taken in January treats every later change as drift, including permanent, welcome changes like new restaurant customers. Updating the reference is a judgment call each time.
- **Retraining pipelines are production systems.** They need tests, alerts and owners. An unattended pipeline can learn a pipeline bug or immature labels and promote the result.
- **Retraining costs stability.** Each new model changes some decisions. Customers whose results change without their own situation changing notice, and so do regulators.
- **Fairness metrics conflict with each other,** and improving one can worsen another or reduce accuracy. Someone has to choose, and that choice has to be written down.
- **Measuring bias needs sensitive data.** Collecting or estimating protected attributes brings privacy and security obligations (Module 34), and group estimates like BISG have their own error rates.
- **Storing prediction logs costs money and brings privacy obligations.** Keeping features for every decision, for as long as outcomes take to arrive, is a lot of personal data.

## Alternatives

- **Periodic model reviews.** A scheduled review (quarterly, say) re-validates the model on new data, with a written report. This is the core of model risk management in banking (the US Federal Reserve's SR 11-7 guidance). It's slower than continuous monitoring, but thorough, and often required alongside it.
- **Simpler, more stable models.** A points-based scorecard with a dozen features is easier to monitor, explain and audit than a 400-tree model. It drifts too, but in ways that are easier to see and reason about.
- **Human review of borderline cases.** Routing applications near the threshold to an underwriter limits the damage when the model drifts, and produces careful labels.
- **A monitoring product.** Evidently (open source, with a hosted version), NannyML, Arize and Fiddler, or the built-in monitoring in SageMaker, Vertex AI and Databricks. They save building dashboards and drift statistics. They still need your prediction log, your outcomes and your decisions about segments and thresholds.
- **Fairness toolkits.** Fairlearn and IBM's AI Fairness 360 compute the metrics above and implement some mitigation methods. They measure; they don't decide which metric is right.

## How to Achieve It

**1. Join predictions to outcomes.** Start from the prediction log (39.2a), and add a table of outcomes keyed by prediction ID or application.
_Move on when_ you can compute last quarter's accuracy from a query.

**2. Add data-quality checks on inputs, by segment.** Check schemas, null and zero rates, ranges and category values against the reference, and alert on sudden changes.
_Move on when_ a test that deliberately breaks a feature raises an alert within an hour.

**3. Monitor the score and decisions for drift.** Compute PSI on the score and on the decision rate by segment, daily. Investigate features when those move.

**4. Track outcomes by cohort, and pick an early outcome.** Build the cohort table, and find the earliest signal that predicts the real label.
_Move on when_ the dashboard would have flagged the January cohort by March.

**5. Measure fairness, with an owner.** Agree on groups, metrics and thresholds with the people responsible for policy. Measure the current model before changing anything.

**6. Build a retraining pipeline with gates.** Use matured labels only, evaluate the challenger against the champion on the newest held-out cohort overall, by segment and by group, then shadow and canary. Promotion needs a person's approval.

**7. Automate triggers last.** Only after several retrains have gone well manually should a schedule or trigger start them, and promotion stays manual for high-stakes models.

## Implementation

The implementation is SQL (PostgreSQL), because monitoring is mostly queries over the prediction log and outcomes. The same queries run in any warehouse with small changes, and a scheduler (Module 10) runs them and raises alerts. There are three: score drift by segment, cohort curves, and a fairness table.

The tables are `prediction_log(application_id, at, model_version, score, decision, aggregator, industry, features jsonb)`, `loans(application_id, funded_at, late30_at, defaulted_at)` and a restricted `fairness_groups(application_id, group_label)`.

### Score drift by segment

```sql
-- PSI of this week's scores against the model's first stable month, per aggregator.
WITH ref_edges AS (
  SELECT percentile_cont(ARRAY[.1,.2,.3,.4,.5,.6,.7,.8,.9])
           WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY score) AS edges
  FROM   prediction_log
  WHERE  model_version = 'pd-2026-01'
    AND  at >= '2026-01-05' AND at < '2026-02-02'
),
binned AS (
  SELECT aggregator,
         at < '2026-02-02' AS is_ref,
         width_bucket(score, (SELECT edges FROM ref_edges)) AS bin   -- 0..9
  FROM   prediction_log
  WHERE  model_version = 'pd-2026-01'
    AND  (at >= '2026-01-05' AND at < '2026-02-02' OR at >= now() - interval '7 days')
),
shares AS (
  SELECT aggregator, bin,
         greatest(count(*) FILTER (WHERE is_ref)::float
                  / sum(count(*) FILTER (WHERE is_ref)) OVER (PARTITION BY aggregator), 1e-4) AS ref,
         greatest(count(*) FILTER (WHERE NOT is_ref)::float
                  / sum(count(*) FILTER (WHERE NOT is_ref)) OVER (PARTITION BY aggregator), 1e-4) AS cur
  FROM   binned
  GROUP  BY aggregator, bin
)
SELECT aggregator, round(sum((cur - ref) * ln(cur / ref))::numeric, 3) AS psi
FROM   shares
GROUP  BY aggregator
ORDER  BY psi DESC;
```

> **Why bin edges come from the reference.** Deciles of the reference put 10% of reference scores in each bin, so any bin that fills up or empties in the current window stands out. The floor of `1e-4` keeps an empty bin from dividing by zero. The same query, with `(features->>'payroll_share_of_outflows')::float` in place of `score`, is the per-feature check. Run that when the score moves, not on every feature every day.

> **What it would have shown in March.** For the affected aggregator, a large share of scores moved into the lowest-risk bins at once, and PSI rose well past 0.25 within days. For the other aggregator, it stayed near zero. Computed over everyone, the change was diluted enough to look like a normal fluctuation.

### Cohort curves that respect maturity

```sql
-- Share of each monthly cohort 30+ days late by each age, only at ages it has reached.
SELECT date_trunc('month', l.funded_at)::date AS cohort,
       m.age_months,
       count(*) AS loans,
       round(avg((l.late30_at IS NOT NULL
                  AND l.late30_at < l.funded_at + make_interval(months => m.age_months))::int), 4)
         AS late30_rate,
       e.late30_rate AS expected
FROM   loans l
CROSS  JOIN generate_series(1, 6) AS m(age_months)
JOIN   expected_curve e USING (age_months)          -- from the training-period cohorts
WHERE  l.funded_at + make_interval(months => m.age_months) <= now()
GROUP  BY 1, 2, e.late30_rate
ORDER  BY 1, 2;
```

> **The `WHERE` clause is the rule retraining must follow too.** A loan funded six weeks ago appears at age 1 but not at age 2, so its missing late payment isn't counted as a good outcome at an age it hasn't reached. The training query needs the same condition at six months. Without it, the most recent loans all look like successes, and the retrained model learns that the newest kind of borrower is the safest.

### A fairness table

```sql
SELECT g.group_label,
       count(*) AS applicants,
       round(avg((p.decision = 'approve')::int), 3) AS approval_rate,
       round(avg((p.decision = 'approve')::int)
             / max(avg((p.decision = 'approve')::int)) OVER (), 2) AS ratio_to_highest,
       -- Calibration: only on funded loans that have had six months to default.
       round((avg(p.score) FILTER (WHERE l.funded_at <= now() - interval '6 months'))::numeric, 3)
         AS predicted_default,
       round(avg((l.defaulted_at IS NOT NULL)::int)
               FILTER (WHERE l.funded_at <= now() - interval '6 months'), 3)
         AS actual_default
FROM   prediction_log p
JOIN   fairness_groups g USING (application_id)   -- restricted access, measurement only
LEFT   JOIN loans l USING (application_id)
WHERE  p.model_version = 'pd-2026-01'
GROUP  BY g.group_label
ORDER  BY approval_rate;
```

> **What this table can and can't show.** Approval rates and calibration come straight from data you have. Whether the model declines good borrowers more often in one group than another (equal opportunity) can't be read here, because declined applicants have no outcomes. That needs the exploration slice from 39.1a, or statistical estimation, which is one more reason to fund exploration. The table also measures the whole pipeline. If the rules around the model also decline applicants, their effect shows up here too, and that's the effect applicants experience.

---

The habit to take from this: a deployed model is a claim about the world, made at training time and repeated with every prediction. Keep checking whether the world still agrees: first whether the inputs still mean what they meant, then whether they still look like training, and finally, when the outcomes arrive, whether the claim was true, for everyone it was made about.
