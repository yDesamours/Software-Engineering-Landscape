# Is This a Prediction Problem? From Hand-Written Rules to Learned Models

_Module 39.1a — Machine Learning & LLM-Powered Systems_

## The Problem

Suppose you run an invoicing tool for small businesses. Anyone can start a 14-day trial, and trial accounts can send invoices by email from your domain. That last detail is what makes you a target: a phishing email sent from a real invoicing company, with a real "Pay now" button, gets past spam filters that would stop the same message from a random domain.

**Month 1: six rules.** The first wave of abuse is crude, so the fixes are too. Block disposable email domains. Allow at most three signups per IP per day (Module 18). Hold any trial invoice whose payment link points somewhere other than your own checkout. Abuse drops to near zero.

**Month 9: 140 rules.** Each new campaign is a new rule, and each rule lasts about a week before the attackers adjust. Signups now come from residential proxies, so the IP rule catches coworking spaces instead. One engineer understands how the rules interact, and she's the only one who dares to delete any. Two numbers are moving in the wrong direction at once:

```
                 abusive trials caught     real customers blocked (per 100 blocks)
Month 1          ~95%                      3
Month 5          ~80%                      11
Month 9          ~60%                      27
```

The abuse that gets through is sending thousands of phishing invoices. The complaint rate on your sending domain climbs until the email provider puts your account under review (Module 14), which threatens invoice delivery for every paying customer. Meanwhile support gets angry tickets from bookkeepers who were blocked for signing up from a shared office.

No one of the 140 rules is wrong. The problem is that the real signal is spread across dozens of weak hints (the email domain's age, how fast the form was filled in, whether the company name looks like the email address, how many customers the first invoice goes to, what time zone the browser reports), and the way those hints combine keeps changing. Writing and reweighting combinations of weak signals by hand, every week, is exactly the job people stop being good at.

**Attempt one: "let's use ML."** A data scientist exports every account, labels each one `fraud = suspended_for_abuse`, pulls 60 columns from the `accounts` table, shuffles, trains on 80% and tests on 20%. The model scores 0.99 on the test set. It ships behind a threshold, and in its first week in production it blocks almost nothing and misses most of the new campaign.

Every choice in that export was a reasonable default, and four of them were fatal:

- **It learned from the future.** The columns were read *today*. `last_login_at`, `invoices_bounced`, `support_notes_count` and `plan_status` all changed *because* the account was caught. Suspended accounts stop logging in. The model learned "accounts that were suspended look suspended," which is useless at 10:04 on the morning someone signs up.
- **It was asked the wrong question at the wrong moment.** The decision happens twice: at signup, and before the first invoice goes out. At those moments an account has perhaps 15 facts about it, not 60.
- **Its labels were the rules' opinion.** "Fraud" meant "caught by the rules or by support." Abuse that slipped through was labeled legitimate, and accounts blocked at signup never sent anything, so nobody ever learned whether they were really abusive. The model was trained to imitate the 140 rules, blind spots included.
- **The test set came from the same weeks as the training set.** Shuffling put siblings from the same campaign on both sides of the split. Real traffic always comes from next week, and next week's campaign looks different.

The model wasn't the problem. The framing was.

## What It Is

A hand-written rule is a decision function a person wrote. A **learned model** is a decision function fitted from examples: you show it many past situations along with what turned out to be true, and it finds the weighting of signals that best separates the outcomes. This is **supervised machine learning**, and most ML inside ordinary products is some version of it.

The part that decides whether it works isn't the algorithm. It's the **framing**: turning a vague product wish ("stop abuse," "show relevant items," "warn us before customers leave") into a precise prediction problem with five parts:

1. **The decision**: the moment something is decided, and what is known at that moment.
2. **The prediction**: the one number the model produces, such as "probability this trial sends a phishing invoice in the next 30 days."
3. **The label**: the observable outcome that says whether the prediction was right, and how long it takes to observe.
4. **The action**: what the system does with the number, including what it does in the uncertain middle.
5. **The baseline**: what you do today, measured the same way, so "better" has a meaning.

Two neighbors are worth separating now. **"AI" and "ML" aren't a choice between rules and magic.** Most production systems keep rules for the things that must be absolute (never let an unverified account send more than 50 invoices a day) and use a score for everything that is a judgment call. And **a classical model isn't an LLM.** A classical model is trained on *your* labels to produce *your* number. An LLM is a pretrained general model you instruct in words. For some problems below, the second has replaced the first; How It Works says where.

## Recognizing the Problem

Signs you've outgrown hand-written rules:

- The rule set grows every week, and rules are added far more often than removed.
- One person understands how the rules interact.
- Every threshold change fixes one complaint and causes another.
- Adversaries, or just changing user behavior, make a new rule stale within days.
- Humans reviewing cases "just know" in seconds, but can't write down why.

Signs an ML effort is framed wrong:

- Offline accuracy is spectacular and production results are mediocre.
- The model's most important features are *consequences* of the outcome: "days since last login" predicting churn, "number of refunds" predicting fraud.
- Training data comes from a current-state table rather than from what was known at decision time.
- The label is defined as "what our current process flagged."
- Nobody can say what the product does differently when the score is high.
- There's no measurement of the rules or process the model is replacing.

## How It Works

### The anatomy of a prediction

```
          decision point                                        label known
 signup ────────●──────────────────────────────────────────────────●──────▶ time
                │                                                  │
   features must come from here:                     complaint, abuse report,
   what was knowable at 10:04                        or confirmed "legitimate"
                │                                                  │
                └──── last_login_at, invoices_bounced, plan_status ┘
                      live in this gap: using them is leakage
```

Everything the model sees must be computed **as of the decision point**. Using information from the gap is called **leakage** (or target leakage). It's the most common reason an offline score doesn't survive contact with production, and it rarely looks like cheating. It looks like a convenient column. Any value that's updated after the decision is a suspect: counters, statuses, "last seen" timestamps, anything denormalized onto the current row.

The fix is structural, not a matter of care: **record features when the decision is made** (the Implementation below does exactly this), or reconstruct them with point-in-time queries against event history (Module 16's event log, or a feature store, 39.2). "Compute it again later from the tables" is what caused the 0.99.

The **label** has its own clock. If abuse shows up within 30 days, a decision made yesterday has no label yet. Training and evaluation can only use decisions whose **label window** has closed, which means the freshest data you can learn from is always at least a month old. Short label windows are one of the things that make a problem a good fit for ML.

### Four shapes the same idea takes

Most ML use cases in products fit one of four shapes. They differ mainly in where the labels come from.

**1. Scoring an entity (predictive analytics).** Fraud, churn, lead scoring, "will this invoice be paid late." Each case gets a probability, and a threshold turns it into an action. Labels come from future outcomes, so they arrive late, and some never arrive (the blocked account in the Problem). For tabular data like this, the strong defaults are logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, LightGBM). Trees still generally beat deep learning on this kind of data, and they're cheap to train and to serve.

One trap is specific to this shape: **predicting an outcome isn't the same as predicting who an action will change.** A churn model finds customers likely to leave, including many who'll leave whatever you do and many who'd stay without a discount. If the action is "send a retention offer," the question you actually care about is who *responds* to the offer. That's **uplift modeling**, and it needs data from a randomized experiment to learn from.

**2. Ranking and recommendation.** "Which 10 of these 20,000 templates, articles or products should this user see first?" There are no explicit labels, only **implicit feedback**: clicks, saves, purchases. Two problems come with that:

- **You only observe reactions to what you showed.** Items ranked first get clicked more partly because they're first (position bias), so a naive model learns to keep ranking them first.
- **New items and new users have no history** (cold start), so something non-learned has to handle them.

At scale, systems split the job in two: cheap **candidate generation** narrows 20,000 items to a few hundred, and a more expensive **ranker** orders those. At small scale, "most popular among customers like you" is a baseline that's hard to beat, and you should measure against it before building anything.

**3. Anomaly detection.** "Tell us when a tenant's usage, a payment pattern or an API client's traffic looks wrong." Usually there are *no* labels, so the model learns what normal looks like and flags distance from it. The catch is that **unusual isn't the same as bad.** A tenant's Black-Friday spike, a big new customer and a planned migration are all anomalies. Without a feedback loop ("this alert was useful / was noise"), anomaly detection becomes alert fatigue. Start with robust statistics before models: a per-tenant, per-hour-of-week baseline with a median and a spread measure catches most of what matters. Isolation forests and similar methods earn their place when many metrics have to be judged together.

**4. Understanding unstructured input (classical NLP and image recognition).** Routing support tickets, tagging documents, extracting fields from a receipt photo, detecting inappropriate uploads. This is the shape the last few years changed most. It used to mean collecting thousands of labeled examples and training a text or image classifier. Today the first option is usually a pretrained model you don't train at all:

- **An LLM or vision model you prompt** (39.3). Describe the 12 ticket queues, pass the ticket, get a category back. It needs zero labeled examples to start and handles messy, novel input well.
- **Embeddings from a pretrained model plus a small classical classifier** on top. You still need labels, but hundreds rather than tens of thousands, and prediction is fast and cheap.
- **A small model trained or fine-tuned for the one task.** This is still the right answer at high volume, or under tight latency or cost limits.

The deciding numbers are volume, latency and cost per decision. Suppose each LLM call costs a tenth of a cent and takes 800 ms. For 1,500 tickets a day, that's about $1.50 a day and nobody notices the latency: use the LLM. For a check on every one of 40 million API requests a day that has to finish in 5 ms, it's $40,000 a day and 160 times over the latency budget: train a small model, possibly using the LLM to label its training data. Many teams do exactly that: prototype with an LLM, log its answers, and later train a cheaper model on them.

### Feedback loops: the model decides what it learns from

```
  score ──▶ action ──▶ outcomes you get to observe ──▶ labels ──▶ next model
    ▲        (block)     (blocked accounts never send,              │
    │                     so their label never appears)             │
    └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Whenever a decision changes what happens next, it also changes which labels you collect. Blocked accounts produce no evidence about whether they were abusive. Unshown items produce no clicks. Customers who got the retention offer produce outcomes contaminated by the offer. This is the **selective labels** problem. Left alone, each new model is trained on data filtered by the previous one, and errors become invisible because the evidence against them is never gathered.

The remedy is a small, deliberate **exploration** budget: a random slice of decisions where you take a milder action than the score suggests, so the truth can surface. For signup abuse, that means sending a random 2% of would-be blocks to a *limited* mode (five invoices a day, links rewritten through your checkout) instead of blocking them. The damage is bounded, and you learn how often a block would have been wrong. For recommendations, it means occasionally showing items the ranker didn't pick. It costs something every day, and it's the only way to know how good the system actually is.

### Evaluate the way you'll deploy

An offline evaluation is a rehearsal of production. It's only as honest as its resemblance to production:

- **Split by time, not at random.** Train on decisions before a date, test on decisions after it, with the label window closed on both sides. This measures what you actually need: how well last month predicts next month.
- **Measure at the operating point.** A single summary score (AUC and similar) averages over thresholds you'll never use. What matters is behavior at the threshold you'll ship: if the operations team can review 200 accounts a day, how many of the model's top 200 are really abusive?
- **Count errors in the product's units.** A false positive here is a paying bookkeeper who can't send invoices. A false negative is 3,000 phishing emails from your domain. They aren't the same size, and the threshold should reflect that.
- **Compare against the baseline on the same data.** The 140 rules flag a set of accounts; take the model's top accounts of the same size and compare both on caught abuse and real customers blocked. If the model doesn't beat the rules there, it doesn't ship, however good its summary score looks.

## When It's Appropriate

- **High-volume judgment calls.** 4,000 trial signups a day, each needing an allow/limit/block decision in under a second. Humans can't review them all, and rules can't keep up with how the signals combine.
- **Many weak signals, none decisive.** Fraud and abuse, lead scoring and late-payment risk, where 30 facts each shift the odds a little.
- **Patterns that drift faster than people can rewrite rules.** Adversarial settings especially: a model retrained weekly on fresh labels adapts without anyone writing a new rule.
- **Labels that arrive on their own, quickly.** Complaints within days, chargebacks within weeks, clicks within seconds. The faster and cheaper the label, the better the fit.
- **Ranking a large catalog for many users.** 20,000 items and 50,000 users is past what curated lists can personalize.
- **Watching thousands of similar series.** Usage for 3,000 tenants, where a person can't eyeball every chart and a single global threshold fits nobody.
- **Unstructured input at volume.** 1,500 support tickets a day into 12 queues, or photos of receipts: a prompted pretrained model is often enough on day one.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Low volume.** A few hundred decisions a week can be reviewed by a person, whose judgment is also generating the labels you'd need later.
- **A rule catches nearly everything.** If "hold trial invoices with external payment links" stops 90% of the damage, ship the rule and measure what's left before reaching for a model.
- **No label, and no way to get one.** If you can't eventually observe whether a prediction was right, you can't train, evaluate or monitor a supervised model. (Anomaly detection is the exception, and it pays for that with noise.)
- **No action attached.** A churn score on a dashboard that nobody acts on is a cost with no return. Name the action first.
- **Decisions that must be fully explainable to the person affected**, such as regulated credit or employment decisions. Learned models are allowed in many of these, but explanation, audit and fairness obligations change what model you can use and how much work it takes. Don't discover that after building it.
- **No owner for the model after launch.** A model is a living dependency (see Trade-offs). If nobody will watch its inputs, its labels and its error rates, a well-kept rule set is safer.

## Trade-offs

- **Failures are silent.** A broken rule throws an error or blocks everyone. A degraded model keeps returning plausible scores while getting steadily worse. You need monitoring on the inputs, the score distribution and the delayed labels (39.2), and that monitoring is permanent work.
- **Everything is entangled.** Change one feature's definition, the upstream event schema or the logging code, and the model's behavior changes in ways no test catches. Sculley et al. call this "changing anything changes everything." Data pipelines that feed models become production code with production-grade review.
- **Behavior is a distribution, not a spec.** You can't write an assertion that the model is right. You can only measure how often it's wrong, on data that's already at least a label window old.
- **Explanations are harder.** "Rule 88 fired" is easy to show a support agent. "The score was 0.93" needs feature attributions, and those are approximations.
- **Feedback loops cost money to break.** Exploration means deliberately letting some would-be blocks through in limited form, every day, forever.
- **Bias arrives with the labels.** If past decisions were unfair to some group (blocking signups from certain countries, say), a model trained on them repeats that with more confidence and less visibility.
- **New infrastructure and skills.** Training is mostly a Python ecosystem, even when serving happens in TypeScript, Go or Java. Someone has to own the path from logged data to trained model to deployed scorer.

## Alternatives

- **Better rules, organized.** A scoring table (each rule adds points, a threshold decides), version control, a test set of known cases and a monthly review to delete rules. This postpones ML for a long time in many products, and it becomes the baseline if you move on later.
- **Human review with a queue.** Rules route uncertain cases to people. It scales poorly, but it's precise, and every decision is a clean label.
- **Buy the model.** Where the problem is common and the data isn't uniquely yours, a specialist often has more signal than you do: payment providers' fraud scoring, email-validation and IP-reputation services, managed recommendation services. You give up control and explanation, and your data leaves your system.
- **Simple statistics.** For anomaly detection, seasonal baselines and robust thresholds. For recommendation, popularity by segment. Both are cheap, explainable and surprisingly strong.
- **Friction instead of prediction.** Sometimes the cheapest defense is changing the product: require card verification before a trial can send externally, or cap trial sending volume. Abuse becomes unprofitable without anyone predicting who's abusive.

## How to Achieve It

Most of the work happens before any model exists.

**1. Write the framing down.** Decision point, prediction, label and label window, action (including the middle band), and how the current process will be measured. One page.
_Move on when_ someone outside the team can read it and say what the product does when the score is 0.9.

**2. Measure the baseline.** How many abusive accounts do the rules catch, and how many real customers do they block, per week? This is the number every later step has to beat.

**3. Log decisions with their features.** At each decision point, record what was known, what the rules decided and what the system did. Start this *weeks* before you need a training set; you can't backfill it honestly.
_Move on when_ one full label window of logged decisions has accumulated.

**4. Add exploration and a label job.** A small random slice of would-be blocks goes to limited mode. A daily job attaches outcomes to decisions whose window has closed.

**5. Train a simple model offline and compare.** Time-based split, same-size comparison against the rules, in product units. Logistic regression or gradient-boosted trees first.
_Move on when_ it beats the baseline on both caught abuse and customers blocked.

**6. Run it in shadow mode.** Score live traffic and log the score, but don't act on it. Check that the production score distribution looks like the offline one. If it doesn't, you have leakage or a feature computed differently in the two places (39.2 calls this training–serving skew).

**7. Act on it gradually.** Start with the milder action (limited mode, not block) on high scores, keep the rules as a floor, and widen as the delayed labels confirm the offline numbers. Then set up retraining and monitoring (39.2).

## Implementation

The implementation is in TypeScript, and it deliberately contains no training code. Training usually happens in Python, and what fits in there changes yearly. What decides whether the model can work at all lives in the product code: what gets recorded at decision time, and how the result is judged.

### Record the decision, not just the outcome

```ts
type Action = "allow" | "limited" | "block";

interface DecisionRecord {
  decisionId: string;
  accountId: string;
  point: "signup" | "first_send";
  decidedAt: Date;
  features: RiskFeatures;          // exactly what the scorer saw, frozen
  ruleVerdict: Action;
  modelScore?: number;             // shadow mode: logged, not acted on
  modelVersion?: string;
  action: Action;                  // what the system actually did
  explored: boolean;               // randomly softened from "block" to "limited"
}

const EXPLORE_RATE = 0.02;

async function decideFirstSend(account: Account, invoice: Invoice): Promise<Action> {
  const decidedAt = new Date();
  const features = await featuresAsOf(account.id, invoice, decidedAt);
  const ruleVerdict = rules.evaluate(features);

  // Shadow scoring must never be able to block a send: a timeout is just "no score".
  const modelScore = await scorer.score(features, { timeoutMs: 50 }).catch(() => undefined);

  let action = ruleVerdict;
  const explored = action === "block" && Math.random() < EXPLORE_RATE;
  if (explored) action = "limited";

  await decisionLog.append({
    decisionId: crypto.randomUUID(), accountId: account.id, point: "first_send",
    decidedAt, features, ruleVerdict, modelScore, modelVersion: scorer.version,
    action, explored,
  });
  return action;
}
```

> **Why store the features instead of recomputing them later.** `featuresAsOf` is the only place leakage can be prevented. If training later rebuilds features from the `accounts` table, it will read `plan_status = 'suspended'` for exactly the accounts that turned out to be abusive, and you're back at 0.99. Freezing the input at decision time also gives you the thing step 6 needs: the exact values the live scorer saw, to compare with what training saw.

> **The log write is on the critical path.** If `append` fails, you've made a decision you can't learn from. Writing it to an outbox in the same transaction as the send (the transactional outbox from Module 37) avoids losing it; dropping it silently biases the training set toward whatever conditions make logging fail.

### Judge it the way it will be used

```ts
interface Labeled extends DecisionRecord { abusive: boolean } // label window closed

// Rows are decisions made AFTER the model's training cutoff, so the model never saw them.
function compareWithRules(rows: Labeled[]) {
  const flaggedByRules = rows.filter((r) => r.ruleVerdict !== "allow");
  const budget = flaggedByRules.length;          // same number of accounts touched
  const flaggedByModel = [...rows]
    .filter((r) => r.modelScore !== undefined)
    .sort((a, b) => b.modelScore! - a.modelScore!)
    .slice(0, budget);

  const summarize = (flagged: Labeled[]) => ({
    abuseCaught: flagged.filter((r) => r.abusive).length,
    customersHit: flagged.filter((r) => !r.abusive).length,
  });
  return { rules: summarize(flaggedByRules), model: summarize(flaggedByModel) };
}
```

> **Where the comparison still lies.** Labels only exist for accounts that were allowed or limited. Among rule-blocked accounts, only the explored 2% have outcomes, so each one stands for about 50 blocked accounts. A fair comparison weights them that way (inverse-propensity weighting). Leaving the weights out makes the rules look better or worse than they are, depending on how the explored accounts happened to behave.

## Further Reading

- **Martin Zinkevich, "Rules of Machine Learning: Best Practices for ML Engineering"** (Google). Its first rules say to launch without ML if you have to, and to build metrics before models, which is the order this article recommends.
- **D. Sculley et al., "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems"** (NeurIPS 2015). The source of "changing anything changes everything," and a map of why the model is the small part of an ML system.
- **Chip Huyen, _Designing Machine Learning Systems_** (O'Reilly, 2022). Chapters on framing, training data and labels, and data leakage cover this article's ground in more depth.
- **Shachar Kaufman et al., "Leakage in Data Mining: Formulation, Detection, and Avoidance"** (ACM TKDD, 2012). The standard treatment of leakage, with real competition examples.
- **Himabindu Lakkaraju et al., "The Selective Labels Problem"** (KDD 2017). Why decisions hide their own mistakes, and how to evaluate anyway.
- **scikit-learn documentation, "Common pitfalls and recommended practices"** and **`TimeSeriesSplit`**. Short and practical on leakage and time-ordered evaluation.
- **Léo Grinsztajn et al., "Why do tree-based models still outperform deep learning on typical tabular data?"** (NeurIPS 2022). The evidence behind "start with gradient-boosted trees" for tabular scoring.
- **Paul Covington et al., "Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations"** (RecSys 2016). The candidate-generation-plus-ranking split, from a system at the far end of the scale.
- **Fei Tony Liu et al., "Isolation Forest"** (ICDM 2008). The anomaly-detection method to reach for after robust statistics.
- **In this collection:** Module 16 (event tracking, the raw material for point-in-time features), Module 14 (email deliverability, the cost in the Problem), Module 18 (rate limits, the first rules), Module 26 (feature flags and A/B testing, for gradual rollout and experiments), Module 37 (transactional outbox), 39.2 (serving, training–serving skew, drift and retraining), 39.3 (prompting an LLM for classification and extraction).

---

The habit to take from this: when someone proposes "adding AI" to a feature, ask the five framing questions before anything else. What's decided, when, with what knowledge? What outcome proves it right, and how long until you see it? And what do you do today, measured the same way? If those can't be answered, the model won't fix it. If they can, the model is often the easy part.
