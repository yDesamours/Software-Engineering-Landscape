# Answers from Your Own Documents: The Core of Retrieval-Augmented Generation

_Module 39.4a — Machine Learning & LLM-Powered Systems_

## The Problem

Suppose you build an HR platform for mid-sized companies. Each customer company uploads its policies: the employee handbook, the benefits guide, the travel and expense policy, leave rules for each province or state, often as PDFs. Employees keep asking HR the same questions, so you add **Ask HR**: an employee types a question and gets an answer from their own company's documents.

A typical customer has 180 documents, around 2 million tokens. That's too much to send with every question (39.3b showed what that costs), so the first version takes the standard approach:

1. Extract the text from every PDF and split it into pieces of 1,000 characters.
2. Turn each piece into an **embedding**, a list of numbers representing its meaning, and store it.
3. When an employee asks a question, embed the question, find the three most similar pieces, and give them to the model along with the question.

It demos well. In the pilot with three customers, HR teams collect the failures:

- **"How many weeks of parental leave do I get in Quebec?"** The answer, 18 weeks, is in a table. The 1,000-character split put the table's header row in one piece and the Quebec row in the next, so the retrieved piece reads `Quebec | 18 | 5 | 55%`. The model can't tell which number is weeks, and answers "12 weeks."
- **"What's the meal allowance in Montréal?"** The number is in "Schedule B" at the end of the travel policy, a table of cities and amounts that never uses the words "meal" or "allowance." The search returns three paragraphs *about* meal allowances, none with the number. The model says the policy "provides a daily meal allowance that varies by city."
- **"Can I bring my dog to the office?"** No document mentions pets. The three most similar pieces are about office hours and visitors. The model answers "Pets are generally welcome with manager approval," which is plausible, and appears in no policy anywhere.
- **"What's the home-office stipend?"** Both the 2025 and 2026 handbooks were uploaded. The search returns pieces of each, and the answer gives the 2025 amount.
- **Nobody can say why an answer was wrong.** Did the search miss the right text, or did the model misread text it was given? The team only has examples of bad answers, and the next change to the splitting fixes some and breaks others.

Every one of these answers came back fluent and confident. The model only knows what the search gives it, and the search only finds what the splitting and embedding made findable.

## What It Is

**Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** answers questions from your own documents by retrieving the passages most likely to contain the answer, placing them in the prompt, and instructing the model to answer from those passages, and to cite them.

It has two halves that run at different times:

- **Indexing**, done when documents change: extract text, split it into **chunks**, compute an embedding for each chunk, and store the chunks with their embeddings and metadata.
- **Answering**, done for each question: embed the question, **retrieve** the chunks whose embeddings are closest (filtered to the right company and the current documents), then **generate** an answer grounded in them.

The idea underneath is that a model is good at reading and reasoning over text it's given, and unreliable at recalling specific facts it was never given, like one company's leave policy. RAG supplies the facts at question time. It's also the practical way to keep answers current (update the documents, not the model), to delete information (remove the chunks), to keep one customer's data out of another's answers, and to show sources.

Two neighbors are often proposed instead:

- **Fine-tuning** a model on the documents changes how the model writes and what it tends to say, but it's an unreliable way to teach specific facts. It can't cite sources, can't be updated per document, and would mix every customer's policies together.
- **Long context**, putting all the documents into every prompt, works when the collection is small enough (see *When It's Unnecessary*). At 2 million tokens per customer, it doesn't fit, and 39.3b covers the cost and quality problems when it nearly does.

## Recognizing the Problem

Signs you need retrieval:

- Users ask questions whose answers are in documents you have and the model has never seen: your policies, your product docs, your contracts, your tickets.
- The collection is too large to include in every prompt, or it changes often.
- Answers need to cite where they came from, or vary by customer, team or permission.

Signs a retrieval pipeline is failing:

- Answers are fluent but wrong on specific numbers, names or dates.
- Answers draw on tables incorrectly or ignore them.
- Questions with no answer in the documents get a plausible answer anyway.
- Answers mix old and new versions of a document.
- The retrieved chunks are "about the topic" without containing the answer.
- Chunks start or end mid-sentence or mid-table.
- Nobody has measured whether the right passage is retrieved for a set of known questions.

## How It Works

### The pipeline

```
 INDEXING (when documents change)
  PDF/DOCX/HTML ─▶ parse ─▶ chunk by structure ─▶ add context ─▶ embed ─▶ store
                   text,     sections, whole       title, section         chunk + vector +
                   headings, tables, heading       path, effective        tenant, document,
                   tables    path per chunk        dates                  version, dates

 ANSWERING (each question)
  question ─▶ embed ─▶ search: nearest chunks WHERE tenant = … AND current ─▶ top k
          ─▶ prompt: numbered sources + rules ─▶ answer with [citations] ─▶ check citations
```

Each stage can lose the answer, and a later stage can't recover what an earlier one lost. If parsing turns a table into a jumble of numbers, no embedding model will make it searchable. If the right chunk isn't retrieved, no prompt will make the answer correct. That's why failures have to be located by stage (last section) rather than judged by the final answer alone.

### Embeddings

An **embedding model** turns a piece of text into a fixed-length list of numbers (a vector, typically a few hundred to a few thousand numbers) such that texts with similar meanings produce vectors pointing in similar directions. "How much parental leave do I get?" and "Employees are entitled to 18 weeks of leave following the birth or adoption of a child" end up close together, even though they share almost no words. Closeness is usually measured with **cosine similarity**, the angle between two vectors.

Embedding models are separate from chat models, cheap to call, and offered by most AI providers (OpenAI, Google, Cohere, and Voyage, which Anthropic's documentation points to since Anthropic doesn't offer its own), as well as open-weight models you can run yourself. What matters when choosing one:

- **Use the same model for documents and questions.** Vectors from different models, or different versions of one model, aren't comparable. Changing models means re-embedding everything (39.4b).
- **Mark questions and documents as such if the model supports it.** Many models take an input type (`query` or `document`), because a short question and a long passage with the same meaning are phrased very differently.
- **Check languages and domain.** A model trained mostly on English web text may do poorly on French policy documents or on medical terms. Test with your own questions.

Embeddings capture topic and meaning. They're weak at the things the Problem's failures turned on:

- **Exact identifiers:** policy numbers, product codes, names.
- **Specific numbers.**
- **Negation.** "Pets are not allowed" is close to "pets are allowed."
- **The difference between discussing a topic and answering a question about it.**

"Most similar" is not the same as "contains the answer." The Schedule B failure is exactly that gap. 39.4b adds keyword search alongside embeddings to cover the identifier and exact-term cases.

### Parsing: where most answers are lost

Getting clean text out of real documents is unglamorous and decides more quality than any other step:

- **PDFs don't contain paragraphs or tables.** They contain characters placed at coordinates. Extractors have to rebuild reading order, and they get it wrong for multi-column layouts, headers and footers repeated on every page, footnotes and tables.
- **Tables are the worst case.** A plain text extractor produces the cell values in a row with the column meanings lost, which is exactly what made `Quebec | 18 | 5 | 55%` meaningless.
- **Scanned documents need OCR** (optical character recognition) first.

Layout-aware parsers recognize headings, lists and tables and output structured text, often Markdown. Open-source options include Docling and Unstructured, and cloud providers have document-analysis services. Some teams use a vision-capable LLM to convert difficult pages, which is slower and costlier but handles layouts that defeat rule-based tools. Whatever you use, look at the output for a sample of real documents. Parsing bugs are invisible from the answer end.

### Chunking

A **chunk** is the unit you embed and retrieve. Its size is a real trade-off:

- **Too small** (a sentence or two): the embedding is precise, but the chunk lacks context. "It is 18 weeks" means nothing without knowing what "it" is.
- **Too large** (several pages): the chunk carries context, but its embedding is an average of many topics, so it matches no question strongly. Each retrieved chunk also costs more tokens in the prompt.

Typical sizes are a few hundred to about a thousand tokens. More important than the number is **where the boundaries fall**:

- **Fixed-size splitting** (every N characters, with some overlap between neighboring chunks) is the simplest. It cuts sentences, lists and tables in half, as it did to the Quebec row.
- **Structure-aware splitting** follows the document's own structure. It splits at headings, keeps each section together if it fits, and splits long sections at paragraph boundaries. It never splits a table row from its header. This is the right default for policies, manuals and documentation, which are written in sections.
- **Semantic splitting** puts boundaries where the topic changes, measured by comparing embeddings of consecutive sentences. It helps with unstructured text, such as transcripts or long emails, that has no headings to follow.

Tables need their own treatment. Convert each row into a self-describing line that repeats the column names ("Province: Quebec. Weeks of parental leave: 18. Weeks for the second parent: 5. Pay: 55% of salary."), and keep the table's caption and section with it. Every row then carries its own meaning, whichever chunk it lands in.

### Giving each chunk its context

A chunk that reads "Employees may claim up to $85 per day" doesn't say which policy, which country or which year. Two techniques add that context:

- **Heading paths and document facts.** Prefix each chunk with the document's title, its section path ("Travel & Expense Policy 2026 › Schedule B › Canada") and its effective dates before embedding. This costs nothing and fixes many "found the right text but didn't know what it was" failures, including Schedule B, whose rows can now match "meal allowance" through the section's context.
- **Generated context.** For each chunk, ask an LLM to write a sentence or two situating it within the whole document, and prepend that. Anthropic's "Contextual Retrieval" study found this reduced retrieval failures by 35% on its own, and by 49% when combined with keyword search. This is an indexing-time cost, paid once per chunk (and cheap with prompt caching, since the whole document is the same prefix for every chunk in it).

A related pattern is **small-to-big retrieval**: search over small, precise chunks, but put the larger section each one belongs to into the prompt. The search matches precisely, and the model reads with enough context.

### Retrieval: filters first, then similarity

Retrieval finds the `k` chunks whose embeddings are closest to the question's, but only among chunks the question is allowed to see and that are currently valid:

- **Tenant.** A question from an employee at one company must only ever search that company's documents. This filter is a security boundary (Module 5), so it belongs inside the query, not applied afterward to the results. Filtering after retrieval can also leave zero results when the nearest chunks all belong to other tenants, and the model then answers from nothing. How vector indexes behave under filters is 39.4b.
- **Validity.** Store each document's version and effective dates, and retrieve only current versions by default. The home-office stipend failure came from treating two versions of a handbook as two equally valid documents. If a question asks about the past ("What was the stipend last year?"), that's a deliberate, different filter.
- **How many.** Retrieving five to twenty chunks is typical. More chunks raise the chance the answer is included and raise cost and distraction. A minimum similarity threshold can drop chunks that are only weakly related, but the right threshold depends on the embedding model and has to be tuned on your data.

### Generation: answer from the sources, or say so

The prompt (39.3a's structure applies) gives the model:

- the retrieved chunks, each numbered and labeled with its document, section and effective date
- instructions to answer only from those sources, cite each claim with the source number, and say plainly when the sources don't contain the answer
- instructions for conflicts: if sources disagree, prefer the most recent effective date and mention the difference

That handles the dog question: with nothing relevant retrieved, the correct answer is "I couldn't find a policy about pets. You may want to ask HR directly," which is more useful to an employee than a plausible invention.

The instructions don't guarantee compliance, so check the output:

- **Every citation must refer to a source that was actually provided.** A citation to "[7]" when five sources were given is a fabrication.
- **Show the cited sources to the user**, with links to the document section. People then check important answers themselves, and a wrong answer is visibly wrong rather than quietly trusted.
- **For higher stakes, verify that each cited passage supports the claim.** That's a second, cheaper model call, or a structured-output answer where each claim carries a quote you can check against the source text, as in 39.3a.

### Measuring retrieval separately from answers

A wrong answer has two possible causes: the answer wasn't retrieved, or it was retrieved and the model got it wrong. They have different fixes, so measure them separately.

Build a **retrieval test set**: 50–200 real questions, each labeled with the chunk or passage that answers it (and some with no answer in the documents). Then measure:

- **Recall@k**: the fraction of questions where an answering chunk appears in the top `k` results. If recall@10 is 70%, then 30% of questions can't be answered correctly whatever the prompt says, and the work belongs in parsing, chunking or retrieval.
- **Answer quality given correct retrieval**: whether the answer is correct and faithful to the sources when the right chunk was included. Evaluating that at scale (graders, LLM-as-judge) is 39.7.

Run the retrieval set on every change to parsing, chunking, embedding model or retrieval settings. It turns "the new chunking feels better" into "recall@10 went from 71% to 89%, and table questions went from 40% to 92%."

## When It's Appropriate

- **Question answering over documents you own that change over time:** policies, product documentation, internal wikis, contracts, support history. A new handbook becomes answerable as soon as it's indexed.
- **Per-customer or per-user knowledge in a multi-tenant product.** Each company's employees get answers only from that company's documents, enforced by a filter.
- **Collections too large for the prompt.** At 2 million tokens per customer, sending everything is impossible or unaffordable.
- **Answers that must show their sources:** HR, legal, compliance, healthcare policy and support, where an answer without a source isn't trusted or allowed.
- **Frequent updates and deletions.** Removing a chunk removes it from every future answer, which matters for superseded policies and for deletion requests (Module 34).

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Small collections.** If a customer's documents fit comfortably in the context window (Anthropic suggests under about 200,000 tokens, roughly 500 pages), put them all in the prompt and use prompt caching (39.3b). There are no chunking bugs, no retrieval misses, and it's often better quality. Several of the pilot customers had small handbooks and would have been better served this way.
- **Structured data.** "How many vacation days do I have left?" is a database query, not a document search. Give the model a tool that looks it up (39.5).
- **Questions ordinary search already answers.** If users want to find a document, not get an answer, a good search page with snippets is simpler, faster and more trusted.
- **When a few documents cover almost every question.** An FAQ or a set of reviewed answers for the 30 most common questions may beat a general system on accuracy and cost.
- **Stable general knowledge.** Questions the model already answers reliably from training ("What's a 401(k)?") don't need retrieval, although in a product like this you usually want even those grounded in the company's own documents.

## Trade-offs

- **A pipeline with many stages, each able to fail silently.** Parsing, chunking, embedding, filtering and generation each need their own checks, and a failure in any of them surfaces only as a worse answer.
- **Retrieval sets a ceiling on quality.** A great model can't answer from a passage it wasn't given, and it will often produce a confident answer anyway.
- **Indexing costs and delays.** Parsing, generating context and embedding cost money and time for every new or changed document, and answers lag behind the documents until indexing finishes (39.4b covers keeping up).
- **Chunking choices trade precision against context,** and a setting that works for handbooks may fail for spreadsheets or transcripts. Mixed collections need more than one strategy.
- **Similarity isn't relevance.** Embedding search finds text about a topic, not necessarily the text that answers the question, and it's weak on identifiers and numbers.
- **Another copy of sensitive data.** Chunks and embeddings are derived copies of the documents. They inherit every access rule, retention period and deletion obligation of the originals (39.4b, Module 34). Embeddings can also leak some of the text they were computed from, so treat them as sensitive.
- **Answers still need verification.** Citations make errors checkable, not impossible.

## Alternatives

- **Long context with caching**, for collections that fit (see above).
- **Keyword search**, such as Postgres full-text search or Elasticsearch (Module 12), with or without an LLM summarizing results. It's strong on exact terms, identifiers and numbers, and it's the other half of hybrid search in 39.4b.
- **Tools over structured data** (39.5), when the answer lives in database rows rather than documents.
- **Agentic search** (39.6): the model decides what to search for, reads results, and searches again. It handles questions that need several lookups ("Compare the leave policies in Quebec and Ontario"), at the cost of more calls and less predictable latency.
- **Curated answers**: a maintained FAQ or knowledge base of reviewed answers, served by search or by exact matching of common questions.
- **Fine-tuning**, for style, format or domain vocabulary, usually in combination with retrieval, not as a replacement for it.

## How to Achieve It

**1. Collect questions and label the answers.** Gather 50–200 real questions from support tickets or HR inboxes. For each one, note where the answer is in the documents, or that it isn't there.
_Move on when_ the set includes table questions, questions using exact terms or identifiers, questions with no answer, and questions affected by multiple versions.

**2. Check whether you need retrieval at all.** Count each tenant's tokens. For small collections, try the whole collection in the prompt with caching, and measure it on the question set.

**3. Get parsing right.** Use a layout-aware parser, convert tables to self-describing rows, and inspect output for a sample of real documents.

**4. Chunk by structure, with context.** Split on headings and paragraphs, keep table rows with their headers, and prefix each chunk with its title, section path and dates. Store tenant, document, version and effective dates with each chunk.

**5. Measure retrieval.** Compute recall@k on the question set, broken down by question type.
_Move on when_ recall@10 is high enough that the remaining failures are worth fixing with the techniques in 39.4b (hybrid search, re-ranking), not with basic parsing and chunking.

**6. Generate with citations and check them.** Number the sources, instruct the model to cite and to say when there's no answer, validate that citations exist, and show sources in the UI.

**7. Launch with feedback.** Let users mark answers as helpful or not, and route "not found" and unhelpful answers to the HR team. They're the next additions to the question set, and sometimes a sign that a policy is missing.

## Implementation

The implementation is TypeScript with PostgreSQL and the pgvector extension. That means the embeddings live in the same database as everything else, the tenant filter is an ordinary `WHERE` clause, and there's no new system to run. It has three pieces: the chunk table, a structure-aware chunker, and answering with filtered retrieval and checked citations. The embedding and chat calls are wrapped in small functions, since the providers differ only in details.

### The chunk table

```ts
await db.query(`
  CREATE EXTENSION IF NOT EXISTS vector;

  CREATE TABLE doc_chunks (
    id             bigserial PRIMARY KEY,
    tenant_id      uuid        NOT NULL,
    document_id    uuid        NOT NULL,
    version        int         NOT NULL,
    effective_from date        NOT NULL,
    effective_to   date,                       -- null: still in force
    heading_path   text        NOT NULL,       -- "Travel & Expense 2026 › Schedule B › Canada"
    content        text        NOT NULL,       -- what the model reads
    embedding      vector(1024) NOT NULL       -- of heading_path + content, from ONE model
  );
  CREATE INDEX ON doc_chunks (tenant_id, document_id);
`);
```

> **Why effective dates and not "delete the old version."** Deleting 2025's handbook when 2026's arrives would fix the stipend question, but some questions are legitimately about the past ("What was the policy when I was hired?"), and some documents take effect on a future date. Storing validity dates makes "current" a filter you choose per question, not a destructive step at upload time.

### A structure-aware chunker

```ts
interface Section { headingPath: string[]; blocks: Block[] }   // from a layout-aware parser
type Block = { kind: "paragraph"; text: string }
           | { kind: "table"; caption?: string; header: string[]; rows: string[][] };

const MAX_TOKENS = 500;
const approxTokens = (s: string) => Math.ceil(s.length / 4);

export function chunkSection(docTitle: string, section: Section): { headingPath: string; content: string }[] {
  const headingPath = [docTitle, ...section.headingPath].join(" › ");
  const chunks: { headingPath: string; content: string }[] = [];
  let current: string[] = [];

  const flush = () => {
    if (current.length) chunks.push({ headingPath, content: current.join("\n\n") });
    current = [];
  };
  const add = (text: string) => {
    if (approxTokens([...current, text].join("\n\n")) > MAX_TOKENS) flush();
    current.push(text);          // a single oversized paragraph becomes its own chunk
  };

  for (const block of section.blocks) {
    if (block.kind === "paragraph") add(block.text);
    else for (const row of block.rows) {
      // Each row carries its own meaning: column names travel with the values.
      const described = block.header
        .flatMap((h, i) => (row[i]?.trim() ? [`${h}: ${row[i].trim()}`] : []))   // skip empty cells
        .join(". ");
      add(block.caption ? `${block.caption}. ${described}.` : `${described}.`);
    }
  }
  flush();
  return chunks;                 // chunks never cross a section boundary
}
```

> **What this fixes and what it doesn't.** The Quebec row becomes "Parental leave by province. Province: Quebec. Weeks of parental leave: 18. …", which is findable and readable on its own. The Schedule B rows gain "Travel & Expense 2026 › Schedule B › Canada" in their heading path, which is embedded with them. What the chunker can't fix is bad input. If the parser flattened the table into loose numbers, there are no `header` and `rows` to work with. That's why step 3 of How to Achieve It (inspecting the parser's output) comes before chunking.

### Retrieve, answer, check

```ts
export async function answer(tenantId: string, question: string, today: string) {
  const [q] = await embed([question], { inputType: "query" });

  const { rows: sources } = await db.query(
    `SELECT id, heading_path, content, effective_from, 1 - (embedding <=> $1) AS similarity
       FROM doc_chunks
      WHERE tenant_id = $2                                          -- security boundary, never post-filtered
        AND effective_from <= $3 AND (effective_to IS NULL OR effective_to > $3)
      ORDER BY embedding <=> $1
      LIMIT 8`,
    [toSql(q), tenantId, today]);

  const numbered = sources.map((s, i) =>
    `<source n="${i + 1}" section="${s.heading_path}" effective="${s.effective_from}">\n${s.content}\n</source>`);

  const reply = await chat({
    system: `You answer employees' questions using only their company's policy excerpts.
Cite every factual claim as [n] using the source numbers. If the sources do not answer the question,
say you couldn't find it in the company's policies and suggest asking HR. Do not use general knowledge
about what companies usually do. If sources conflict, prefer the latest effective date and say so.`,
    user: `${numbered.join("\n\n")}\n\nQuestion: ${question}`,
  });

  const cited = [...reply.matchAll(/\[(\d+)\]/g)].map((m) => Number(m[1]));
  const invalid = cited.filter((n) => n < 1 || n > sources.length);
  if (invalid.length) return { status: "needs_review" as const, reply, sources };

  return {
    status: cited.length ? ("answered" as const) : ("not_found" as const),
    reply,
    sources: [...new Set(cited)].map((n) => sources[n - 1]),   // shown under the answer, with links
  };
}
```

> **The tenant filter is in the query, not after it.** With the `WHERE tenant_id = $2` inside the search, the nearest chunks are always the nearest *within that company*. Without any index, pgvector scans every row that matches the filter, which is exact and fast enough for tens of thousands of chunks per tenant. Once you add an approximate vector index for speed, filtered queries can return fewer than eight rows unless the index is configured for it (pgvector 0.8 added iterative scans for this). That, and choosing between pgvector and dedicated vector databases, is 39.4b.

> **`not_found` is a feature.** An answer with no citations is treated as "the policies don't cover this," and it routes to HR. Counting `not_found` answers by topic shows which policies employees need and don't have, which is often the most valuable report the feature produces.

---

The habit to take from this: when a RAG answer is wrong, don't start by changing the prompt. First check whether the right passage was retrieved. If it wasn't, check whether it was chunked sensibly, and before that, whether it was parsed correctly. Most failures happen well before the model sees anything, and the fix belongs where the answer was lost.
