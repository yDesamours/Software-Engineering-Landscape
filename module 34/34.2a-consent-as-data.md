# Consent as Data: Banners, Signals, and Enforcing Choices Where Data Is Used

_Module 34.2a — Legal & Compliance Considerations_

> Engineering shape, not legal advice. Consent rules differ by country, by purpose and — in the US — by state, and they're changing: the EU is negotiating changes to its cookie rules, and California will require browsers to offer an opt-out signal from 2027. What this article teaches is how to build a system where whatever the rules are can be applied consistently and proved afterwards.

## The Problem

Suppose you run a language-learning app: a marketing site, a web app and two mobile apps. The free tier shows ads; the paid tier doesn't. Marketing runs paid acquisition and measures it with conversion pixels. Product uses an analytics SDK. There's a newsletter.

A year ago someone installed a cookie-banner product. It shows "Accept all" and "Settings." Everyone considered consent done.

Then a complaint reaches a European regulator, and the team is asked to demonstrate compliance. What they find over the next two weeks:

- **The pixels fire before anyone clicks anything.** The tag manager loads on page load; the banner script loads alongside it and asks the question after the ad and analytics tags have already set their cookies.
- **Rejecting takes four clicks; accepting takes one.** "Reject all" lives on the second screen of the banner.
- **The web choice doesn't reach the app.** A user who rejected advertising on the website logs into the mobile app, whose analytics and ad SDKs initialize with their own defaults.
- **The backend ignores the banner entirely.** Signups are sent to an ad platform's server-side conversion API from the backend, for every user, because the banner only controls scripts in the browser.
- **Newsletter consent can't be proved.** The signup checkbox was pre-ticked for eight months, nobody recorded what text people saw, and "subscribed: true" is all the database knows.
- **Withdrawal is slow.** Users who turn off marketing emails in settings keep receiving them for up to a week, because the list syncs to the email vendor on Sundays.
- **Californian users with Global Privacy Control turned on** in their browsers are tracked for advertising anyway. Nobody reads the signal.

Every one of these has a banner in front of it that looks correct. The problem isn't the banner. It's that consent was treated as a piece of UI, when it's a piece of data that every system using personal data has to consult.

## What It Is

**Consent** is a person's recorded choice about a specific use of their data. Two families of law ask for it in different shapes:

- **Opt-in (EU, UK and similar).** Some uses may not start until the person says yes. The EU's ePrivacy rules require consent before storing or reading information on someone's device — cookies, local storage, SDK device identifiers, tracking pixels, fingerprinting — unless it's strictly necessary for a service they asked for. Separately, where the GDPR's lawful basis for a purpose is consent (34.1a), that consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, given by a clear affirmative act, provable, and as easy to withdraw as it was to give. Pre-ticked boxes don't count; the EU Court of Justice said so in _Planet49_ (2019).
- **Opt-out (most US state laws).** Uses may proceed until the person says no. The CCPA gives Californians the right to opt out of the "sale" or "sharing" of their personal information — sharing meaning cross-context behavioral advertising — and a dozen states now require businesses to honor **opt-out preference signals** like Global Privacy Control, a setting the browser sends with every request. Several US laws still require opt-in for sensitive data such as health or precise location.

Enforcement is real on both sides. In September 2025, France's regulator fined Google €325 million and Shein €150 million, in large part for setting advertising cookies without valid consent. California's enforcement actions have targeted ignored GPC signals, opt-out flows harder than opt-in, and a misconfigured banner product — the vendor's configuration was still the company's liability.

Three disambiguations, because each one causes real bugs:

- **Consent isn't the only lawful basis, and often the wrong one.** Processing needed to run the account is based on contract. Asking consent for it implies the person can withdraw and still get the service, which isn't true. Ask for consent only where refusal is a real option.
- **Accepting terms of service is not consent.** A "by signing up you agree to our Terms" click is contract acceptance, recorded differently and for different reasons (34.2b). Bundling data-use consent into it makes the consent invalid.
- **Platform permissions aren't legal consent.** Apple's App Tracking Transparency prompt is an iOS permission. It doesn't satisfy the EU's consent requirement, and the EU requirement doesn't satisfy it. Where both apply, you need both.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Third-party requests or cookies appear in the browser before the banner is answered.
- "Reject all" takes more clicks than "Accept all," or doesn't exist.
- Consent is stored only in a cookie, so it's lost on a new device and invisible to the backend.
- Server-side integrations — conversion APIs, CRM sync, audience exports, reverse ETL — run without checking any consent state.
- The database says `marketing_opt_in: true` with no timestamp, source or wording attached.
- Nobody reads `Sec-GPC` headers or `navigator.globalPrivacyControl`.
- Withdrawing consent takes days to reach the email vendor or ad platforms.
- Marketing can add tags through the tag manager without anyone else knowing.
- Mobile SDKs initialize in `Application.onCreate` or `didFinishLaunching`, before any choice exists.
- Nobody can say which vendor or tag serves which purpose.

## How It Works

### Purposes, not cookies

A banner that lists forty cookies by name is unreadable, and it's also the wrong model. What a person consents to is a **purpose**: analytics, personalization, advertising, marketing email. Cookies, SDKs, pixels, server-side integrations and pipelines are just implementations of purposes.

So the foundation is a purpose catalog, and a mapping from every data-using component to the purposes it serves:

| Purpose | Default (EU) | Default (California) | Implemented by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strictly necessary | on, no choice | on | session, CSRF, load balancer, consent-record cookie |
| Preferences | off | on | language, theme |
| Product analytics | off (some regulators exempt narrowly configured audience measurement) | on | analytics SDK, event pipeline |
| Advertising | off | on, until opted out of sale/sharing | ad SDK, pixels, conversion API, audience exports |
| Marketing email | off (opt-in) | off or on depending on context and anti-spam law | email vendor sync |

"Strictly necessary" is narrow: needed for a service the user explicitly asked for. Security, load balancing, keeping someone logged in, a shopping cart, remembering their consent choice. Analytics is usually not, however useful it is.

### The consent record

Consent must be demonstrable. "subscribed: true" can't demonstrate anything. What can is an append-only log of decisions, each with enough context to show what the person agreed to:

- **Who** — a device identifier for anonymous visitors, an account ID once known.
- **What** — the purpose, and the decision (granted, denied, withdrawn).
- **How** — the source: banner, settings page, signup checkbox, a GPC signal, a support request.
- **Under what notice** — a version identifier or hash of the exact text and vendor list shown.
- **When and where** — timestamp, the jurisdiction rules applied.

The current state is a fold over the log, the same idea as event sourcing (2.4) at a small scale. The log answers the regulator's question months later: this person, on this date, clicked "Accept" on version 14 of the banner, which listed these vendors. It also answers the engineer's question: why did we send this user's signup to the ad platform?

When the purposes or vendor list change materially, the old consent doesn't cover the new thing; the notice version changes and people are asked again. Regulators also expect choices, including refusals, to be re-asked eventually rather than stored forever and not so often that asking becomes pressure; a validity of around six months to a year is common guidance.

The consent log is itself personal data. It goes in the data map (34.1a), with its own retention: long enough to prove consent was valid while you relied on it.

### Resolving what's allowed, right now

Several inputs combine into one answer to "may we use this person's data for this purpose, now?":

```
  jurisdiction rules ──┐
  device choice ───────┤
  account choice ──────┼──►  resolve(subject, purpose)  ──►  allowed? + why
  opt-out signal (GPC) ┤
  age / sensitive data ┘
```

The usual precedence:

1. **Strictly necessary** is always allowed; nothing else is decided here.
2. **The most restrictive applicable choice wins.** If the device said yes and the account said no, it's no.
3. **An opt-out signal is a denial** for the purposes it covers (sale, sharing, targeted advertising) wherever the law recognizes it, and it overrides an earlier yes from the same browser. California also requires you to show that the signal was honored.
4. **Absent any choice, the jurisdiction's default applies**: denied where opt-in is required, allowed where the regime is opt-out.
5. **Once someone logs in, account-level choices follow them** across devices; anonymous choices from the current device are merged into the account on login — denials always, grants only if the notice version is current.

Resolution returns _why_ along with the answer. "Denied: GPC signal, 2026-09-14" is what support needs, what an audit needs, and what the California "opt-out honored" message needs.

### Enforcement at every point of use

This is where the Problem's systems failed. The banner is where choices are **collected**. They have to be **enforced** everywhere data is used:

```
                              consent service
                           (log + resolve + API)
             ┌───────────────┬───────┴───────┬──────────────────┐
             ▼               ▼               ▼                  ▼
      browser tag       mobile SDK      server pipelines     vendors
      loader            init            (conversion API,     (propagate
      (nothing loads    (SDK stays      CRM & email sync,    withdrawal:
      until resolved)   off until       audience export,     unsubscribe,
                        resolved)       warehouse jobs)      delete audience)
```

- **In the browser**, non-essential tags must not load until a choice exists, and then only those whose purposes are allowed. In practice the tag manager itself waits for the consent state, or each tag declares its purposes and a loader gates it. Loading "the consent script" first is not enough if other scripts are on the page too.
- **In mobile apps**, the same rule applies to SDK initialization. Most ad and analytics SDKs have a "don't start until told" mode, and it has to be used.
- **On the server**, every pipeline that sends data somewhere for a consent-based purpose checks the resolved state **at the moment of sending**, not at the moment of collection. Consent can be withdrawn between the two, and a queue of events collected under yesterday's consent may not be sent today.
- **At vendors**, withdrawal has to reach the places the data already went: unsubscribe in the email platform, removal from ad audiences, deletion requests where the vendor supports them. This is propagation, the same idea as Article 19 in 34.1a.

Ad-tech has standards for passing consent downstream — the IAB's Transparency and Consent Framework encodes choices in a string passed along the ad supply chain, and Google's Consent Mode passes consent state to Google tags. They're useful plumbing. Neither decides for you whether your own server-side integrations are allowed to run.

### What a valid banner looks like

Regulators' guidance converges on a short list:

- **Refusing is as easy as accepting**, on the first layer. If "Accept all" is one click, "Reject all" is one click.
- **No pre-ticked boxes, no consent by scrolling or continuing to browse.**
- **Granular by purpose** in the second layer, not forced as all-or-nothing, and not forty toggles either.
- **Honest design**: no visually muted reject button, no guilt-trip wording, no repeated nagging after a refusal. Regulators call these deceptive design patterns and enforce against them.
- **Withdrawal available at any time**, from somewhere permanent like a footer link or settings page.
- **The service works after refusal**, except where refusal is genuinely incompatible with what was asked for. Cookie walls and "pay or consent" models are contested territory; take legal advice before building one.

### Mobile and the backend have no banner

The banner is a web idea, but the obligations aren't. Mobile apps need a first-run consent screen that gates SDK initialization, and a settings screen for changing it. Backend processes need no UI at all — they need the consent API. The Problem's server-side conversion upload was the clearest failure, because the team's mental model of consent was "the thing the banner does."

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any site or app with non-essential trackers and EU or UK visitors** needs a working opt-in consent flow. That covers most sites with advertising, retargeting or third-party analytics.
- **Selling or sharing data, or targeted advertising, with US users in the relevant states** requires an opt-out mechanism and honoring GPC — twelve states as of 2026, and every major browser will offer a signal to Californians from 2027.
- **Marketing email or SMS** needs consent records wherever anti-spam law requires opt-in, and suppression handling everywhere.
- **Server-side marketing integrations** — conversion APIs, audience uploads, CRM enrichment — are exactly where the account-level consent service and send-time checks earn their cost.
- **Multiple platforms** (web plus mobile) make account-level consent worthwhile, because device-only choices diverge immediately.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A banner when you have nothing non-essential.** If a site uses only strictly necessary cookies, it doesn't need a consent banner. GitHub removed all non-essential cookies in 2020 and removed its banner with them. Often the best consent project is deleting trackers.
- **Asking consent for processing that doesn't need it.** Consent to "process your account data" misrepresents the legal basis and creates a withdrawal right you can't honor.
- **Building your own banner and consent platform** when a vendor would do. Build the parts vendors don't reach — server-side enforcement and account-level state — and buy the banner.
- **Per-cookie toggles.** Consent is per purpose. Forty switches don't make choices more informed.
- **An account-level consent service for a marketing site with one analytics tag.** A well-configured banner vendor and a tag loader that waits for it are enough.

## Trade-offs

- **You lose data, deliberately.** With a real "Reject all" button, a substantial share of visitors refuse. Advertising attribution, remarketing audiences and analytics coverage all shrink.
- **What remains is biased.** People who accept tracking differ from people who don't, so analytics on consenting users is a skewed sample (16.1). Decisions based on it need care.
- **Every data-using pipeline gains a dependency.** The consent service is now on the path of every marketing integration; if it's down, those pipelines must fail closed, which means dropped or delayed data.
- **Marketing agility decreases.** Adding a tag becomes a governed change instead of a tag-manager click. That's the point, and it's friction every time.
- **The consent log is sensitive and long-lived.** It reveals browsing and choices, and it must be kept for as long as consent is relied on.
- **The banner costs UX and performance** on the first visit, and adds a script to every page.
- **Rules keep moving.** The EU is negotiating changes to cookie consent — including one-click refusal and browser-level signals — and US states keep adding signal requirements. A purpose-and-signal architecture absorbs these; a hard-coded banner doesn't.
- **Vendors' behavior is your liability.** A banner product misconfigured for a month, or an SDK that phones home before being told to, is your violation.

## Alternatives

**Don't track.** Remove non-essential trackers, move to contextual advertising instead of behavioral, and consent largely disappears as a problem. Underrated.

**Privacy-preserving analytics.** Aggregate first-party measurement without persistent identifiers or cross-site tracking. In some jurisdictions a narrowly configured version is exempt from consent; in others it isn't. It reduces the problem but doesn't always remove it.

**A consent management platform** (OneTrust, Usercentrics, Didomi, Osano, Ketch and others): banners, per-jurisdiction rules, consent logs, scanning for trackers, GPC support, and ad-tech framework integration. The right default for the web layer. They don't reach your backend pipelines unless you integrate them there.

**Legitimate interests instead of consent** for some processing — under the GDPR, a real option for things like fraud prevention or some product analytics. But it doesn't remove the ePrivacy requirement for device access, so it doesn't make tracking cookies consent-free.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Inventory and remove.** Crawl the site and list every third-party request and cookie; map each to a purpose and an owner; delete what nobody can justify. Install a consent platform with one-click reject and make the tag manager wait for it. *Signal to move on:* anything non-essential still loads before a choice, or you have server-side marketing integrations.

**L1 — Records and signals.** A purpose catalog, consent events with source and notice version, a permanent withdrawal link, GPC honored on the web, and newsletter consent recorded with its wording. *Signal:* users on more than one platform, or backend pipelines that send personal data to vendors.

**L2 — Consent as a service.** Account-level consent with the resolution rules above, an API every pipeline calls at send time, mobile SDKs gated on it, withdrawal propagated to vendors within hours, and a consent regression crawl in CI. *Signal:* regulators, auditors or large customers asking you to demonstrate consent for specific users.

**L3 — Coverage and proof.** Per-jurisdiction policy configuration, ad-tech framework integration where you depend on programmatic advertising, age-appropriate handling, periodic audits comparing observed network traffic with declared purposes.

## Implementation

TypeScript. Three pieces: resolving consent, enforcing it on the server, and testing that the browser respects it.

### Consent events and resolution

```ts
type Purpose = "necessary" | "preferences" | "analytics" | "advertising" | "marketing_email";
type Decision = "granted" | "denied";

interface ConsentEvent {
  subject: { deviceId?: string; accountId?: string };
  purpose: Purpose;
  decision: Decision;
  source: "banner" | "settings" | "signup_form" | "gpc_signal" | "support";
  noticeVersion: string;            // hash of the exact text + vendor list shown
  at: Date;
}

interface Resolution { allowed: boolean; reason: string }

const SIGNAL_COVERS: Purpose[] = ["advertising"];     // sale/sharing/targeted ads

function resolve(purpose: Purpose, ctx: {
  jurisdiction: Jurisdiction;
  events: ConsentEvent[];           // account's events + this device's, newest last
  gpc: boolean;                     // Sec-GPC: 1 header, or navigator.globalPrivacyControl
  currentNotice: string;
}): Resolution {
  if (purpose === "necessary") return { allowed: true, reason: "strictly necessary" };

  if (ctx.gpc && SIGNAL_COVERS.includes(purpose) && ctx.jurisdiction.honorsOptOutSignals) {
    return { allowed: false, reason: "opt-out preference signal" };   // overrides any earlier grant
  }

  const relevant = ctx.events.filter(e => e.purpose === purpose);
  if (relevant.some(e => e.decision === "denied" && isLatestFromItsSource(e, relevant))) {
    return { allowed: false, reason: "denied by user" };               // most restrictive wins
  }
  const grant = relevant.findLast(e => e.decision === "granted");
  if (grant && grant.noticeVersion === ctx.currentNotice && !expired(grant, ctx.jurisdiction)) {
    return { allowed: true, reason: `granted via ${grant.source} on ${grant.at.toISOString()}` };
  }
  return ctx.jurisdiction.optInRequired(purpose)
    ? { allowed: false, reason: "no valid consent; opt-in required" }
    : { allowed: true,  reason: "opt-out regime; no objection recorded" };
}
```

> **A stale grant is not a grant.** A "yes" given under notice version 13 doesn't cover the vendors added in version 14. That's why the notice version is stored with every event, and why the resolver compares it before allowing anything. Denials don't need a version check: a refusal to an older, smaller vendor list still means no.

### Enforcing at send time, on the server

```ts
// The conversion uploader runs from a queue. Events were enqueued at signup time,
// possibly hours ago; consent is checked now, when the data actually leaves.
async function uploadConversions(batch: SignupEvent[]) {
  const allowed: SignupEvent[] = [];
  for (const e of batch) {
    const r = await consent.resolve(e.accountId, "advertising");
    if (r.allowed) allowed.push(e);
    else metrics.increment("conversions.dropped", { reason: r.reason });
  }
  if (allowed.length) await adPlatform.sendConversions(allowed.map(toHashedPayload));
}
```

> **Fail closed.** If `consent.resolve` throws — the service is down, the account can't be found — the event is not sent. It's retried later or dropped. The opposite default, "send it and hope," is how an outage becomes a violation for every user in the batch.

> **Withdrawal also reaches backwards.** Send-time checks stop future uploads; they do nothing about the audience lists already built from past uploads. A withdrawal event should also enqueue removal from those lists and an unsubscribe at the email vendor, with a target measured in hours, not days.

### Testing that the browser actually waits

```ts
// Consent regression test (Playwright), run in CI against staging, per jurisdiction.
test("nothing non-essential loads before a choice (EU)", async ({ browser }) => {
  const ctx = await browser.newContext({ locale: "de-DE", geolocationOverride: EU });
  const page = await ctx.newPage();
  const thirdParty: string[] = [];
  page.on("request", r => { if (!isFirstParty(r.url())) thirdParty.push(r.url()); });

  for (const path of ["/", "/pricing", "/app/login", "/blog/some-post"]) {
    await page.goto(path);
    await page.waitForLoadState("networkidle");
  }

  expect(thirdParty.filter(u => !ESSENTIAL_THIRD_PARTIES.has(host(u)))).toEqual([]);
  const cookies = await ctx.cookies();
  expect(cookies.map(c => c.name).filter(n => !NECESSARY_COOKIES.has(n))).toEqual([]);
});

test("GPC suppresses advertising (California)", async ({ browser }) => {
  const ctx = await browser.newContext({ extraHTTPHeaders: { "Sec-GPC": "1" } /* ... */ });
  // ...accept all in the banner, then assert no advertising hosts are contacted
  // and the "opt-out request honored" indicator is shown.
});
```

> **Why a crawl rather than a code review.** The failures in the Problem all came from things that no single code change introduced: a tag added in the tag manager, an SDK default, a banner vendor's configuration. Only observing what the browser actually does catches them. The same test run against production on a schedule catches the tags marketing adds after CI has passed.

## Further Reading

- **ePrivacy Directive, Article 5(3)** — the device-access consent rule behind cookie banners, one sentence long.
- **GDPR Articles 4(11), 7 and 8, and Recitals 32, 42 and 43** — the definition and conditions of consent, including withdrawal and children.
- **EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 on consent** — what "freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous" means in practice, with examples.
- **EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 on the technical scope of Article 5(3)** — why pixels, URL tracking, local storage and SDK identifiers are covered, not just cookies.
- **EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce report (2023)** — regulators' shared positions on reject buttons, pre-ticked boxes, deceptive design and "legitimate interest" toggles.
- **CJEU, _Planet49_ (C-673/17, 2019) and _IAB Europe_ (C-604/22, 2024)** — pre-ticked boxes are not consent; ad-tech consent strings can be personal data.
- **CNIL, cookie guidelines and recommendations** — the most detailed national guidance, including the conditions for exempt audience measurement.
- **Global Privacy Control specification** — the signal, the header and the JavaScript property.
- **CCPA regulations, §§7025–7026, and California's Opt Me Out Act (AB 566)** — opt-out signal handling and the browser requirement from 2027.
- **EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on deceptive design patterns** — a catalog of the interface tricks regulators treat as invalidating consent.
- **In this collection:** 34.1a (lawful bases and the data map), 34.1b (where the consent log lives), 34.2b (terms acceptance, which is not consent), 16.1 and 16.4 (analytics design and its sampling), 14.5 (notification preferences, the product-side sibling of consent), 42.1 (mobile SDK initialization), 2.4 (event sourcing, the same fold at a larger scale).

No sibling article is proposed: the architecture is language-neutral, and the most interesting second implementation — mobile SDK gating in Swift or Kotlin — is outside this collection's three languages.

---

The habit to take from this: **a consent choice is only as real as the last system that ignores it.** When you see a banner, ask where the choice goes next, and which pipeline, SDK or vendor never asks.
