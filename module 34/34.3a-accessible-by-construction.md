# Accessible by Construction: WCAG, Semantic HTML, Keyboard and Focus

_Module 34.3a — Legal & Compliance Considerations_

> This article covers the foundation: what accessibility requires, why semantic HTML carries most of it, and how keyboard and focus work. 34.3b builds on it with screen readers, ARIA, custom widgets and testing. Legal deadlines below are accurate as of 2026; which laws apply to your product is a question for counsel.

## The Problem

Suppose you build a task-management tool — boards, cards, assignments, comments — sold mostly to businesses. A state university wants to roll it out to 30,000 staff. Their procurement team asks for an **Accessibility Conformance Report** against WCAG 2.1 AA, and their accessibility office runs a test session with two staff members: one is blind and uses a screen reader, the other has a repetitive strain injury and uses only the keyboard.

The session lasts twenty minutes. Here's what the keyboard user finds:

- **Tab skips the "New task" button.** It's a `<div onClick>`, so the keyboard can't reach it.
- **Focus is invisible.** A CSS reset set `outline: none` years ago. The user presses Tab and can't tell where they are.
- **The "Edit task" modal opens, and focus stays behind it.** Tab moves through the board underneath the dimmed overlay. Escape does nothing.
- **Cards can only be moved by dragging.** There is no other way to change a card's column.
- **Deleting a comment sends focus to the top of the page,** forty tab stops away from where the user was.

And the screen-reader user:

- **Form fields have no labels.** The placeholder "Task name" disappears when typing starts, and the screen reader announces "edit text, blank."
- **Errors are red borders.** Nothing says what's wrong, in text, anywhere.
- **Navigating to another board changes nothing that's announced.** The single-page app swaps content silently; the user doesn't know the page changed.

The accessibility office also notes that grey placeholder-style text fails contrast, the app breaks at 200% zoom, and login requires solving an image puzzle.

None of this was a decision. Nobody chose to exclude blind or keyboard-only users. Every failure came from a small default — a `div` instead of a `button`, a CSS reset, a modal library, a drag-and-drop package — made thousands of times across the codebase. The deal stalls, and the remediation estimate is two quarters.

## What It Is

**Accessibility** means people with disabilities can perceive, operate and understand your software as well as anyone else. That includes blind and low-vision people, deaf and hard-of-hearing people, people with motor impairments, and people with cognitive, learning and vestibular disabilities — and, in practice, anyone with a broken wrist, a glaring screen or a slow connection.

The shared standard is the **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)** from the W3C. It's organized around four principles — content must be **Perceivable, Operable, Understandable and Robust** — broken into testable success criteria at three levels: A (minimum), AA (the standard almost every law and contract cites) and AAA (enhanced; not recommended as a blanket target, since some criteria can't be met for all content). WCAG 2.2 (2023) is current; most laws still reference 2.1, and 2.2 adds to it rather than changing it. WCAG 3 is a draft and not yet a target.

Why this is in a compliance module:

- **US private businesses** face lawsuits under ADA Title III, which courts have applied to websites and apps. There's no specific technical regulation, but claims are measured against WCAG 2.1 AA, and thousands are filed every year.
- **US state and local governments**, including public universities, must meet WCAG 2.1 AA under a 2024 Justice Department rule. The deadlines were pushed back by a year in April 2026: April 2027 for larger entities, April 2028 for smaller ones. The rule covers content "provided or made available" through vendors, so it flows straight into their software purchases — like the Problem's.
- **US federal agencies** buy under Section 508, and healthcare organizations funded by the Department of Health and Human Services have their own deadlines.
- **The EU's European Accessibility Act** has applied since June 2025 to consumer-facing products and services — e-commerce, banking, e-books, ticketing, communications — with EN 301 549 (which incorporates WCAG 2.1 AA) as the technical reference. EU public bodies were already covered, and they buy under the same standard.

For B2B software, the usual mechanism is procurement: customers with legal obligations ask for an Accessibility Conformance Report (often written using the VPAT template) and write conformance into contracts.

One disambiguation: **accessible by construction** vs **remediation**. Remediation is finding and fixing failures in an existing product. Construction is building so they don't arise: native elements, accessible components, and checks that run before shipping. Both are needed, but only one gets cheaper over time.

## Recognizing the Problem

- `<div onClick>` or `<span onClick>` anywhere in the codebase.
- `outline: none` or `outline: 0` in a global stylesheet without a replacement focus style.
- You can't complete signup, login or your core workflow using only Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space, Escape and arrow keys.
- Modals don't move focus inside, don't trap it, or don't close on Escape.
- Some action is only possible by drag, hover or a precise gesture.
- Inputs use placeholders as labels.
- Errors are indicated by color alone.
- Text is light grey on white, and nobody has checked a contrast ratio.
- The layout breaks or needs horizontal scrolling at 200–400% zoom.
- Page changes in the single-page app don't move focus or change the document title.
- Login needs a puzzle, a CAPTCHA with no alternative, or blocks pasting passwords.
- Customers or prospects ask for a VPAT or ACR and nobody knows what that is.

## How It Works

### How people actually use software

Before rules, the mechanics:

- **Keyboard only.** Tab and Shift+Tab move between focusable elements; Enter and Space activate; arrow keys move within composite widgets like menus and tabs; Escape dismisses. Used by people with motor impairments, by many screen-reader users, and by power users.
- **Screen readers** (NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver, TalkBack) don't read the screen's pixels. They read the **accessibility tree**, a structure the browser derives from the DOM, in which each element has a role ("button"), a name ("New task"), and states ("expanded," "disabled"). What isn't in the tree doesn't exist for the user. 34.3b goes deep on this.
- **Magnification and zoom**, from 200% browser zoom to 10x screen magnifiers showing a tiny slice of the page.
- **Voice control** — saying "click New task." It works only if the visible label matches the element's accessible name.
- **Switch access**, captions, reduced-motion settings, high-contrast modes.

The consistent theme: assistive technology relies on the **semantics** your markup provides. Most of accessibility is getting those semantics right, which mostly means using the right elements.

### Semantic HTML carries most of the load

A native `<button>` gives you, without a line of extra code:

| Behavior | `<button>` | `<div onClick>` |
|---|---|---|
| Reachable by Tab | yes | no |
| Activated by Enter and Space | yes | no |
| Announced as "button" with its text as name | yes | no ("New task", maybe, with no role) |
| Disabled state understood | yes | no |
| Works with voice control ("click New task") | yes | unreliable |
| Submits its form | yes | no |

Rebuilding that on a `div` takes a role, a tabindex, two key handlers and state attributes — and is still subtly worse. The first rule of ARIA, the attribute system for adding semantics (34.3b), is to not use it when a native element does the job.

The same principle across the page:

- **Links navigate; buttons act.** A "button" that changes the URL should be a link (so it can be opened in a new tab and is announced correctly); a "link" that opens a modal should be a button.
- **Headings form an outline.** One `h1` per view, nested levels without skipping. Screen-reader users jump between headings the way sighted users scan.
- **Landmarks** — `header`, `nav`, `main`, `aside`, `footer` — let people jump to regions.
- **Lists are lists, tables are for tabular data** with header cells, and **forms have labels** associated with their inputs.
- **`<html lang="…">`** so screen readers pronounce text in the right language (25.x, where content switches language mid-page, set `lang` on that element).

### Keyboard operability

Every function must work from the keyboard, without requiring specific timing:

- **Reachable.** Anything interactive can receive focus. Native interactive elements already do.
- **Logical order.** Tab order follows the DOM. If the visual order and DOM order differ — a CSS grid rearranging things, a sidebar positioned first visually but last in source — keyboard users experience a scramble. Fix the DOM order; never use positive `tabindex` values to patch it.
- **No traps.** Focus can always leave a component the same way it arrived — except modal dialogs, which deliberately contain focus while open.
- **Expected keys inside widgets.** Menus, tabs, listboxes and grids use arrow keys between items and one Tab stop for the whole widget. These patterns are standardized; 34.3b covers them.
- **A skip link** at the top ("Skip to main content") so keyboard users don't tab through the whole navigation on every page.
- **Single-key shortcuts** (like "c" to create a card) must be switchable off or remappable, because voice-control users trigger them by accident when dictating.

### Focus management: focus goes where the user's attention goes

The browser manages focus well for static pages. Applications change the page under the user, and every change raises the question: where is focus now?

- **Visible, always.** Replace the default outline if you must, never remove it. `:focus-visible` shows focus rings for keyboard users without showing them on mouse clicks. The indicator needs sufficient contrast (3:1 against adjacent colors), and WCAG 2.2 adds that a focused element mustn't be entirely hidden behind sticky headers or cookie banners.
- **Dialogs.** On open, move focus into the dialog; contain it there; close on Escape; on close, return focus to the element that opened it. The native `<dialog>` element opened with `showModal()` does most of this.
- **Route changes in single-page apps.** A traditional page load resets focus to the top and announces the new title. A client-side route change does neither. After navigation, update `document.title` and move focus to the new view's heading:

  ```tsx
  useEffect(() => {
    document.title = `${board.name} · Tasks`;
    headingRef.current?.focus();            // <h1 tabIndex={-1} ref={headingRef}>
  }, [board.id]);
  ```

- **Deletions.** When the focused element disappears, focus falls to `<body>` — the top of the page. Move it deliberately: to the next item in the list, the previous one if it was last, or the list's container.
- **Errors after submission.** Move focus to an error summary, or to the first invalid field.

### Visual requirements

- **Contrast.** Text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 with its background (3:1 for large text). Interface components and graphical objects needed to understand the UI — input borders, icons, focus indicators — need 3:1. This is the single most common failure on the web.
- **Not color alone.** An error shown only by a red border, a required field marked only in red, a chart distinguished only by hue: add text, icons or patterns.
- **Zoom and reflow.** Content must work at 200% zoom, and reflow into a single column at a 320-CSS-pixel width (equivalent to 400% zoom on a desktop) without horizontal scrolling — data tables and canvases excepted. Responsive design mostly delivers this, if you've tested it.
- **Text spacing.** Layouts mustn't break when users override line height and letter spacing with their own styles.
- **Motion.** Nothing flashes more than three times a second. Respect `prefers-reduced-motion` for large animations; parallax and zooming transitions cause real nausea for people with vestibular disorders.

### Input, forms and authentication

- **Persistent labels.** A visible `<label>` for every input. Placeholders disappear on typing and are usually too low-contrast anyway.
- **Errors in text,** next to the field, associated with it, saying what's wrong and how to fix it.
- **`autocomplete` attributes** on fields about the user (`email`, `given-name`, `postal-code`) so browsers and assistive tools can fill them.
- **Targets large enough.** WCAG 2.2 asks for at least 24×24 CSS pixels, or enough spacing around smaller ones.
- **An alternative to dragging.** WCAG 2.2 requires that anything done by dragging can also be done with single clicks or taps. For the Problem's board, a "Move to…" menu on each card.
- **Accessible authentication.** WCAG 2.2 doesn't allow login to depend on a cognitive test — remembering, transcribing or solving something — without an alternative. Allowing paste into password fields, supporting password managers, and offering passkeys or email links all satisfy it (4.1). Blocking paste fails it.
- **Time limits adjustable.** Warn before session expiry and let people extend it (4.4).

### Construction: build it into the components

The Problem's failures were repeated defaults, so the fix is in the defaults. Most of the work lands in the design system: a `Button` component that renders a `<button>`, a `Dialog` that manages focus, a `TextField` that requires a label, a `Menu` with correct keyboard behavior, color tokens that meet contrast. Product teams then get accessibility by using the components, and lose it only by going around them — which lint rules can flag.

Automated checks (axe-core, eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y, Lighthouse) catch missing labels, contrast failures, invalid structure and similar mechanical problems. They can't tell whether focus order makes sense, whether a label is meaningful or whether a flow is usable. They're a floor, not a verdict; 34.3b covers testing properly.

## When It's Appropriate

Basic accessibility — semantic HTML, keyboard access, visible focus, labels, contrast — applies to everything, because it's cheap when built in and it's the default of the platform if you don't fight it. The level of investment beyond that scales with exposure:

- **Selling to government, public universities, public healthcare or federal agencies**: WCAG 2.1 AA conformance, a credible ACR, and remediation commitments in contracts. The Title II deadlines of 2027 and 2028 are driving procurement now.
- **Consumer-facing products in the EU** covered by the European Accessibility Act: conformance, an accessibility statement, and a feedback mechanism.
- **Consumer products in the US at scale**: litigation risk scales with visibility, and e-commerce and hospitality are heavily targeted.
- **Enterprise B2B**: large customers increasingly include accessibility in procurement for their own employees, regardless of sector.
- **Products whose users skew older** — health, finance, government services — where a large share of users have some disability.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **AAA conformance as a blanket goal.** The W3C itself advises against requiring it for entire sites. Target AA; adopt specific AAA criteria where they fit your users.
- **Custom widgets where native elements exist.** A hand-built select, checkbox or date picker costs many times more to make accessible than the native element, and usually ends up worse.
- **Auditing the whole legacy product before fixing the design system.** An audit of 400 screens produces 4,000 findings; fixing ten shared components resolves a large share of them at once.
- **Treating an automated score of 100 as done.** It's evidence that mechanical checks pass, not that the product is usable.
- **Separate "accessible versions."** A parallel text-only site is a maintenance trap that falls behind within months, and a segregated experience besides.

## Trade-offs

- **Design constraints are real.** Contrast rules exclude some brand colors for text; visible focus rings change the look; persistent labels take space that placeholders didn't. Designers need to own these as constraints from the start, not receive them as bug reports.
- **Custom interaction costs more.** Drag-and-drop, canvases, rich text editors, and complex grids each need alternative interaction paths and careful keyboard models. Some product ideas get more expensive.
- **Testing takes human time.** Automated tools catch a portion of issues; keyboard walkthroughs and screen-reader testing take real time per release.
- **Third-party components set your ceiling.** Payment forms, video players, maps, chat widgets and embedded analytics are part of your conformance, and you can only pressure their vendors.
- **Retrofitting is far more expensive than construction.** The Problem's two-quarter estimate is typical; building the same components right would have cost days.
- **Conformance claims are liabilities.** An ACR that overstates conformance, or an accessibility statement that promises what the product doesn't do, becomes evidence in a dispute. Be accurate, including about known gaps.

## Alternatives

**Remediation projects after the fact.** Sometimes necessary for legacy products, usually driven by an external audit. Expensive, and it regresses unless the construction practices change too.

**Accessibility overlays** — third-party scripts that claim to make a site compliant automatically. They don't fix the underlying markup, often interfere with users' own assistive technology, and are widely opposed by disabled users and accessibility practitioners. In 2025 the FTC required one major overlay vendor to pay $1 million over claims that its product made websites compliant. Sites using overlays continue to be sued.

**Accessible component libraries** — React Aria, Radix Primitives, Headless UI, and design systems like GOV.UK's — as the foundation for your own components. A strong choice: they've solved focus and keyboard patterns that are expensive to get right, and you style them. You still have to use them correctly.

**Native platform controls** in mobile apps, which carry accessibility semantics for free on iOS and Android in the same way HTML elements do on the web.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Stop adding failures.** Accessibility lint rules and axe checks in CI; restore visible focus globally; set `lang`; add landmarks and a skip link; fix contrast in the color tokens; replace `div`-buttons as you touch them. *Signal to move on:* checks pass but a keyboard walkthrough of a core flow still fails.

**L1 — Make the critical flows work.** Keyboard walkthroughs of signup, login, the core task and billing; focus management for dialogs, route changes and deletions; labels and text errors on every form; a non-drag alternative for every drag interaction. *Signal:* a customer asks for an ACR, or a public-sector deal is on the table.

**L2 — Build it into the design system.** Accessible primitives with documented keyboard behavior, contrast-safe tokens, lint rules that flag bypassing them, and a Definition of Done that includes keyboard and 200% zoom checks. Publish an honest ACR. *Signal:* conformance is contractual, or you're covered by the EAA or Title II via customers.

**L3 — Verify with real assistive technology and people.** Screen-reader testing in regression passes, users with disabilities in research and usability testing, training for designers and engineers (34.3b).

## Implementation

TypeScript with React, where most of these failures are born — and where fixing the shared components fixes the product.

### Buttons and links: use the element that has the behavior

```tsx
// Before: invisible to the keyboard, silent to screen readers.
<div className="btn" onClick={openNewTask}>New task</div>

// After: focusable, Enter/Space, announced as "New task, button". Nothing else needed.
<button type="button" className="btn" onClick={openNewTask}>New task</button>

// Navigation is a link, even when it looks like a button: it opens in a new tab,
// shows its URL, and is announced as a link.
<Link to={`/boards/${board.id}`} className="btn">Open board</Link>

// An icon-only button still needs a name.
<button type="button" onClick={onClose} aria-label="Close">
  <XIcon aria-hidden="true" />
</button>
```

> **Why not add `role="button"` and `tabIndex={0}` to the div?** You'd then need Enter and Space handlers, a disabled state, and focus styling, and it would still not submit forms or work reliably with voice control. It's more code for a worse result, which is why lint rules like `jsx-a11y/no-static-element-interactions` flag it.

### A dialog that manages focus

```tsx
function EditTaskDialog({ open, onClose, task }: Props) {
  const ref = useRef<HTMLDialogElement>(null);
  const returnTo = useRef<HTMLElement | null>(null);

  useEffect(() => {
    const dialog = ref.current!;
    if (open && !dialog.open) {
      returnTo.current = document.activeElement as HTMLElement;
      dialog.showModal();          // top layer: focus moves in, background is inert, Escape closes
    } else if (!open && dialog.open) {
      dialog.close();
    }
  }, [open]);

  function handleClose() {
    onClose();
    // Return focus to the trigger if it still exists; otherwise somewhere sensible.
    const target = returnTo.current?.isConnected ? returnTo.current : document.querySelector("main h1");
    (target as HTMLElement | null)?.focus();
  }

  return (
    <dialog ref={ref} onClose={handleClose} aria-labelledby="edit-task-title">
      <h2 id="edit-task-title">Edit task</h2>
      <form method="dialog">
        <label htmlFor="task-name">Task name</label>
        <input id="task-name" name="name" defaultValue={task.name} autoComplete="off" />
        {/* ... */}
        <button type="submit" value="save">Save</button>
        <button type="submit" value="cancel" formNoValidate>Cancel</button>
      </form>
    </dialog>
  );
}
```

> **What `showModal()` buys you.** Before the native element was widely supported, every modal library had to implement focus containment, Escape handling and making the background inert — and many got it wrong, which is how the Problem's modal let Tab wander behind it. Current browsers also restore focus on close, but the explicit handling above still matters for the case the browser can't solve: the trigger no longer exists, because the dialog deleted the thing it was opened from.

### Moving a card without dragging

```tsx
function Card({ card, columns, onMove }: CardProps) {
  const cardRef = useRef<HTMLLIElement>(null);

  async function moveTo(columnId: string) {
    await onMove(card.id, columnId);
    // The card re-renders in its new column. Keep the user with it,
    // rather than letting focus fall to the top of the page.
    requestAnimationFrame(() =>
      document.getElementById(`card-${card.id}`)?.focus()
    );
  }

  return (
    <li id={`card-${card.id}`} ref={cardRef} tabIndex={-1} draggable onDragStart={/* ... */}>
      <h3>{card.title}</h3>
      <MoveMenu
        label={`Move "${card.title}"`}
        options={columns.filter(c => c.id !== card.columnId)}
        onSelect={moveTo}
      />
    </li>
  );
}
```

> **Dragging stays, as a shortcut.** The drag interaction remains for people who can use it; the menu is the path everyone can use, including keyboard, switch and voice users, and people on touchscreens with limited fine motor control. A screen-reader user also needs to _hear_ that the move happened — "Moved to In progress" — which is a live-region announcement, covered in 34.3b. `MoveMenu` itself is a menu-button widget whose keyboard behavior is standardized; building it correctly is also 34.3b's territory, and a component library is the sensible place to get it.

## Further Reading

- **WCAG 2.2**, with **Understanding WCAG 2.2** and the **How to Meet WCAG quick reference** — the standard itself, and the explanations and techniques for each criterion. The "Understanding" documents are where the reasoning lives.
- **MDN, Accessibility section** — semantic HTML, keyboard navigation and the `<dialog>` element, with browser support tables.
- **WebAIM Million** (annual) — a scan of a million home pages, showing how few pass and how the same few failures dominate.
- **Heydon Pickering, _Inclusive Components_** — accessible versions of common components, reasoned through from first principles.
- **Adam Silver, _Form Design Patterns_** — forms, labels, errors and validation done accessibly.
- **GOV.UK Design System** — a production design system with documented accessibility decisions and research behind each component.
- **European Accessibility Act (Directive (EU) 2019/882) and EN 301 549** — the EU consumer rules and their technical standard.
- **US DOJ Title II web rule (28 CFR Part 35, Subpart H) and the April 2026 compliance-date extension** — the public-sector requirement driving procurement.
- **The VPAT template (ITI)** — the format behind most Accessibility Conformance Reports.
- **In this collection:** 34.3b (screen readers, ARIA, widgets, testing), 4.1 and 4.4 (authentication and session timeouts), 25.x (language attributes, RTL), 21.3 (UI testing), 26.1 (admin tools, which need accessibility too).

No sibling article is proposed; the web platform is the subject, and TypeScript with React is where most readers will meet it. Native mobile accessibility would be a genuinely different article, but in Swift and Kotlin, outside this collection's languages.

The habit to take from this: **put the mouse away for five minutes on every feature you ship.** If you can't reach it, see where you are, or get back out, neither can a meaningful fraction of your users.
