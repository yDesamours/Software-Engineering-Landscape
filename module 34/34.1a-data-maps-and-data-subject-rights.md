# Privacy Regulations as Engineering: Data Maps and Data Subject Rights

_Module 34.1a — Legal & Compliance Considerations_

> This article is about the engineering shape of privacy law, not legal advice. Deadlines and definitions below are accurate as of 2026, but which law applies to you, which exemptions you can use, and how long you keep things are decisions for counsel. Your job is to build a system in which whatever they decide can actually be done.

## The Problem

Suppose you run a job board with an applicant-tracking side. Candidates keep profiles, upload CVs and apply to jobs. Employers get a workspace where recruiters move applicants through stages, rate them and leave comments. About 400,000 candidates, a few thousand employers, a small engineering team.

One Tuesday, support receives this:

> Under Article 15 GDPR, please send me a copy of all personal data you hold about me, the purposes you process it for, who you've shared it with, and how long you'll keep it.

The support agent doesn't recognize it as anything special and files it as a normal ticket. It reaches engineering eight days later. An engineer starts grepping for the candidate's email address:

```
                 candidate@example.com
                          │
   ┌──────────┬───────────┼───────────┬────────────┬─────────────┐
   ▼          ▼           ▼           ▼            ▼             ▼
 users    profiles    applications  CV files    email provider  support desk
 (found)  (found)     (found)       (S3, found) (found, by API) (found)
   │
   ├── recruiter notes & ratings in 3 employer workspaces   (found — and sent. Oops.)
   ├── analytics events, keyed by anonymous_id               (missed)
   ├── data warehouse copy of everything above               (missed)
   ├── match-score features for the recommendation model     (missed)
   ├── CRM contact from a marketing webinar                  (missed)
   └── request logs with the email in a query string         (missed)
```

Nine working days later, the candidate receives a zip of CSV dumps. It includes a recruiter's comment — _"strong, but will want remote, probably leaves in a year"_ — pulled straight from an employer's workspace. The employer finds out when the candidate forwards it to them.

Three things went wrong, and only one of them is speed:

- **It was slow.** The legal clock started when support received the email, not when engineering picked it up.
- **It was incomplete.** Half the places this person's data lives weren't found, because nobody had a list of places.
- **It was wrong in a way that's worse than incomplete.** The recruiter notes are the employer's hiring records. The job board processes them on the employer's behalf; it wasn't the job board's call to disclose them.

Then the volume changes. A data-removal service — a company people pay to send privacy requests on their behalf — sends forty requests in one week. One of them arrives from an address that isn't on any account, claiming to be a candidate who "lost access to her old email." Support is about to reply with her export.

That last one is the moment the team realises a privacy request is also a way to steal someone's data.

## What It Is

Modern privacy laws — the EU's **GDPR** and the UK's near-identical version, California's **CCPA** (as amended by the CPRA), roughly twenty other US state laws, Brazil's LGPD, and a growing list elsewhere — differ in detail and rhyme in structure. For an engineer, they reduce to three kinds of obligation:

1. **Know what you hold, and why.** What personal data, in which systems, for what purpose, on what legal justification, kept for how long, shared with whom.
2. **Honor people's rights over it, on a deadline.** Access, correction, deletion, portability, objection and opt-outs, each a request that must be answered within weeks.
3. **Keep your promises about how it's used.** Collect only what the purpose needs, don't repurpose it silently, protect it, and delete it when the reason expires.

The artifact behind the first is a **data map** (GDPR calls its legal form a _record of processing activities_). The machinery behind the second is a **data subject request** (DSR, or DSAR for the access flavor) pipeline. This article is about both. Deletion's hard mechanics — backups, audit logs, crypto-shredding — were 24.4. Where data physically lives is 34.1b. Consent is 34.2a.

A few terms carry most of the weight:

- **Personal data** (CCPA: _personal information_) is any information relating to an identifiable person. It is much wider than "the users table" — more on that below.
- **Controller** (CCPA: _business_) decides why and how data is processed. **Processor** (CCPA: _service provider_) processes it on a controller's instructions. The job board is a controller for candidate profiles and a processor for employers' recruiter notes. That distinction is what the Problem got wrong.
- **Lawful basis** is the GDPR's required justification for each processing purpose. There are six, and which one you rely on changes which rights apply.

One disambiguation: **privacy is not security.** Security keeps unauthorized people out. Privacy governs what _you_ do with data you hold legitimately. A perfectly secured system can still violate privacy law by keeping data forever, using it for something users weren't told about, or being unable to find it when someone asks. A SOC 2 report says nothing about whether you can answer an access request.

Two applicability notes. The GDPR applies based on whose data you process, not where you are incorporated: offering services to people in the EU, or monitoring their behavior, is enough. The CCPA applies to for-profit businesses doing business in California that cross one of its thresholds (revenue above an inflation-adjusted figure of roughly $26.6 million, or buying, selling or sharing the data of 100,000 or more consumers or households, or earning half of revenue from selling or sharing it). And the EU's proposed _Digital Omnibus_ would amend parts of the GDPR, including the definition of personal data and limits on access requests, but as of mid-2026 that part is still being negotiated and member states have pushed back on the definitional change. Build against the law in force.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Nobody can list, from memory or a document, every system that holds a given user's data.
- A privacy request would start with "grep for their email."
- Support doesn't have a macro, label or escalation path for "send me everything you have on me."
- Personal data is keyed by email in some stores, by user ID in others, and by an anonymous cookie ID in analytics — and nothing links them.
- You couldn't say, for a given dataset, whether you're the controller or processing it for a customer.
- Nobody knows the legal basis for any given use of data, or it's "consent" for everything by default.
- "Download my data" in account settings exports the profile table and nothing else.
- Data was copied to a warehouse, a vendor or a model's feature store, and those copies aren't in any list.
- Enterprise customers' security questionnaires ask how you assist with data subject requests, and the answer is "email us."
- Someone could obtain another user's data by writing to support with a plausible story.

## How It Works

### Personal data is wider than the users table

"Relating to an identifiable person" catches far more than names and emails:

- **Identifiers that don't look like identities:** IP addresses, device IDs, cookie IDs, advertising IDs, an internal `candidate_id`.
- **Free text** that mentions someone: support tickets, chat messages, comments.
- **Opinions and assessments about a person.** The EU Court of Justice held in _Nowak_ (2017) that an examiner's comments on an exam script are the candidate's personal data. Recruiter notes and ratings are the same kind of thing.
- **Inferences and derived data:** a match score, a churn prediction, a "likely to relocate" flag.
- **Pseudonymized data**, where names are replaced by an ID but a mapping exists somewhere, is still personal data under current law. Genuinely anonymous data is not, but real anonymization is much harder than dropping a column (24.4).

The practical consequence: the data map can't be built by searching for columns named `email`. It's built by asking, store by store, "could this row be tied back to a person, by us, with means we actually have?"

### Controller or processor is decided per dataset, not per company

Most SaaS companies are both, for different data. For the job board:

| Dataset | Role | Who answers a request about it |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate accounts, profiles, CVs | Controller | You |
| "My applications" list shown to the candidate | Controller | You |
| An employer's pipeline stages, ratings, recruiter notes | Processor for that employer | The employer; you assist |
| Recruiter user accounts, logins | Controller | You |
| Billing contacts for employers | Controller | You |
| Product analytics, marketing CRM | Controller | You |

As a controller, you answer requests. As a processor, you generally don't respond to the person directly about the controller's data. You tell them promptly who the controller is, forward the request if your contract says to, and give the controller the tools to answer it themselves — the GDPR requires processors to assist controllers with these requests (Article 28(3)(e)), and enterprise customers will ask how. The CCPA's service-provider rules have the same shape.

Where a dataset is genuinely ambiguous — the application record sits between the candidate's account and the employer's pipeline — decide with counsel and write the decision into the map. An ambiguity that's written down is a policy. One that isn't gets decided by whichever engineer handles the next request.

### Lawful basis decides which rights apply

The GDPR recognizes six bases for processing: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task and legitimate interests. They aren't paperwork; they change what a request obliges you to do:

| Basis (example at the job board) | Erasure | Portability | Objection |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contract** — running the candidate's account | Yes, once the contract ends (subject to other retention) | Yes | No |
| **Consent** — job-alert emails | Yes, on withdrawal | Yes | Withdraw instead |
| **Legitimate interests** — fraud detection, product analytics | If the objection wins or the purpose ended | No | Yes; weigh it |
| **Legal obligation** — invoices for tax | No, until the obligation ends | No | No |

Objection to direct marketing is absolute: no weighing, you stop. Consent is not the default basis, and treating it as one creates obligations — withdrawal at any time, proof of consent — that other bases don't carry (34.2a).

The engineering takeaway is that the basis must be recorded **per purpose**, next to the data, because the request pipeline needs it to decide what to do.

### The data map

A data map is a list of every store holding personal data, with enough detail to answer any question a regulator or a data subject might ask. The GDPR's record-of-processing requirement (Article 30) has a nominal exemption for organizations under 250 employees, but it doesn't apply when processing is anything more than occasional — which, for a SaaS product, it always is.

For each store, record:

- **What:** categories of data and whose (candidates, recruiters, website visitors).
- **Why:** each purpose and its lawful basis.
- **How long:** a pointer to the retention matrix from 24.4.
- **Role:** controller or processor, and for whom.
- **Who else gets it:** subprocessors and recipients (email provider, warehouse, support desk vendor).
- **Where:** region (34.1b cares).
- **How to find someone in it:** the subject key — the field engineers usually forget to add, and the one that makes requests executable.
- **How to act on it:** an export handler, an erase handler, or a runbook link if it's manual.

The legal record and the engineering map can be the same document or two linked ones. What matters is that the engineering version includes the last two fields, and that it lives somewhere it will be kept true (see below).

### Subject resolution: finding one person across systems

A request names a person; systems store keys. Before any export, the pipeline has to turn "candidate@example.com" into every identifier that person has:

```
 email (as given) ──► user_id 81234 ──┬─► candidate_id c_5521
                                      ├─► anonymous_ids a_x1, a_q9  (linked at login)
                                      ├─► email_provider recipient  (by email)
                                      ├─► support requester 44102   (by email)
                                      └─► crm contact 9f3e          (by email, older address!)
```

This is where 24.4's advice — key everything on internal IDs, not email — pays off twice. Emails change, so a lookup by the current email misses the CRM record created under the old one; the pipeline should expand across **all** emails the account has ever had.

Some data can't be linked at all: page views from before the person ever logged in, under a cookie that was never tied to an account. The GDPR doesn't require you to collect extra information just to identify someone for a request (Article 11). But if the person supplies the identifier themselves — "here's my cookie ID" — you must include what it unlocks. Pre-login data is legitimately out of scope until someone hands you the key.

### The request pipeline

```
 intake ─► classify ─► verify ─► resolve ─► fan out ─► assemble ─► review ─► deliver ─► record
   │          │           │                    │           │          │
   │          │           │                    │           │          └ human check of free text
   │          │           │                    │           └ data + "about the processing" info
   │          │           │                    └ one handler per store; processor stores → forward
   │          │           └ proportionate to what would be disclosed
   │          └ which right, which law, which deadline
   └ clock starts here, whatever the channel
```

**Intake.** A request doesn't need a form, a keyword or the name of the law; "send me everything you have on me" in a support chat counts. Train support to recognize it and route it on day one. Give people an obvious channel — a privacy page, a form, an in-app option — because a clear channel means fewer requests arrive disguised as ordinary tickets.

**Classify and set the clock.** Each regime has its own deadlines:

| Regime | Acknowledge | Respond | Extension |
|---|---|---|---|
| GDPR / UK GDPR | — | 1 month from receipt | +2 months for complex or numerous requests, notice within the first month |
| CCPA: know, delete, correct | 10 business days | 45 calendar days from receipt, **regardless of how long verification takes** | +45 days, with notice within the first 45 |
| CCPA: opt-out of sale/sharing | — | 15 business days | — |
| Other US states | varies | commonly 45 days | commonly +45 |

The simplest robust policy is to apply the strictest applicable deadline to everyone, rather than first working out which law a requester falls under.

**Verify, proportionately.** Verification protects the person from impostors — and impostors are real. At Black Hat in 2019, researcher James Pavur sent access requests on behalf of his fiancée (with her consent) to around 150 companies; about a quarter of those that responded handed over her data on the strength of little more than her name and contact details. But verification can also become an unlawful barrier, and EU regulators say you shouldn't demand ID documents by default.

The practical scale:

- **Logged-in request from the account** — strong, and cheap. Prefer it.
- **Request from the account's verified email** — confirm via a link to that address.
- **Request from an unknown address** — the "lost access" story. Verify against information only the real person would know, or through the account's existing channels, never by replying with data to the new address.
- **Authorized agents** (explicitly allowed under the CCPA) — verify both the agent's authority and the consumer.

Scale strictness to what would be disclosed. Confirming that you'll stop sending marketing emails needs almost nothing; mailing out a full CV and application history needs more.

**Resolve and fan out.** Expand the identity as above, then call every store's handler. Stores where you're a processor don't export; they create a forwarding task to the relevant controller — here, each employer whose workspace holds the candidate. Handlers can fail; a request with a failed handler stays open. Silently shipping 9 out of 11 stores is how the Problem's incomplete export happened.

**Assemble.** An access response under the GDPR has two parts: a copy of the data, and information about the processing — purposes, categories, recipients, retention periods, the data's source if it didn't come from the person, the existence of automated decisions, and the person's other rights. Most of the second part comes straight out of the data map, which is one more reason to keep the map in structured form. The first copy is free. For portability, the data the person provided must be in a structured, machine-readable format (JSON or CSV, not a PDF of screenshots).

**Review and redact.** The right of access mustn't adversely affect others. Before sending, remove other people's personal data (another candidate named in a message thread), and apply any exemptions counsel has approved. Free-text fields — messages, tickets, notes — are where this matters, and they're why a human reviews the package before it goes out.

**Deliver.** Only through the verified channel: behind login, or an expiring link sent to the account's email. Never as an attachment to whatever address the request came from.

**Record.** Keep a record of the request, the verification, what was sent and when — without keeping a second copy of the export itself (24.4's rule about logs applies here too).

### The other rights, briefly

- **Rectification.** Fix it at the source of truth, then propagate to copies and tell recipients you shared it with (Article 19 requires notifying them unless that's impossible or disproportionate).
- **Restriction.** Keep the data but stop using it, typically while an accuracy dispute or objection is resolved. That needs a real "restricted" state that downstream jobs — emails, matching, analytics — actually respect.
- **Objection and opt-outs.** A suppression flag, consulted by every pipeline that does the objected-to thing. For the CCPA's opt-out of sale and sharing (which includes most cross-context behavioral advertising), the flag also has to reach the ad and analytics partners it concerns, within the deadline.
- **Erasure.** The saga-with-a-ledger from 24.4, triggered from this pipeline.
- **Automated decisions.** If the board auto-rejects candidates whose match score is below a threshold, that's the textbook case of a solely automated decision with significant effects — the GDPR's recitals name e-recruiting without human intervention explicitly. People have the right to human review, which means the system needs a human-review path, not just an appeal email address. It also almost certainly means a data protection impact assessment before you ship it.

### The map has to stay true

A data map is accurate the day it's written and decays from then on. New tables, new analytics events, a SaaS tool someone bought on a company card, a model trained on a warehouse export. Three controls cover most drift:

- **At the schema.** CI fails when a table appears that no store in the map claims (Implementation below).
- **At procurement.** No new vendor that receives personal data without a map entry and a data processing agreement (34.2b).
- **At design.** A short privacy question in design review — what personal data, what purpose, what basis, how long, how would a request reach it — catches the rest earlier and more cheaply than any audit.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any product with users in the EU, UK or California** needs a written data map and a request runbook from the first user. That's a document and a support macro, not a system.
- **One to five requests a month:** handler scripts for the major stores, run by an engineer, with a human assembling and reviewing. A day of engineering per request is a sign you're here and not yet ready for more.
- **Tens to hundreds a month** — typical for a consumer product once data-removal services find it: a real pipeline with deadlines, verification and an audit trail, plus a self-serve export in account settings that absorbs many requests before they become tickets.
- **B2B products where you're mostly a processor:** customer-facing admin tooling (export or delete an end user across the workspace) matters more than your own request pipeline. Enterprise customers will test it in procurement.
- **Automated decisions about people, or sensitive categories** (health, biometrics, children's data): all of the above, plus impact assessments before building.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A workflow engine for three requests a year.** A runbook and a good map are complete at that volume.
- **Buying a privacy platform before you have a map.** The platform can discover SaaS tools and run workflows, but it can't know what your own tables mean or which role you play for them.
- **Separate implementations per law.** Build once to the shared shape; parameterize deadlines and verification.
- **Collecting passports to verify requests.** It creates a new sensitive dataset and is usually disproportionate.
- **Engineers deciding legal questions** — retention periods, whether an exemption applies, controller or processor for an ambiguous dataset. Build the switch; let counsel set it.
- **Chasing unlinked pre-login analytics** to attach it to a requester, which increases linkage for everyone in order to comply for one.

## Trade-offs

- **Every new store carries a tax.** A map entry, a subject key, handlers, retention. Features get slightly slower to ship, forever.
- **A stale map is worse than none.** It produces confident, incomplete exports, and an incomplete export sent on time still breaks the law.
- **The export pipeline is an exfiltration tool.** It gathers everything about one person into a single package on request. Build it with the same care as admin impersonation (26.1): strict verification, audit logging, rate limits, alerts on unusual volume.
- **Verification is a dial with a failure at each end.** Too loose and you breach someone's data to an impostor; too strict and you're obstructing a legal right.
- **Deadlines create an on-call obligation** that doesn't pause for holidays, launches or the one engineer who understands the handlers being away.
- **Identity resolution cuts both ways.** Linking every identifier a person has makes requests complete and also makes your data more identifiable. The same graph that powers access requests would power a much more invasive profile.
- **The request log is sensitive in itself.** That someone asked for deletion, or to stop being tracked, is information about them.
- **Minimization collides with product and ML appetite.** "Collect it, we might need it" is the default instinct of every analytics team, and every field collected is a field every request must reach.
- **Processor obligations flow from your customers' contracts**, which means your request tooling's behavior is partly set by what sales agreed to.

## Alternatives

**A spreadsheet map and a manual runbook.** Entirely viable at low volume, and what most companies actually run for their first years. It fails silently as the product grows, so review it on a schedule rather than when a request arrives.

**Privacy platforms** — OneTrust, Transcend, DataGrail, Ketch, Osano and others — provide request portals, deadline tracking, verification flows and prebuilt connectors to common SaaS tools, and some offer automated data discovery. They're strongest on the SaaS sprawl and weakest on your own databases, where you'll still write the handlers. Worth it when the number of third-party tools, not your own schema, is what makes requests hard.

**Self-serve export and deletion in the product.** Reduces volume a lot and people like it. It isn't a complete answer: it doesn't reach warehouses, vendors or non-account-holders, and a GDPR access response still needs the "about the processing" information.

**Collect less.** The cheapest request to answer is about data you never stored. Dropping a store, shortening retention, or aggregating analytics early removes work from every future request. This is a genuine alternative, not a platitude.

**Don't serve a jurisdiction.** Some companies geoblock markets whose compliance cost outweighs the revenue. A legitimate business choice, and a last resort.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Written map, monitored inbox, trained support.** A spreadsheet listing every store with the fields above, a privacy contact address, a support macro that recognizes requests and routes them the same day, and a runbook. *Signal to move on:* your first real request takes more than a day of engineering, or misses a store.

**L1 — Subject keys and handlers.** Put internal IDs in every store, link analytics identities at login, record every email an account has ever used, and write export and erase handlers for the five or six stores that hold most of the data. Run them with a script. *Signal:* requests arrive monthly, or you find a store the script didn't cover.

**L2 — Map as code, pipeline with deadlines.** Move the map into the repository so CI enforces it; add an orchestrator that tracks deadlines, verification, fan-out, review and delivery with an audit trail; ship self-serve export. *Signal:* tens of requests a month, the first bulk batch from a data-removal service, or an enterprise customer asking how you support their requests.

**L3 — Reach and propagation.** Connectors for SaaS vendors, suppression flags propagated to partners, customer-facing tooling for data you process as a processor, and periodic reconciliation between the map and reality. *Signal:* the long tail of third-party tools is now where requests get stuck.

## Implementation

TypeScript, because the core of this is typed declarations and orchestration rather than anything language-specific.

### The map as code

```ts
type Basis = "consent" | "contract" | "legal_obligation"
           | "legitimate_interests" | "vital_interests" | "public_task";

type Handlers =
  | { kind: "automated"; exportFor(ids: SubjectIds): Promise<ExportPart>;
                         erase(ids: SubjectIds): Promise<void> }
  | { kind: "forward_to_controller"; controllersFor(ids: SubjectIds): Promise<ControllerRef[]> }
  | { kind: "manual"; runbook: string };

interface DataStore {
  id: string;
  owner: string;                           // team accountable for keeping this entry true
  tables: string[];                        // what the schema check matches against
  role: "controller" | "processor";
  subjects: Array<"candidate" | "recruiter" | "visitor">;
  purposes: Array<{ purpose: string; basis: Basis }>;
  retention: RetentionRuleId;              // entry in the 24.4 matrix
  recipients: string[];                    // subprocessors that receive copies
  region: "eu" | "us";
  subjectKey: "user_id" | "candidate_id" | "anonymous_id" | "email";
  handlers: Handlers;
}

export const stores: DataStore[] = [
  {
    id: "candidate-profiles", owner: "team-candidates",
    tables: ["candidates", "candidate_profiles", "cv_files"],
    role: "controller", subjects: ["candidate"],
    purposes: [{ purpose: "provide candidate account", basis: "contract" }],
    retention: "account-life-plus-30d", recipients: ["s3"], region: "eu",
    subjectKey: "candidate_id", handlers: profileHandlers,
  },
  {
    id: "employer-pipeline", owner: "team-ats",
    tables: ["pipeline_stages", "ratings", "recruiter_notes"],
    role: "processor", subjects: ["candidate"],
    purposes: [{ purpose: "employer's hiring process", basis: "contract" }],
    retention: "per-employer-contract", recipients: [], region: "eu",
    subjectKey: "candidate_id",
    handlers: { kind: "forward_to_controller", controllersFor: employersHoldingCandidate },
  },
  {
    id: "crm", owner: "team-marketing", tables: [],
    role: "controller", subjects: ["candidate", "recruiter", "visitor"],
    purposes: [{ purpose: "webinar follow-up", basis: "consent" }],
    retention: "consent-life", recipients: ["crm-vendor"], region: "us",
    subjectKey: "email",
    handlers: { kind: "manual", runbook: "https://wiki.internal/privacy/crm" },
  },
  // ...analytics, warehouse, email provider, support desk, match-model features
];
```

The `manual` handler is fine. The point of the map is that the CRM appears in it at all, so the next request reaches it even if a person has to click through the vendor's UI to do so.

### Keeping it true: a schema check in CI

```ts
const claimed = new Set(stores.flatMap(s => s.tables));
const exempt = new Map(Object.entries(noPersonalData)); // table -> reason, reviewed like code

const unclaimed = (await listTables(db)).filter(t => !claimed.has(t) && !exempt.has(t));
if (unclaimed.length > 0) {
  fail(`Tables not in the data map: ${unclaimed.join(", ")}.
Add them to a store in privacy/stores.ts, or to noPersonalData with a reason.`);
}
```

> **What this check can't see.** It catches the new table; it doesn't catch a new column of personal data on an existing table, a JSON blob that grew an `email` field, a new analytics event, or a vendor. It enforces that someone _thought_ about each table, which is most of the value. The procurement gate and design review cover the rest.

### The orchestrator: deadlines, fan-out, forwarding

```ts
function deadline(req: PrivacyRequest): Date {
  // Clock starts at receipt by anyone in the company, not when engineering sees it.
  const byGdpr = addCalendarMonths(req.receivedAt, 1);
  const byCcpa = addDays(req.receivedAt, 45);
  return min(byGdpr, byCcpa); // strictest applicable, applied to everyone
}

async function runAccessRequest(req: VerifiedRequest): Promise<AccessResult> {
  const ids = await resolveSubject(req.subject);     // expands every email, id, linked anonymous id
  const parts: ExportPart[] = [];
  const pending: PendingItem[] = [];

  for (const store of stores.filter(s => s.subjects.includes(req.subjectType))) {
    const h = store.handlers;
    try {
      if (h.kind === "automated") {
        parts.push(await h.exportFor(ids));
      } else if (h.kind === "forward_to_controller") {
        for (const c of await h.controllersFor(ids)) {
          pending.push(await notifyController(c, req)); // tell the employer; tell the person who to ask
        }
      } else {
        pending.push(await openManualTask(store, req, deadline(req)));
      }
    } catch (err) {
      pending.push(failedHandler(store, err)); // request stays open; never ship partial silently
    }
  }

  return {
    data: parts,
    aboutProcessing: describeFromMap(stores, ids),   // purposes, recipients, retention, rights
    pending,                                          // blocks delivery until empty or resolved
    needsHumanReview: parts.some(p => p.hasFreeText), // redact third parties before it goes out
  };
}
```

> **The package is the most sensitive file you'll ever produce about this person.** Build it into encrypted storage, deliver it behind login or through an expiring link to the account's verified email, delete it after a short window, and record a manifest of what was sent — store names, row counts, a hash — rather than a copy.

Notice what `describeFromMap` implies: the "information about the processing" half of the response is generated from the same declarations CI enforces. If the map is wrong, the response is wrong in the same way, and that's the correct coupling — there's only one place to fix it.

## Further Reading

- **GDPR text, Articles 5, 6, 11, 12, 15–22, 28, 30 and 35** — principles, lawful bases, the rights and their deadlines, processor duties, the record of processing, impact assessments. Shorter and more readable than its reputation; Recital 71 is where e-recruiting appears.
- **EDPB Guidelines 01/2022 on data subject rights — Right of access** (final version, 2023) — the most detailed official treatment of access requests: scope, verification, format, the "about the processing" information, and limits.
- **UK ICO, "Right of access" guidance** — practical and example-heavy, including handling requests made through third-party services.
- **California Code of Regulations, Title 11, §7000 et seq. (CCPA regulations)** — request methods, verification standards, authorized agents, and the deadlines table above.

- **LINDDUN** — a privacy threat-modeling method, useful for the design-review step.
- **IAPP US State Privacy Legislation Tracker** — which state laws exist and what they require, kept current.

The habit to take from this: **every time you add a table, an event or a vendor, ask whose data it is, why you have it, and how you'd hand it to them or remove it within a month.** If you can't answer, the answer will be improvised under a deadline.
