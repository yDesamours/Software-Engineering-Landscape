# Legal Documents as System Artifacts: Versioned Terms, Acceptance Records, and Contracts That Change Behavior

_Module 34.2b — Legal & Compliance Considerations_

> Engineering shape, not legal advice. Lawyers write the terms, decide what counts as a material change, and negotiate the contracts. This article is about the system underneath: one that can prove who agreed to what, change documents without breaking promises, and make contract terms actually govern what the product does.

## The Problem

Suppose you run an online whiteboard for teams. Anyone can sign up and use it free; teams pay by card; large companies sign enterprise contracts with a negotiated agreement, a data processing agreement (DPA) and security commitments. The legal documents live in the CMS as pages: Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, a list of subprocessors.

Over one year, four things happen.

**A dispute nobody can prove.** A customer sues over a billing issue. Your lawyers point to the arbitration clause in the Terms. The customer's lawyers ask: which version of the Terms, and how did my client agree? The Terms page has been edited six times in the CMS, each edit overwriting the last. The signup page said "By continuing, you agree to our Terms" in small grey text below the button, and nobody kept a copy of that page either. There's a `created_at` on the user row and nothing else. The arbitration clause may well not bind them.

**A quiet change that wasn't quiet.** The product team launches AI features and wants to improve them using boards people have drawn. Someone updates the Privacy Policy to say content "may be used to improve our services, including machine learning models," publishes it, and moves on. Three weeks later a user notices the diff, it spreads on social media, enterprise customers' legal teams start emailing, and a regulator asks questions. The US FTC had said publicly in 2024 that expanding data use through a quiet, retroactive policy change may be unfair or deceptive.

**A vendor added too quickly.** Engineering integrates a new transcription vendor for voice notes and ships it on a Tuesday. Your enterprise DPA promises customers 30 days' notice before any new subprocessor processes their data, and the right to object. A customer's data protection officer notices the subprocessor page changed — after their employees' audio has already been sent.

**A negotiated term the product didn't know about.** A bank negotiated "no use of our content for AI training" and "EU hosting only" into its contract. Those clauses live in a signed PDF in the sales team's drive. The AI training pipeline reads every board in every tenant where a feature flag is on. The flag has never heard of the bank's contract.

In every case, the legal text was fine. What failed was the system around it: documents without versions, agreement without evidence, changes without a process, and contract terms that never became configuration.

## What It Is

Legal documents are **versioned artifacts**, and people's relationships to them — who accepted which version, how, on whose behalf, and which terms apply to which customer — are **data**. Treating them that way is what this article describes.

The documents fall into two groups that behave differently:

- **Agreements** — Terms of Service, an enterprise master agreement and order form, a DPA, an acceptable use policy, an SLA. People accept them, and they bind both sides.
- **Notices** — a privacy policy (privacy notice), a cookie notice, a subprocessor list. They inform; nobody "agrees" to them.

That second distinction matters more than it looks. Making people tick "I agree to the Privacy Policy" suggests their data use rests on agreement, which muddles the lawful basis (34.1a) and invites the argument that withdrawing the agreement should stop the processing. The usual pattern is "I agree to the Terms" plus "see our Privacy Policy." Likewise, **accepting terms is not consent** to optional data uses (34.2a); bundling the two tends to invalidate the consent.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Legal documents are edited in place; nobody can produce the exact text of the Terms as they were on a given date.
- The database can't answer "which version of the Terms did this user accept, and how?"
- Signup says "by continuing you agree" with no checkbox, and nobody knows whether the link to the Terms was even visible on mobile.
- Terms changes are announced by editing the page.
- Nobody decides whether a change is material, or what that implies.
- API-only customers and dormant accounts have never seen any updated terms.
- The subprocessor list is a hand-edited page, and adding a vendor in code doesn't touch it.
- Negotiated contract terms (data region, AI training exclusions, retention, notice periods) exist only in PDFs.
- The Privacy Policy describes data uses that the data map (34.1a) doesn't, or the other way round.
- In a B2B product, you record which user clicked "accept," but not which organization they accepted for, or whether they were allowed to.

## How It Works

### Documents are immutable, versioned artifacts

Every published version of every legal document gets:

- **A stable identifier and version** — `terms/2026-07`, `dpa/v4`.
- **An effective date**, separate from the publish date, because a change is usually announced in advance.
- **A content hash** of the exact text, and an archived rendered copy. Once published, a version is never edited; corrections are a new version.
- **Locale variants**, with a note of which language version controls if they differ.
- **A change summary** in plain language, which is also what you'll send to users.

Keeping them in version control, or a CMS configured to keep immutable history, is enough. What matters is that the question "what exactly did the Terms say on 3 March 2025?" has a guaranteed, provable answer.

### How agreement is presented decides whether it holds

In US courts, whether online terms bind someone depends heavily on the design of the page where they supposedly agreed. The pattern of decisions is consistent enough to build from:

- **Clickwrap** — an unticked checkbox or an "I agree" button next to a link to the terms. The strongest form; courts routinely enforce it.
- **Sign-in wrap** — "By signing up, you agree to our Terms," near the button. Enforced when the notice is clearly visible and the action unambiguously signals agreement; not enforced when the notice is small, low-contrast, or separated from the button. _Meyer v. Uber_ (2017) upheld one; _Berman v. Freedom Financial Network_ (2022) rejected another largely on its visual design.
- **Browsewrap** — a Terms link in the footer, and "using the site means you agree." Rarely enforced, since _Specht v. Netscape_ (2002).

EU consumer law adds its own layer — terms must be transparent, and unfair terms don't bind consumers whatever the design — but the engineering lesson is the same everywhere: **the UI is evidence**. So record how acceptance was presented, not just that it happened: which flow, which UI version, the exact assent wording, whether it was a checkbox or a button. Keep screenshots of each version of those flows the same way you keep versions of the documents.

### The acceptance record

An append-only record, one row per acceptance:

- **Who** — the user.
- **For whom** — in B2B, the organization being bound. A user accepting enterprise terms on behalf of a company is a different fact from a user accepting consumer terms for themselves, and whether that user had authority to bind the company is a real question. Record their role at the time (an owner, a billing admin), and restrict who can accept organization-level terms.
- **What** — document ID, version and content hash.
- **How** — flow name, UI version, checkbox or button, the assent wording shown.
- **When** — timestamp, and the effective date of the version accepted.
- **Context** — locale and platform. Only as much network metadata as your lawyers actually need.

It's personal data, so it goes in the data map (34.1a). It has an unusual retention: it may be needed as evidence in a dispute long after the account is deleted, which is a legitimate reason to keep a minimal version beyond deletion (24.4's retention matrix is where that gets decided).

For negotiated enterprise contracts, the "acceptance" is a signature outside your product. The system still needs a record that the signed contract exists, which version of your standard documents it incorporates, and which of its terms change product behavior (below).

### Changing documents without breaking promises

Every change is one of two kinds, and someone with legal authority must decide which:

- **Non-material** — typo fixes, clarifications, a new office address. Publish a new version, update the changelog, no notification needed.
- **Material** — anything that reduces users' rights or expands what you do with their data: new data uses, new sharing, a new arbitration clause, price-related terms, a change in liability.

Material changes follow a process:

1. **Advance notice.** Email and in-app notice with the change summary and an effective date — commonly 30 days out. EU consumer law and many contracts expect this, and enterprise agreements usually specify it.
2. **Old terms govern until the new ones take effect.** Both versions are live for a while, so the system must know which version applies to whom and when.
3. **Re-acceptance where needed.** For changes that need affirmative agreement, block at the next login with an interstitial, not a dismissible banner. Decide in advance what happens to people who refuse: usually a path to export their data and close the account.
4. **No retroactive expansion of data use.** A new use of data already collected — training models on existing content, sharing it with a new kind of partner — can't be slipped in through a policy edit. In the US, the FTC's 2024 guidance says so directly. Under the GDPR, a new purpose needs its own lawful basis (34.1a), often consent (34.2a), whatever the policy says. The honest designs are "applies only to data collected after the effective date" or "opt-in," and the data pipeline has to know which.

The awkward populations need deliberate answers: **API-only integrations** that never render an interstitial (notify the account owner and require acceptance in the dashboard; don't break production API traffic without warning), **dormant accounts** (notice by email; acceptance on return), and **enterprise customers on negotiated contracts** (standard-terms changes usually don't apply to them at all unless their contract incorporates the online terms — which the system must know).

### The privacy notice is generated from the data map

A privacy notice has required content: under the GDPR, what data, for which purposes, on which lawful bases, shared with whom, transferred where, kept how long, and what rights people have. California requires the policy to be updated at least once every twelve months. Almost every one of those items already exists in the data map from 34.1a, with its `purposes`, `recipients`, `region` and `retention`.

So generate the factual sections of the notice from the map, and let lawyers write the prose around them. The notice and the reality then drift together or not at all, and a change to the map that affects the notice shows up as a diff someone must approve — which is exactly how the Problem's AI-training change should have surfaced.

### Contract terms become configuration

Enterprise contracts contain clauses that aren't just legal promises; they're product requirements for one customer:

| Clause | What it controls in the system |
|---|---|
| Data hosted in the EU only | Tenant home region (34.1b), vendor selection |
| No use of customer content for AI training or product improvement | Exclusion from training and evaluation pipelines |
| Retention of deleted content limited to 30 days | Retention rules (24.4) for this tenant |
| 30 days' notice of new subprocessors, right to object | Subprocessor gating (below) |
| Breach notification within 48 hours | Incident runbook parameters (24.3) |
| Named support region or personnel | Support access rules (34.1b) |

Each of these becomes a tenant setting with **provenance**: which contract, which clause, which version, who entered it and when. Provenance is what lets you answer a customer's auditor ("show me how you enforce clause 7.2") and what stops someone "cleaning up" a setting they don't recognize.

The source of truth for the contract is usually a contract-management system or a CRM, not your database. The integration goes one way: the contract record drives the tenant configuration; the product never lets an ordinary admin loosen a contract-derived setting.

### Subprocessors: a list with a lifecycle

Under the GDPR, a processor may use sub-processors only with the controller's authorization. The common form is a general authorization with advance notice of changes and a chance to object (Article 28(2)); the EU's Standard Contractual Clauses use the same structure with a notice period the parties choose. In practice, enterprise DPAs promise something like "30 days' notice, subscribe here, object by email."

That turns a vendor integration into a state machine:

```
  proposed ──► announced ──(notice period elapses)──► active
     │            │                                      │
     │            └── customer objects ──► resolve: exclude that tenant,
     │                                     offer alternative, or terminate
     └── rejected                                        │
                                                 retired ──► removed from list
```

And the system enforces it: a vendor's integration can't process a tenant's data until it's `active` for that tenant — meaning the notice period that tenant is owed has elapsed and they haven't objected. Tenants with longer contractual notice periods wait longer. The subprocessor page and the notification emails are generated from the same registry as the data map's `recipients`, so adding a vendor in code without adding it to the registry fails the same CI check (34.1a).

This slows shipping. It's supposed to. The alternative is the Problem's Tuesday release.

## When It's Appropriate

- **From the first signup.** Versioned documents, a clickwrap checkbox and an acceptance record cost a day and are the only way to prove agreement later. There's no later point at which you can reconstruct them.
- **Any product whose terms carry weight in a dispute** — arbitration clauses, liability limits, payment terms, content licenses.
- **Consumer products that will change their data practices**, which is all of them. The notice-and-re-acceptance flow is what makes changes legitimate.
- **B2B products with DPAs**, once any DPA promises notice of subprocessor changes. From then on, the registry and gating are how you keep that promise.
- **Enterprise contracts with operational clauses** — regions, AI exclusions, retention — once there are more than a handful and nobody can hold them all in their head.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Re-acceptance for non-material changes.** Blocking everyone at login over a typo fix trains people to click through without reading, and weakens the flow for the change that matters.
- **Asking people to "agree" to the privacy policy.** It's a notice.
- **Building a contract-management system.** Buy one, or use the CRM, and integrate the clauses that change behavior. Most clauses don't.
- **Per-customer configuration for clauses the product can't vary.** If every customer gets EU hosting in your EU cell anyway, the clause needs a record, not a setting.
- **Negotiating every small customer's terms.** A policy of standard terms below a certain deal size is a business decision with a large engineering payoff: fewer configurations to enforce.
- **A subprocessor state machine before anyone has a DPA with notice terms.** Until then, a maintained list is enough.

## Trade-offs

- **Friction at the moments that matter.** Clickwrap checkboxes reduce signup conversion slightly; re-acceptance interstitials interrupt returning users, and some leave.
- **Two versions live at once.** During every notice period the system must know which terms apply to whom, and features may differ by version.
- **Shipping slows for vendors.** Every new subprocessor waits out the longest notice period owed to any affected customer — a month or more for a feature that took a week to build.
- **Contract-derived configuration fragments behavior.** Each negotiated clause is a conditional somewhere, and every test matrix grows. The fragmentation is the direct cost of saying yes to enterprise redlines.
- **Acceptance records outlive accounts.** They're kept for disputes, minimal and access-controlled, but they're personal data retained after deletion.
- **Legal text changes depend on engineering.** If publishing a new version requires a deploy, legal is blocked on engineering; if it doesn't, engineering must make sure an edit can't overwrite a published version.
- **Evidence is only as good as its capture.** A missing UI version or screenshot for one signup flow — the mobile one, usually — leaves a hole exactly where the dispute will land.

## Alternatives

**Clickwrap and acceptance-management vendors** (Ironclad Clickwrap and others) provide versioning, acceptance records, re-acceptance flows and evidence packages. A reasonable choice when legal wants control and engineering wants to stay out of the document business.

**Contract lifecycle management (CLM) tools** hold enterprise contracts, redlines and obligations; the integration from CLM to tenant configuration is still yours to build.

**Standard terms only.** Refuse negotiated terms below some threshold, and offer a small set of paid, pre-built options — EU hosting, AI exclusion, extended retention — as plan features instead of bespoke clauses. The same legal outcomes, a fraction of the configuration.

**Terms that don't change often.** Draft the Terms generously and stably enough that most product changes don't need to touch them, and keep changeable operational detail in separate, clearly referenced documents (a subprocessor list, a security page).

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Versions and evidence.** Documents in version control with effective dates and hashes; a clickwrap checkbox at signup; an acceptance record with version, flow and wording; archived screenshots of each signup flow. *Signal to move on:* your first material change, or your first enterprise DPA.

**L1 — Change process.** Material/non-material classification by legal, advance notice emails, a public changelog, a blocking re-acceptance flow, handling for API-only and dormant accounts, and the privacy notice's factual sections generated from the data map. *Signal:* enterprise contracts with clauses that change product behavior.

**L2 — Contracts drive configuration.** Contract-derived tenant settings with provenance, synced from the CRM or CLM; the subprocessor registry with lifecycle states, per-tenant notice periods and objections; vendor integrations gated on `active`. *Signal:* more than a handful of negotiated clauses, or an audit asking you to demonstrate enforcement of one.

**L3 — Audit and assurance.** Periodic checks that every tenant's configuration matches its contract, evidence packages generated on demand for disputes, and reporting on who is still on old terms.

## Implementation

TypeScript, in three pieces: requiring current acceptance, gating subprocessors, and contract-derived settings.

### Documents, acceptance, and the re-acceptance gate

```ts
interface DocVersion {
  docId: "terms" | "enterprise-terms" | "aup";
  version: string;                       // "2026-07"
  contentHash: string;
  publishedAt: Date;
  effectiveAt: Date;                     // later than publishedAt for material changes
  material: boolean;
  requiresReacceptance: boolean;
}

interface Acceptance {
  userId: string;
  onBehalfOf: { kind: "self" } | { kind: "organization"; orgId: string; roleAtTime: string };
  docId: DocVersion["docId"];
  version: string;
  contentHash: string;                   // must match the published version
  flow: "signup_web" | "signup_ios" | "reaccept_interstitial" | "dashboard_api_terms";
  uiVersion: string;                     // links to archived screenshots of that flow
  assentText: string;                    // "I agree to the Terms of Service"
  control: "checkbox" | "button";
  at: Date;
}

async function requireCurrentTerms(req: Request, res: Response, next: NextFunction) {
  const current = await docs.inEffect("terms", new Date());
  if (!current.requiresReacceptance) return next();
  if (await acceptances.has(req.user.id, "terms", current.version)) return next();

  if (req.isApiClient) {
    // Don't break production integrations; the account owner is notified
    // and accepts in the dashboard. Track and escalate instead of blocking.
    await reacceptance.flagApiAccount(req.user.accountId, current);
    return next();
  }
  return res.redirect(`/legal/accept?doc=terms&version=${current.version}`);
}
```

> **The hash is the point.** Storing `version: "2026-07"` proves only that someone accepted something with that label. Storing the content hash, and refusing to record an acceptance whose hash doesn't match the published text, proves they were shown _that_ text. If a CMS edit ever changes a published version's content, the mismatch shows up at the next acceptance instead of in court.

### Subprocessors: registry, lifecycle and per-tenant gating

```ts
interface Subprocessor {
  id: string;                            // "transcribe-co"
  purpose: string;
  regions: Region[];
  state: "proposed" | "announced" | "active" | "retired";
  announcedAt?: Date;
}

async function canProcessFor(tenant: TenantContext, subId: string, now = new Date()): Promise<boolean> {
  const sub = await registry.get(subId);
  if (sub.state !== "announced" && sub.state !== "active") return false;
  if (!sub.regions.includes(tenant.region)) return false;                     // 34.1b

  const noticeDays = tenant.contract?.subprocessorNoticeDays ?? DEFAULT_NOTICE_DAYS;
  const noticeElapsed = sub.announcedAt && daysBetween(sub.announcedAt, now) >= noticeDays;
  const objected = await objections.exists(tenant.id, subId);
  return Boolean(noticeElapsed) && !objected;
}

// At the integration point:
async function transcribeVoiceNote(tenant: TenantContext, note: VoiceNote) {
  if (!(await canProcessFor(tenant, "transcribe-co"))) {
    return { status: "unavailable", reason: "pending subprocessor notice or objection" };
  }
  return transcribeCo.transcribe(note);
}
```

> **Per tenant, not global.** "Active" for the registry isn't the same as allowed for every tenant. A tenant whose contract promises 60 days' notice gets the feature 30 days after one with the standard 30. A tenant that objected doesn't get it at all until the objection is resolved. That's why the check takes the tenant, and why a single global feature flag can't express it.

### Contract-derived settings with provenance

```ts
interface ContractDerived<T> {
  value: T;
  source: { contractId: string; clause: string; version: string };
  recordedBy: string;
  recordedAt: Date;
}

interface TenantPolicy {
  region: ContractDerived<Region> | Region;
  excludeFromAiTraining: ContractDerived<boolean> | boolean;
  deletedContentRetentionDays: ContractDerived<number> | number;
  subprocessorNoticeDays?: ContractDerived<number>;
}

// Training pipeline: the eligibility query reads the policy, never a feature flag alone.
const eligibleTenants = (await tenants.all()).filter(t =>
  !valueOf(t.policy.excludeFromAiTraining) &&
  t.aiTrainingOptIn === true            // and, per 34.1a/34.2a, a lawful basis for the new use
);
```

> **Admins can't loosen contract terms.** The settings UI shows contract-derived values as locked, with the clause they came from. Changing one requires the contract record to change first. Without that rule, a customer's admin, or your own support engineer "fixing" a misconfiguration, can quietly override what the company signed.

## Further Reading

- **_Specht v. Netscape_ (2d Cir. 2002), _Meyer v. Uber_ (2d Cir. 2017) and _Berman v. Freedom Financial Network_ (9th Cir. 2022)** — the arc of US case law on when online terms bind, with the page designs described in detail.
- **FTC, "AI (and other) Companies: Quietly Changing Your Terms of Service Could Be Unfair or Deceptive"** (2024) — the US regulator's position on retroactive changes to data use.
- **GDPR Articles 12–14 and 28** — transparency and notice content, and processor obligations including subprocessor authorization.
- **Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914, Clause 9** — how the Standard Contractual Clauses handle subprocessor authorization and notice.
- **EU Unfair Contract Terms Directive (93/13/EEC)** — why consumer terms, and unilateral change clauses in particular, have limits regardless of how they were accepted.
- **Digital Services Act, Article 14** — requirements for terms and conditions of intermediary services, including informing users of significant changes, if you host user content in the EU.
- **California Civil Code §1798.130(a)(5)** — the requirement to update the privacy policy at least every twelve months.
- **In this collection:** 34.1a (the data map the privacy notice is generated from), 34.1b (region clauses), 34.2a (consent, which acceptance is not), 24.3 and 24.4 (incident commitments and retention), 3.4 (feature flags, and why a contract term shouldn't be one), 26.3 (admin permissions and audit logging).

No sibling article is proposed: the substance is records and gating, which read the same in any of the collection's languages.

The habit to take from this: **when a document says the product will or won't do something, find the line of code that makes it true.** If there isn't one, the promise is being kept by luck.
