# Data Residency and Cross-Border Transfers: Where Data Lives, and Who Can Reach It

_Module 34.1b — Legal & Compliance Considerations_

> As in 34.1a: this is the engineering shape of the problem, not legal advice. Transfer law is the fastest-moving corner of privacy law — the main EU–US mechanism has been struck down twice and is under challenge again as this is written. Which mechanism you rely on is counsel's call. Making sure you _can_ switch is yours.

## The Problem

Suppose you run an employee-engagement survey platform. Companies send pulse surveys to their staff; employees answer, often with free-text comments about their managers; HR sees dashboards. You're a US company, hosted in a single AWS region in Virginia, and your customers so far are mostly American.

Then a German manufacturer with 40,000 employees wants to buy. Their security questionnaire asks:

1. Where will our data be stored and processed?
2. Can anyone outside the EU access it — your staff, your vendors, your government?
3. List every subprocessor and its location.
4. What's your legal basis for any transfer outside the EU, and what happens if it's invalidated?

The team's answer is "we'll add an EU region." Two sprints later there's a Postgres cluster in Frankfurt, and new EU tenants are created there. Then someone draws the actual data flows for one EU survey response:

```
                  ┌──────────── Frankfurt ────────────┐
 employee ───────►│  API  ──►  Postgres (EU)           │
                  └───┬──────────┬────────────┬───────┘
                      │          │            │
      nightly ETL ────┘          │            └──── cross-region backup copy
            ▼                    ▼                        ▼
   warehouse (Virginia)   logs + traces (US site      backups (Oregon)
                          of a monitoring vendor,
                          with request bodies)
                                 │
   error tracker (US) ◄──────────┘              search index (one global cluster, Virginia)
   "summarize comments" feature ──► LLM API (US)
   survey invitation emails ──► email provider (US)
   support agents in Austin and Manila ──► admin panel ──► reads Frankfurt directly
```

The database moved. The data didn't. Every one of those arrows is a copy or an access outside the EU, and most were added by people who weren't thinking about geography at all — they were adding backups, observability, search and a helpful AI feature.

And question 4 turns out to be live. The EU–US mechanism most of those US vendors rely on was upheld by the EU's General Court in 2025, is under appeal, and in mid-2026 a US Supreme Court ruling on the independence of the Federal Trade Commission led the privacy group noyb to demand its withdrawal. The customer's lawyers want to know what the platform does if that mechanism falls — and "we'd have to think about it" loses the deal.

## What It Is

"Where does our data live?" is really four separate questions, and teams get into trouble by answering one and assuming it covers the others:

| Term | The question it answers | Typical source |
|---|---|---|
| **Data residency** | Where is the data stored and processed? | Customer contracts, procurement requirements |
| **Data localization** | Must the data (or a copy) stay inside a country by law? | Specific national laws (Russia, China for some data, sectoral rules like India's payment-data rule) |
| **Transfer restrictions** | May data leave, and under what legal mechanism? | GDPR Chapter V, UK GDPR, China's PIPL, others |
| **Data sovereignty** | Whose laws can compel access to it? | The legal nationality of whoever operates the infrastructure |

The GDPR is a **transfer** regime, not a localization regime: EU personal data may go anywhere, provided a valid transfer mechanism and adequate protection travel with it. Most enterprise customers, though, ask for **residency**, because it's easier to verify and removes transfer questions for the data that stays put. And the most skeptical ones ask about **sovereignty**, because residency alone doesn't answer it: under the US CLOUD Act, a US-headquartered provider can be compelled to produce data it controls regardless of where the servers are. Frankfurt servers run by a US company are resident in the EU and still reachable by a US warrant.

Keep the four apart and most of the confusion disappears. "EU region" solves residency for what's in it. It doesn't, on its own, solve transfers (people and vendors still reach it) or sovereignty (the operator is still American).

## Recognizing the Problem

- An EU prospect's security questionnaire asks where data is stored, and the honest answer has more than one region in it.
- "We have an EU region" is true of the database and not of the logs, backups, warehouse or search index.
- Your observability, error-tracking or email vendors are on their default (usually US) site.
- Support or on-call engineers outside the EU can open any tenant's data in the admin panel.
- Disaster-recovery backups replicate to a region in another jurisdiction.
- A new feature calls a third-party API (an LLM, a translation service, an enrichment service) and nobody checked where it processes data.
- Your subprocessor list doesn't include locations, or doesn't exist.
- Nobody can name the legal mechanism for each flow of EU data out of the EU, or what the fallback is if one is invalidated.
- A tenant's region is a config value that could be changed by editing a row.

## How It Works

### How the GDPR treats data leaving the EU

A transfer needs one of these, roughly in order of how often SaaS companies use them:

- **An adequacy decision.** The European Commission has found a short list of countries to offer essentially equivalent protection — among them the UK, Switzerland, Japan, South Korea, Canada (for commercial organizations) and, for companies that self-certify under the **EU–US Data Privacy Framework (DPF)**, the United States. Transfers there need nothing further.
- **Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs).** Commission-approved contract terms (the 2021 set, in modules for controller-to-processor, processor-to-processor and so on) signed between exporter and importer. Since the _Schrems II_ judgment in 2020, SCCs must be accompanied by a **transfer impact assessment**: an honest evaluation of whether the destination's laws, especially surveillance laws, let the importer actually keep the promises — and, if not, **supplementary measures** that close the gap.
- **Binding Corporate Rules.** Approved internal rules for transfers within a multinational group. Slow to obtain; relevant mainly to large groups.
- **Derogations** (Article 49) — explicit consent, necessity for a contract with the individual, and a few others. Meant for occasional transfers, not as the foundation of a product.

The DPF's history is why "what if it falls?" is a normal engineering question rather than paranoia. Its predecessors, Safe Harbor and Privacy Shield, were invalidated by the EU Court of Justice in 2015 and 2020, each time abruptly, each time leaving thousands of companies to switch mechanisms. The DPF (2023) survived its first challenge at the General Court in September 2025; that ruling is on appeal to the Court of Justice. In June 2026 the US Supreme Court held that the President may remove FTC commissioners at will, and noyb — the organization behind both previous invalidations — asked the Commission to withdraw the DPF, arguing the adequacy finding leaned on the FTC's independence. It hasn't been struck down. It may not be. But a design that works _only_ if it stands is a design with a known single point of failure.

The practical posture most counsel recommend: rely on the DPF where vendors are certified, and have SCCs signed in parallel so a ruling changes the paperwork, not the architecture.

**What counts as a transfer is broader than copying files.** Making data available to an importer in a third country — including letting its staff view it remotely — can be a transfer. Whether a particular access is one depends on which legal entities are involved: an engineer employed by your EU entity working from abroad is analyzed differently from one employed by your US parent. That's a question for counsel, but the engineering consequence is the same either way: **human access is part of the data flow**, and your design has to be able to control it.

### Everywhere the data actually goes

The Problem's diagram is the typical list. For each item, residency means "this copy is in-region too," or "this flow is deliberately excluded and covered by a mechanism":

- **Primary stores and their replicas** — including read replicas someone added in another region for latency.
- **Backups and DR copies.** The DR region must be in the same jurisdiction — easy in the EU (Frankfurt and Ireland), hard in a country with one cloud region.
- **Caches and CDNs.** Edge caches serve from everywhere; cached API responses with personal data leave the region by design.
- **Search indexes, queues, stream processors** — each is a store.
- **Logs, traces, metrics and error reports.** The most common leak, because request bodies, emails and IDs end up in them (19.1). Most observability vendors offer an EU site; the default usually isn't it.
- **The analytics warehouse and anything trained from it.**
- **Third-party APIs**: email and SMS delivery, LLMs, translation, enrichment, payment and support tools. Each has its own region options, or none.
- **People**: support, on-call, data analysts, contractors.
- **The control plane**: tenant names, admin emails and billing contacts in a global database, IAM users, audit logs of the control plane itself.

The data map from 34.1a already records `region` and `recipients` per store. Residency work begins by reading it with a map of the world next to it.

### Architectures, from least to most separation

**1. One region, contracts for everything.** Keep a single deployment and cover EU data with DPF certification or SCCs plus a transfer impact assessment, for your own company and down the vendor chain. This is where most companies correctly start, and it satisfies most customers who aren't large enterprises, public bodies or health providers. It fails the procurement question "is our data stored in the EU?"

**2. Regional data plane, thin global control plane.** Each tenant gets a **home region** at signup. Everything that holds tenant data — databases, object storage, search, queues, logs, backups, vendor accounts — is deployed per region, as a cell (5.4, 23.5). A small global layer holds only what's needed to route: tenant ID → region, plus billing and the company's own account data, which is usually controller data you can justify holding centrally.

```
                     ┌────────────── global control plane ─────────────┐
                     │  tenant directory:  t_812 → eu   t_455 → us      │
                     │  billing, company accounts                       │
                     └───────────────┬──────────────────────────────────┘
                     routes by tenant│
             ┌───────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
             ▼                                               ▼
  ┌──────── EU cell (Frankfurt + Ireland DR) ──┐  ┌──────── US cell (Virginia + Oregon DR) ─┐
  │ API · Postgres · S3 · search · queues      │  │ API · Postgres · S3 · search · queues    │
  │ logs → vendor EU site · email → EU account │  │ logs → vendor US site · email → US acct  │
  │ LLM → provider EU endpoint (or disabled)   │  │ LLM → provider US endpoint               │
  └────────────────────────────────────────────┘  └──────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

This is the standard answer for B2B SaaS once residency is a sales requirement. The hard parts are at the seams (below).

**3. Separate deployments per jurisdiction.** Fully independent stacks, often with separate identity systems, separate operations staff residing in the jurisdiction, and sometimes separate legal entities. The hyperscalers now sell this as a product: AWS's European Sovereign Cloud, generally available since January 2026 in Brandenburg, is a separate partition run by EU-resident staff under German-incorporated entities; Microsoft and Google offer their own versions, and EU-owned providers (OVHcloud, Scaleway, Hetzner, STACKIT and others) answer the sovereignty question by not being American at all. This is what public-sector, defense and some health customers require, and it costs roughly a second company's worth of operations.

**Cutting across all three: split what leaves.** Instead of keeping everything regional, send only non-personal or aggregated data across borders — survey scores aggregated to groups of at least five respondents, say, while raw responses and comments stay home. Genuinely aggregated data isn't personal data. Pseudonymized data still is, so replacing names with IDs and shipping the rows to a US warehouse doesn't take them out of scope.

**And: encryption as a supplementary measure.** Encrypting data with keys held in the region (or by the customer, as bring-your-own-key or hold-your-own-key) is the classic supplementary measure for transfers. It works fully only when the importer never needs the plaintext — backup storage, say. If your service must read the data to do its job, it must be able to reach the key, and so can anyone who can compel you. Customer-held keys also break features that need plaintext server-side: full-text search, AI summaries, server-rendered exports. It's a strong tool for specific flows, not a way to make a US service processing EU plaintext equivalent to an EU one.

### The seams of a regional architecture

Separating data planes is straightforward. The seams are where designs get quietly compromised:

- **Login.** A user types an email address. Which region holds their account? A global email → region index is personal data held centrally. Common answers: region-specific hostnames or workspace URLs (`acme.eu.example.com`) so routing needs no lookup; a global index of hashed emails, which is still pseudonymous personal data but minimal; or a global identity provider with only authentication data, justified as your own controller data. Pick one deliberately.
- **Users in several tenants across regions** — a consultant who belongs to an EU tenant and a US one. Either they get two accounts, or identity is global and their data is split by tenant.
- **Cross-tenant features** — benchmarks ("how does your engagement compare to your industry?"), global admin search, marketplace listings. Build them from aggregates exported by each region, never from raw cross-region queries.
- **Support and operations.** Region-scoped admin roles, EU-based support for EU tenants (often sold as part of an enterprise tier), and just-in-time access with a recorded justification for exceptions. Incident response gets slower when the person who knows the system can't look at the data; decide in advance who may, under what process.
- **Vendors per region.** Every subprocessor needs an EU configuration or an EU alternative. Some don't have one, and that decides whether a feature exists in the EU cell at all. The survey platform's AI summary feature ships in the EU only once its LLM provider offers in-region processing, or ships disabled.
- **Moving a tenant between regions.** Customers will ask. It's a tenant migration (5.3) with the extra rule that nothing may linger in the old region — including backups, which age out on their own schedule (24.4).

### Localization regimes elsewhere

Some countries go beyond transfer rules. Russia requires that personal data of its citizens be initially stored in Russia. China's PIPL requires a security assessment, a standard contract filing or certification for many outbound transfers, with thresholds that have been adjusted several times. India's DPDP Act permits transfers except to countries the government restricts, while sector regulators impose their own rules — payment data must be stored in India under the central bank's directive. Each of these is its own project with its own counsel. The engineering lesson from them is the same as from the EU: if residency is a property of a tenant and the stack can be stamped out per region, a new jurisdiction is a new cell, not a redesign.

### Residency is a property you test

A residency promise is a claim about hundreds of resources and dozens of vendor settings. It decays exactly like the data map (34.1a), and a customer's auditor will eventually check it. So make it checkable:

- **Infrastructure policy:** every resource tagged for the EU cell lives in an EU region, and backup replication targets stay in the EU (Implementation below).
- **Code:** services obtain region-scoped clients from the tenant's context, so there's no global client to reach for by accident.
- **Egress:** network egress from the EU cell only to allow-listed, in-region endpoints; a new vendor fails closed until someone adds it deliberately.
- **Documentation:** a subprocessor list with locations and transfer mechanisms, generated from the same source as the map.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Enterprise, public-sector, health or financial customers in the EU** make residency a procurement gate. When a quarter of your pipeline is blocked on "is our data in the EU?", a regional data plane (architecture 2) pays for itself.
- **Sensitive data** — employee comments about managers, health information, children's data — raises the bar, and German works councils and similar bodies will scrutinize tools used to assess employees.
- **Customers with regulatory residency duties of their own** (banks, insurers, public bodies) pass their requirements on to you contractually.
- **Localization laws** in markets you choose to enter require in-country storage regardless of what customers ask.
- **Transfer-mechanism risk**: when your EU data flows depend on one mechanism that's under active legal challenge, getting critical flows onto in-region processing or a second mechanism is cheap insurance.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **SMB customers who have never asked.** Contracts (DPF where applicable, SCCs everywhere) are a complete and lawful answer for most of them.
- **A new region for one prospect.** Unless that prospect represents a market you mean to serve, you're building and operating a second stack for one contract. A sovereign or dedicated deployment priced as such is often the honest alternative.
- **Per-country cells inside the EU.** The GDPR treats the EU/EEA as one area. Country-level residency is a niche requirement (some public bodies); don't build it speculatively.
- **A sovereign partition for a small company whose customers only ask about GDPR.** It answers a question nobody is asking at a large cost.
- **Claiming residency you don't have.** Worse than not offering it. A "your data stays in the EU" page contradicted by US-hosted logs is a misrepresentation and a breach of contract waiting to be found in an audit.

## Trade-offs

- **Operations multiply by the number of cells.** Deploys, migrations, on-call, monitoring, capacity planning and incident response, each times N. A migration that fails in one region leaves you with regions on different schema versions.
- **Cost rises faster than traffic.** Every cell carries fixed baseline capacity, its own vendor contracts, and sometimes a premium for sovereign infrastructure — while the smaller regions run at lower utilization.
- **Features lag or differ by region.** Cloud services, AI providers and vendors don't all exist everywhere. You'll ship things "US first" and need a way to say so.
- **Cross-region features get expensive or disappear.** Benchmarks, global search, cross-tenant collaboration all need designing around aggregates or explicit consent.
- **Debugging and incident response slow down** when access is restricted by geography, and the restriction must hold during an outage, when it's most inconvenient.
- **Residency is fragile under change.** One new log field, one new vendor, one well-meaning DR tweak breaks it silently. The ongoing cost is the checks, not the initial build.
- **Residency doesn't settle sovereignty,** and sovereignty offerings don't settle residency for your vendors. Each answer is partial, and the questionnaire asks all four questions.
- **Legal instability is permanent.** Mechanisms change on court timelines, not yours. Designing for replaceable mechanisms costs a little all the time instead of a lot at once.

## Alternatives

**Contracts and assessments only.** DPF where available, SCCs with transfer impact assessments everywhere, documented supplementary measures. Lawful for most processing, cheap, and a fully valid long-term answer for many businesses. What it can't do is make a residency claim.

**Encryption with regional or customer-held keys** for specific flows — backups in another jurisdiction, archived data — where the service never needs plaintext.

**Choose an EU-owned provider** for the EU deployment, or for the whole company if the EU is the core market. It answers sovereignty directly and costs you some services and ecosystem breadth.

**Dedicated or self-hosted deployments** for the few customers who need their own jurisdiction or their own infrastructure, priced accordingly — sometimes run by a local partner. It keeps the main platform simpler at the cost of a support matrix.

**Don't collect it centrally.** Aggregate at the edge or in the region, and only let aggregates travel. Often the best answer for analytics and benchmarks.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Know your flows.** Add `region` and `recipients` to every store in the 34.1a map, list every vendor with its processing location and transfer mechanism, and publish a subprocessor list. *Signal to move on:* the first questionnaire you can't answer truthfully, or a flow with no mechanism at all.

**L1 — Fix the cheap leaks and paper the rest.** Move observability, error tracking and email to vendors' EU sites for EU traffic where offered; strip personal data from logs (19.1); sign SCCs alongside any DPF reliance; write the transfer impact assessments. *Signal:* deals now blocked specifically on storage location.

**L2 — Regional data plane for new tenants.** Tenant directory with home regions, cell-per-region stamping from infrastructure-as-code, regional backups and DR, region-scoped clients in code, residency policy checks in CI, and per-region vendor configuration. Existing EU tenants are migrated deliberately, one at a time. *Signal:* enterprise customers ask who can access their data, not just where it sits.

**L3 — Control access and sovereignty.** Region-scoped support roles, EU-based support for EU tenants, just-in-time access with justification, egress allow-lists, and — only if the customer base demands it — a sovereign partition or EU-owned provider for a separate deployment. *Signal:* public-sector or regulated customers whose own obligations reach your operators.

## Implementation

TypeScript. The core ideas are that region is a property of the tenant, that code can only obtain region-scoped dependencies through the tenant, and that infrastructure is checked against the promise.

### Routing by home region

```ts
// Global directory: routing metadata only. No names, no emails, no survey data.
interface TenantRoute { tenantId: string; region: "eu" | "us"; hostname: string }

app.use(async (req, res, next) => {
  const route = await directory.byHostname(req.hostname);   // acme.eu.example.com
  if (!route) return res.status(404).end();

  if (route.region !== THIS_CELL_REGION) {
    // Never proxy the request body across regions: that would be the very transfer
    // the architecture exists to avoid. Redirect the client to the right cell instead.
    return res.redirect(307, `https://${route.hostname}${req.originalUrl}`);
  }
  req.tenant = await loadTenantContext(route);
  next();
});
```

> **The login seam.** Hostname routing works because the tenant is chosen before anyone types an email. If you also want "enter your email to find your workspace," that lookup needs a global index of email → region. Treat that index as personal data in the control plane, store it hashed, record it in the data map, and have counsel sign off on it — don't let it arrive as a convenience feature nobody reviewed.

### Region-scoped dependencies

```ts
// Each cell builds clients for its own region only. There is no global logger,
// no global DB pool, no default-region SDK client for request handlers to import.
interface RegionalDeps {
  db: Pool;
  blobs: ObjectStore;           // bucket in this region
  search: SearchClient;         // cluster in this region
  log: Logger;                  // ships to the vendor's EU or US site accordingly
  errors: ErrorReporter;        // same
  llm: LlmClient | null;        // null where no in-region processing exists
}

const deps: Record<Region, RegionalDeps> = buildDepsFor(THIS_CELL_REGION);

async function summarizeComments(ctx: TenantContext, surveyId: string) {
  const { llm, db } = deps[ctx.region];
  if (!llm) throw new FeatureUnavailableInRegion("comment-summaries", ctx.region);
  const comments = await loadComments(db, ctx.tenantId, surveyId);
  return llm.summarize(comments);
}
```

> **Why `llm` can be `null`.** This is how feature differences between regions stay honest. The UI hides the feature for EU tenants until an in-region provider exists; the alternative — quietly calling a US endpoint "just for this one feature" — is exactly the arrow in the Problem's diagram. A lint rule banning direct imports of vendor SDKs outside the `deps` builder closes the obvious bypass.

### The promise as a CI check on infrastructure

```ts
// Runs against the infrastructure plan (e.g. `terraform show -json`) for each cell.
const EU_REGIONS = new Set(["eu-central-1", "eu-west-1", "eu-west-3", "eu-north-1"]);

const violations = plan.resources
  .filter(r => r.tags?.residency === "eu")
  .flatMap(r => [
    ...(!EU_REGIONS.has(r.region) ? [`${r.address} is in ${r.region}`] : []),
    ...replicationTargets(r)                         // backup plans, bucket replication,
      .filter(t => !EU_REGIONS.has(t))               // cross-region read replicas
      .map(t => `${r.address} replicates to ${t}`),
  ]);

const untagged = plan.resources.filter(r => holdsData(r) && !r.tags?.residency);

if (violations.length || untagged.length) fail([...violations, ...untagged.map(u => `${u.address} has no residency tag`)]);
```

> **What this doesn't cover.** It checks the infrastructure you declare. It can't see a vendor's dashboard setting, a support agent's location, or a request body inside a log line. Those need the vendor inventory, the access controls and the log hygiene above. Residency is held by several fences, and this is just the one that's easiest to automate.

## Further Reading

- **GDPR Chapter V (Articles 44–50)** — the transfer rules themselves, short and readable.
- **Court of Justice of the EU, _Schrems II_ (C-311/18, 2020)** — why Privacy Shield fell, and why SCCs now need transfer impact assessments.
- **EDPB Recommendations 01/2020 on supplementary measures** (final version, 2021) — the step-by-step method for assessing transfers, with worked use cases on when encryption does and doesn't help.
- **EDPB Guidelines 05/2021 on the interplay between Article 3 and Chapter V** — what counts as a transfer, including remote access and the role of legal entities.
- **Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914** — the current Standard Contractual Clauses and their modules.
- **The EU–US DPF adequacy decision (2023); _Latombe v Commission_ (T-553/23, 2025) and the appeal C-703/25 P** — the current mechanism and its legal status. Check the DPF's status before relying on any summary, including this one.
- **The US CLOUD Act (2018)** — the statute behind the sovereignty question.
- **AWS whitepaper, "Reducing the Scope of Impact with Cell-Based Architecture"** — the cell pattern that regional data planes reuse, written for fault isolation but directly applicable.
- **Atlassian's and Slack's data residency documentation** — useful examples of what vendors actually promise, and especially of the explicit lists of what is _not_ covered by residency.
- **In this collection:** 34.1a (the data map this builds on), 5.1 and 5.4 (tenancy and tenant routing), 5.3 (tenant migration), 19.1 (personal data in logs), 23.5 (service boundaries and cells), 24.2 (multi-region HA and DR), 24.4 (backups and erasure), 38.2 (geographic sharding), 39.8 (third-party AI platforms and their data handling), 34.2b (subprocessor lists and DPAs).
