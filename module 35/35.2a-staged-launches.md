# Staged Launches: Dark Launches, Private Betas and Early Access

_Module 35 · Launch Checklist & Go-Live · 35.2a_

## The Problem

Suppose you make software for restaurants: reservations, table management and a waitlist, used by about 4,000 restaurants. The new product is online ordering. Diners order from the restaurant's own site, and the order flows straight into the restaurant's point-of-sale (POS) system and prints in the kitchen. Restaurants use five different POS vendors, and the integration with each one is different.

The team ran a beta. Twelve restaurants used online ordering for three weeks. They were owners the founders knew personally, all small cafés, and all happened to use the same POS vendor. The beta ran on a separate `beta.` deployment with its own database. Weekly check-in calls were warm: the owners loved it and asked for a few small changes. Sales, to get them on board, told them it would be free for them "for as long as they want it."

Launch was a Tuesday: an email to all 4,000 customers, a blog post and an in-app banner, _"Online ordering is live — turn it on in Settings."_ By Friday 1,100 restaurants had turned it on. Friday dinner went like this:

- **Restaurants on POS vendor C** received kitchen tickets without modifiers. "No onions" and "extra cheese, +$1.50" never reached the kitchen or the bill. The beta had never touched vendor C.
- **Multi-location chains** have menus with 400+ items and location-specific pricing. The ordering page took nine seconds to load on a phone. The beta cafés had menus of about 40 items.
- **A 30-location pizza chain** hit its POS vendor's API rate limit for the account within an hour. Its Friday peak was twenty times higher than any beta restaurant's.
- **Support** had three agents trained on online ordering. Ticket volume was sixty times normal.

The team wanted to switch ordering off for restaurants on vendor C. But the only switch was global: online ordering on or off for everyone. And by now, 1,100 restaurants had put "Order online" buttons on their websites and search listings. Switching it off would break all of those. So they fixed forward through the weekend.

Two months later, billing starts charging the twelve beta restaurants. The "free for as long as they want it" promise had never been written down anywhere the billing system could see.

Nothing here was an exotic failure. The trouble is that the launch really had only one stage. The beta answered one question, _do friendly small cafés on vendor A like this?_, and the answer was yes. The questions that mattered for launch were different. Does it work with every POS? Does it hold at chain-sized menus and volume? Can support cope? Nobody designed a stage to answer those. And making the product available and announcing it to 4,000 customers were the same event, so the first time the system met real variety was also the first time it met real volume.

## What It Is

A **staged launch** exposes a new product or capability to progressively larger and more varied groups of users. Each stage is designed to answer a specific question, and moving to the next stage depends on explicit **exit criteria** being met. The usual stages are:

```
 dark launch ─▶ internal ─▶ private beta ─▶ early access ─▶ general availability ─▶ announcement
 (no users see   (your own     (hand-picked      (opt-in or waitlist,  (anyone can use it)    (marketing)
  it; real        staff)        accounts)         released in waves)
  inputs)
```

The first idea to hold onto is that **making something available and announcing it are separate decisions.** Module 30.2b separated _deploying_ code from _releasing_ it. Staged launches separate one step further: releasing to everyone is still not the same as telling everyone. When a launch is going well, the announcement comes after the product has been quietly available for a while, and it's the least risky step of all.

This is different from the progressive delivery of 30.2b in one important way. A canary or percentage rollout picks users or requests **at random**, to catch technical regressions under real traffic. A staged launch picks **specific accounts deliberately**, to learn whether the product works for the kinds of customers you have, and to give each one a consistent experience and a real onboarding. Both are useful, often together. They answer different questions.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The launch plan has one date, when everyone gets access and the announcement goes out.
- Beta participants were chosen by who said yes, or by sales relationships, not by what they'd test.
- All beta feedback is positive, and there are no metrics beyond what participants say on calls.
- The beta has no end date and no exit criteria. Or it's been "beta" for eighteen months.
- The beta runs on a separate environment, branch or database from production.
- Access is gated by a UI toggle, and the API, webhooks or emails don't check it.
- You can switch the feature off for everyone or no one, but not for a segment.
- What beta participants were promised (pricing, support, data) lives in someone's inbox.
- Nobody knows how many new customers support can onboard in a week.

## How It Works

### Each stage answers a question

The stages above aren't a ritual. Each one is a way of answering a question you can't answer earlier, at the lowest cost it can be answered. When planning, write the question down, and pick the smallest, cheapest audience that can answer it:

| Stage | Question it answers | Typical size |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dark launch** | Does the new code work on real production inputs and load, before anyone depends on it? | Zero visible users; all or some real traffic |
| **Internal** | Does the whole flow work end to end, including support and billing, with forgiving users? | Your staff |
| **Private beta** | Does it work for the _variety_ of customers we have? Does it solve their problem? | Tens of accounts, chosen deliberately |
| **Early access** | Does it hold up at growing scale and volume? Can onboarding and support keep pace? | Hundreds to thousands, in waves |
| **General availability** | Can anyone adopt it without hand-holding? | Everyone |
| **Announcement** | (Not a technical question: a marketing moment, timed after the above.) | Everyone who's listening |

A stage without a question is just a delay. A question without a stage is something you'll find out on launch day.

### Dark launches: real inputs, no consequences

A **dark launch** runs the new code path in production against real inputs, but its output isn't shown to anyone or acted on. The term comes from Facebook's 2008 chat launch. Before the chat UI was visible, users' browsers quietly made the requests the chat feature would make, so the backend absorbed real load for weeks before anyone could chat.

For online ordering, the riskiest computation is turning a menu, a basket and modifiers into a POS order with the right price. Restaurants already take in-person orders on their POS every day. So a dark launch could take each real in-person order, rebuild it through the new online-ordering pricing and POS-translation code, and compare the result to what the POS actually recorded. No diner or kitchen ever sees the output. Within a week, vendor C's missing modifiers would show up as thousands of price mismatches, across every POS vendor and every menu shape, without a single real online order.

There are two ways to feed a dark launch:

- **Shadow the real work** (as above): duplicate existing inputs into the new path and compare outputs. This is best for computations, like pricing, search ranking or recommendations, where there's a "right answer" you can compare against.
- **Synthetic load from real clients** (Facebook's approach): real users' clients generate traffic the new backend handles, with results discarded. This is best for capacity questions.

The discipline a dark launch requires is that the new path must have **no side effects**. It can't write to the POS, send emails, charge cards or mutate shared state. And it still costs real capacity, so it needs its own limits.

### Choosing beta participants: coverage, not friendliness

The single most common beta mistake is choosing participants who are easy to work with. Friendly early customers are valuable for one question, whether the product solves the problem, and misleading for all the others, because they're rarely representative.

Choose private-beta participants the way you'd design a test matrix. List the dimensions along which your customers differ in ways that could break the product, and make sure each meaningful value is covered:

```
                     POS vendor
                A      B      C      D      E
             ┌──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┐
 small café  │ ████ │      │      │      │      │   ████ = the actual beta:
             ├──────┼──────┼──────┼──────┼──────┤          12 restaurants,
 full-service│      │      │      │      │      │          one cell
             ├──────┼──────┼──────┼──────┼──────┤
 multi-      │      │      │      │      │      │
 location    │      │      │      │      │      │
             └──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┘
   ...plus: menu size, modifier complexity, peak order volume, region/tax rules
```

Fifteen cells, at two restaurants each, is thirty restaurants. That's not much more than the twelve the team had, and it would have covered vendor C, multi-location menus and chain-scale volume. Some cells are hard to fill: large chains are cautious, and the rare POS vendor has few customers. Those are exactly the cells worth an incentive to fill, or the cells to fill in the dark launch instead.

It also helps to include a few customers who will complain. The café owner who's a friend of the founder won't tell you the tablet flow is confusing. The operations manager at a demanding chain will, in writing, on day one.

### Getting honest signal from a beta

What beta participants say is a weak signal. People are polite, especially when they know the team, and they report what they notice, not what they silently work around. Rob Fitzpatrick's _The Mom Test_ is about this problem for customer interviews, and the same idea applies here: trust what people do over what they say about the future.

So instrument the beta stage as carefully as production:

- **Behavioral metrics:** orders per restaurant per day, drop-off in the ordering flow, how often staff manually edit orders in the POS (a sign the translation is wrong), how many restaurants turn it off.
- **Operational metrics:** error rates per POS vendor, POS API latencies and rate-limit responses, menu page load times by menu size.
- **Support load:** tickets per active restaurant, and time to first successful order after enabling. That second one is the input to how fast you can go later.
- **Silence as data:** a beta restaurant that stops using the product and says nothing is the most important signal you'll get. Call them.

### Early access in waves

After the private beta, **early access** opens the product to more accounts, usually by opt-in or waitlist, released in **waves**. Wave size isn't a matter of taste. It's set by whichever capacity runs out first:

- **Infrastructure** and third-party limits (like the POS vendors' rate limits).
- **Onboarding:** if each restaurant needs a menu import and a 30-minute setup call, a team of three can onboard about 60 a week, whatever the servers can handle.
- **Support:** tickets per new account in the beta, times the wave size, compared to what the support team can answer.

In the scenario, support was the binding constraint, not the servers. 1,100 restaurants in three days was more than the onboarding and support model could absorb, whatever else had gone right.

A **waitlist** does two jobs at once: it measures demand, and it lets you pace admission. It also creates expectations. Tell people roughly how long they'll wait, admit them in the order they were promised, and remember that interest decays. Someone admitted six months after signing up may have moved on.

A variant from mobile games is the **geographic soft launch**: release fully in one or a few smaller markets first, and treat them as a full-scale rehearsal before the main market. It works when markets are fairly independent and one is a good proxy for the others.

### Exit criteria, written before the stage starts

Each stage gets criteria for moving on, written down before it starts, so the decision doesn't get made by momentum or by a date on the marketing calendar:

```
Private beta → Early access, when:
  • every POS vendor has ≥ 2 beta restaurants with ≥ 2 weeks of live orders
  • price mismatches between order and POS record < 0.1% for every vendor
  • ≤ 1 support ticket per restaurant per week, for 2 consecutive weeks
  • median time from "enable" to first successful order < 1 day
  • menu page p95 load < 2.5 s at the largest beta menu size
```

Also write the criteria that would send you _back_, or pause the rollout: a rising rate of restaurants turning ordering off, mismatches reappearing for a vendor, support backlog over a threshold.

### Beta terms are obligations

Everything you tell beta participants becomes something you owe them. That's fine, as long as it's decided deliberately and recorded where systems can see it. The terms to decide up front:

- **Price after the beta,** and whether any discount is time-limited or permanent. If it's permanent, it's an entitlement in the billing system, not an email.
- **What's not promised:** uptime, support hours, feature stability.
- **What happens to their data** when the beta ends or the product changes. The beta restaurants' order history should survive GA without a migration. That's much easier if the beta ran in production all along (next section).
- **When the beta ends.** A beta with no end date becomes the product's permanent label, and a standing excuse for its rough edges.

### One production path, gated by entitlement

The beta in the scenario ran on a separate deployment with its own database. That means the beta tested a system that wasn't the one that launched, and GA required a data migration on launch day.

The better shape is to run every stage on **the same production system**, with access controlled by **entitlements**: a per-account record saying which stage of which product this account is in. Pete Hodgson calls these _permissioning toggles_ in his classification of feature flags. They're long-lived and per-account, unlike short-lived release toggles.

An entitlement gate has to be checked at **every entry point**, not just the settings screen. For online ordering, that includes the public ordering page, the ordering API, the POS webhook handlers, the emails that confirm orders, and the mobile app. Beta leaks usually come from the entry point nobody gated: the public URL shared on social media, or the API a partner found.

Entitlements also give you the segment-level switch the scenario lacked. Pausing online ordering for accounts on POS vendor C, while leaving everyone else alone, is a query on entitlements plus account attributes.

Exposure also gets harder to reverse over time, in the sense of 35.1b. Removing access from a beta restaurant in week one costs an apology. Removing it after diners have been ordering through its website for a month breaks something the restaurant's customers rely on. So the pause switch needs to be designed for "stop admitting new accounts, and keep existing ones running in a degraded but safe mode" as much as for "off."

## When It's Appropriate

- **New products or major capabilities with lots of customer variety,** like integrations with many third parties, many configurations, or very different customer sizes. Variety is what beta stages exist to explore.
- **B2B products where each account needs onboarding,** since onboarding and support capacity will bound adoption regardless of infrastructure.
- **Features where correctness is hard to check before real inputs arrive,** like pricing, tax, search relevance or integrations. These make good dark launches.
- **Anything hard to take back once customers depend on it,** where you want the first failures to happen with a few dozen accounts who agreed to be early.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Small features on a well-understood path.** A new report column doesn't need a private beta. A flag and a percentage rollout (30.2b) are enough.
- **Consumer launches with network effects or a press moment,** where a slow staged rollout would kill the thing that makes the product work, like a social product where nobody's friends are on it yet. Here a well-rehearsed hard launch (35.1b, 35.2b) may be the right call. You still can, and should, dark launch the backend.
- **Early startups finding product-market fit.** Every customer is effectively a beta customer. What matters is being honest with them about it, not building an entitlement system.
- **Staging for the sake of it.** Five stages with no question attached to each one just delays learning, and teaches the team that the stages are paperwork.

## Trade-offs

- **Slower time to full availability.** A well-run staged launch takes weeks or months to reach GA. Marketing, sales and competitors don't wait, and the pressure to skip stages is constant.
- **Two classes of customers.** Some accounts have a feature and some don't. Support, documentation, sales and billing all need to know which is which, and customers compare notes.
- **Entitlement complexity.** Per-account gates at every entry point are real code that lives for a long time. They need tests, cleanup when the product reaches GA, and care to avoid leaks.
- **Dark launch costs.** Shadowing doubles the work for the shadowed path, needs its own capacity and limits, and requires strict side-effect isolation. Comparisons can also be noisy: you'll spend time explaining differences that aren't bugs.
- **Beta obligations accumulate.** Discounts, special support arrangements and "you were promised this feature" all outlive the beta.
- **Residual selection bias.** Even a well-chosen beta is made of people who opted in. The customers who never read your emails and enable features by accident arrive only at GA.
- **Long betas rot.** A beta that never ends stops producing learning and becomes an excuse.

## Alternatives

**Hard launch.** Everyone gets it at once, with the announcement. It's the simplest to coordinate and can generate the most attention. It's reasonable when the change is low-risk, when network effects or a press moment matter more than learning, or when the product genuinely can't be partially available (a new version of a shared marketplace, say). It puts all the weight on readiness (35.1a), the launch plan (35.1b) and surge handling (35.2b).

**Random percentage rollout only** (30.2b). This is great for catching regressions and capacity problems with real traffic, and it needs no participant selection. It's poor for B2B products where an account needs a consistent experience and onboarding, and it can't target the variety you care about: a 5% random rollout may still include zero restaurants on vendor C.

**Launch quietly and never announce.** Make it available, let it be discovered, and grow by word of mouth. This is underused for features that aren't big enough to announce. The cost is slower adoption, and the benefit is that load arrives gradually.

In practice these combine: dark launch the backend, run a selected beta, open early access in waves with a percentage rollout inside each wave for technical risk, and announce last.

## How to Achieve It

**Step 1: Separate availability from announcement.** Even if nothing else changes, make the product available quietly days before any announcement. This alone turns launch day from a surprise into a checkpoint. _Next-step signal:_ you realize you'd like to limit who gets it during those days.

**Step 2: Gate by entitlement, on production.** Add a per-account entitlement for the product, checked at every entry point, with the ability to query and change it by segment. Run any beta on production behind it, not on a separate environment. _Next-step signal:_ you're about to choose beta participants.

**Step 3: Choose the beta by coverage, and instrument it.** Build the variety matrix, fill the cells, and measure behavior, operations and support load. Write exit criteria before starting, and write down beta terms. _Next-step signal:_ the beta meets its criteria and you need to open further than onboarding can handle at once.

**Step 4: Early access in waves.** Size waves by the tightest of infrastructure, third-party limits, onboarding and support. Keep a pause switch that stops admissions and a segment switch for problems. _Next-step signal:_ the product includes a hard-to-verify computation or an unknown load profile.

**Step 5: Dark launch the risky parts first.** For the next launch, move the riskiest computation into a shadow comparison against real inputs before the beta even starts. It's cheaper than finding the problems with customers.

## Implementation

TypeScript. Three pieces: an entitlement gate, choosing beta participants by coverage, and a shadow comparison for the dark launch.

### Entitlements, checked everywhere

```ts
type Stage = "internal" | "private-beta" | "early-access" | "ga";

interface Entitlement {
  accountId: string;
  product: "online-ordering";
  stage: Stage;
  status: "active" | "paused";   // paused = keep existing orders flowing, no new setup
  grantedAt: Date;
  terms?: { priceOverride?: "free"; until?: Date; note: string }; // beta promises, recorded
}

interface Gate {
  allowed: boolean;
  mode: "full" | "existing-only" | "none";
  reason: string;
}

async function checkOrdering(accountId: string, ctx: "setup" | "diner-order" | "pos-webhook"): Promise<Gate> {
  const rollout = await config.onlineOrdering();           // e.g. { gaOpen: false, pausedVendors: ["C"] }
  const account = await accounts.get(accountId);
  const ent = await entitlements.find(accountId, "online-ordering");

  if (!ent && !rollout.gaOpen) return { allowed: false, mode: "none", reason: "not in any stage" };

  const vendorPaused = rollout.pausedVendors.includes(account.posVendor);
  if (ent?.status === "paused" || vendorPaused) {
    // Existing restaurants keep taking orders; nobody new gets set up.
    return ctx === "setup"
      ? { allowed: false, mode: "existing-only", reason: vendorPaused ? `vendor ${account.posVendor} paused` : "account paused" }
      : { allowed: true, mode: "existing-only", reason: "paused: existing flow only" };
  }
  return { allowed: true, mode: "full", reason: ent ? `stage ${ent.stage}` : "ga" };
}
```

> **Aside: "paused" is not "off."** The scenario's only switch was global off, which would have broken 1,100 restaurants' websites. This gate distinguishes contexts. A paused vendor blocks new setups but keeps accepting diner orders for restaurants already live, because by then those orders are something diners depend on. Whether "keep going" is actually safe for a given failure is a judgment call. If vendor C's orders are wrong, keeping them flowing may be worse than stopping. So the mode is a separate decision per context, not a boolean.

> **Aside: gate every entry point.** `checkOrdering` must run in the public ordering page, the ordering API, POS webhook handlers and the order-confirmation email job, not only in the settings screen. A cheap safeguard is a test that enumerates every route and job tagged `online-ordering` and fails if any doesn't call the gate.

### Choosing the beta by coverage

```ts
interface Candidate {
  accountId: string;
  posVendor: "A" | "B" | "C" | "D" | "E";
  format: "cafe" | "full-service" | "multi-location";
  willing: boolean;
}

function chooseBeta(candidates: Candidate[], perCell = 2): { chosen: Candidate[]; uncovered: string[] } {
  const cellOf = (c: Candidate) => `${c.posVendor}/${c.format}`;
  const byCell = new Map<string, Candidate[]>();
  for (const c of candidates.filter(c => c.willing)) {
    byCell.set(cellOf(c), [...(byCell.get(cellOf(c)) ?? []), c]);
  }

  const allCells = VENDORS.flatMap(v => FORMATS.map(f => `${v}/${f}`));
  const chosen: Candidate[] = [];
  const uncovered: string[] = [];

  for (const cell of allCells) {
    const pool = byCell.get(cell) ?? [];
    chosen.push(...pool.slice(0, perCell));
    if (pool.length < perCell) uncovered.push(`${cell} (${pool.length}/${perCell})`);
  }
  return { chosen, uncovered };
}
```

> **Aside: `uncovered` is the important output.** The list of cells nobody volunteered for is the list of things you'll learn about at GA unless you do something: offer an incentive to fill them, cover them in the dark launch instead, or explicitly accept that risk in the readiness review (35.1a). In the scenario it would have been fourteen of fifteen cells, which is hard to ignore when printed.

### Dark launch: shadow the pricing path

```ts
// Called for every real in-store order the POS reports. Never affects that order.
async function shadowPrice(posOrder: PosOrder): Promise<void> {
  if (!(await shadowBudget.tryAcquire())) return;          // bounded cost: skip when over budget
  try {
    const basket = basketFromPosOrder(posOrder);             // items + modifiers as the diner would pick them
    const translated = await onlineOrdering.priceAndTranslate(basket, {
      dryRun: true,                                           // no writes, no POS calls, no emails
      vendor: posOrder.vendor,
    });
    const diff = compareOrders(posOrder, translated);         // totals, line items, modifiers, tax
    await shadowResults.record({
      vendor: posOrder.vendor,
      menuSize: posOrder.menuSize,
      match: diff.length === 0,
      diff: diff.slice(0, 20),
    });
  } catch (err) {
    await shadowResults.recordError(posOrder.vendor, err);   // errors are findings too
  } finally {
    shadowBudget.release();
  }
}
```

> **Aside: `dryRun` has to be enforced, not trusted.** A flag passed down a call stack is easy for one new function to ignore. Safer is to give the shadow path its own POS client that throws on any write, and a database connection that's read-only. Then a missed `dryRun` check fails loudly instead of sending a phantom order to a kitchen.

> **Aside: the comparison is only as good as its notion of "equal."** Rounding, tax rules and modifier ordering will produce differences that aren't bugs. Expect to spend the first days tuning `compareOrders` so the mismatch rate means something. Then break it down by vendor and menu size, which is the matrix the beta should have covered.

## Further Reading

- **Eugene Letuchy, "Facebook Chat," Facebook Engineering (2008).** The origin of the term "dark launch," and a readable account of loading a backend with real traffic before a feature is visible.
- **Google, _Site Reliability Engineering_ (O'Reilly, 2016), Chapter 27, "Reliable Product Launches at Scale."** Gradual and staged rollouts, feature flags, and dark launches as part of launch practice. Free at sre.google/books.
- **Pete Hodgson, "Feature Toggles (aka Feature Flags)," martinfowler.com.** The classification of toggles by lifetime and dynamism. Entitlements here are his permissioning toggles, and why they're managed differently from release toggles.
- **GitHub, "Scientist: Measure Twice, Cut Over Once" (2016),** and the Scientist library. A well-engineered shadow-comparison pattern for code paths, with the noise and side-effect issues handled.
- **Rob Fitzpatrick, _The Mom Test_ (2013).** Short and practical on why people tell you what you want to hear and how to get signal instead. Aimed at customer interviews, but directly applicable to beta feedback.
- **Marty Cagan, _Inspired_, 2nd edition (Wiley, 2017),** on customer discovery programs and reference customers. How to recruit a small group of customers who are representative rather than merely willing.
- **Within this collection:** Module 26.2 (feature flag management), Module 30.2b (progressive deployment), 35.1a (readiness), 35.1b (reversibility), 35.2b (surges when you do open the doors).

---

The habit to take from this: **before any launch, write down the questions you don't yet know the answers to, and for each one, the smallest group of real users that could answer it.** If the answer to several of them is "everyone, on launch day," the launch has one stage, whatever the plan calls it.
