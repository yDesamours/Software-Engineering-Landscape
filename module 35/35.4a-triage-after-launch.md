# Triage After Launch: Bugs, Feedback and Severity

_Module 35 · Launch Checklist & Go-Live · 35.4a_

## The Problem

Suppose you make field-service software for plumbing and HVAC companies. Dispatchers use a web app to schedule jobs. Technicians use a mobile app in basements and crawl spaces, often offline, to record notes, take photos, collect the customer's signature and create an invoice on the spot. The mobile app syncs when it gets a signal. You launched to 900 companies (about 6,000 technicians) five weeks ago. Hypercare (35.3a) went reasonably well and ended last week. Now the reports keep coming, from everywhere:

- **Support tickets:** about 60 a day, filed by support agents with a priority field. The options are Low, Medium, High and Urgent.
- **In-app feedback:** a "Send feedback" button that posts to a Slack channel.
- **The error tracker:** 2,300 distinct issues, most of them never looked at.
- **App store reviews:** 1,100 so far, averaging 3.4 stars.
- **Customer success managers,** who bring requests from the largest accounts directly to engineers they know.

Here's what the team's handling of that inflow looks like after five weeks.

**The logo.** The owner of the largest customer, 400 technicians, emails the CEO: the company logo on invoice PDFs is slightly cut off. The CEO forwards it with "please prioritize." Two engineers spend most of a week on PDF rendering.

**The notes.** Over the same five weeks, support has received tickets saying "tech's notes disappeared," "photos gone from job," "customer signature missing after sync," and "invoice shows old price." Each one arrived in different words from a different company, was marked Medium, and got the reply "we're looking into it." Nobody connected them. The error tracker has an issue called `SyncConflictError: stale revision` with 9,000 events across 700 devices, also never linked to anything. When two devices edit the same job offline (a technician and their helper, say), the second one to sync silently overwrites the first. Technicians' notes, photos and signatures are being lost, in roughly 3% of multi-technician jobs. Some customers are disputing invoices because the signature proving the work was approved is gone.

**Everything is High.** By week three, 64% of new tickets are marked High or Urgent. Support agents learned in week one that Medium tickets never got a reply from engineering. So High no longer means anything, and engineers read tickets in whatever order they like.

**The meeting.** The team holds a 90-minute weekly triage meeting with eight people. It gets through about 40 tickets. The backlog has 1,400 open items and grows by about 250 a week. Nobody believes the backlog represents anything anymore.

**The mixture.** About a third of "bugs" aren't bugs. "Can't find where to export invoices" is a confusing menu. "Should be able to invoice half a job" is a feature request. "App logged me out" turns out to be a phone setting. They all sit in the same queue at the same priority as the data loss.

**Silence.** Customers who reported a problem never hear back, even when it's fixed. So they report it again, to a different person, in different words.

In week six, a large customer threatens to leave after losing a signed approval on a $14,000 job. The team finally traces the sync bug, finds its 9,000 events, and realizes they'd been receiving reports about it since day four. The fix takes three days.

The team wasn't lazy, and the reports weren't hidden. The problem was that **they were sorting reports by who shouted loudest and how each one was phrased, rather than by what was actually wrong and how many people it hurt.** The most serious bug of the launch came in quietly, in dozens of different words, and was never counted.

## What It Is

**Triage** is the process of turning a stream of reports into decisions. For each incoming report it answers five questions, quickly:

1. **What is it?** A bug, a feature request, a usability problem, a question, a duplicate of something known.
2. **What's actually wrong?** Which underlying problem this report is evidence of, which may already have fifty other reports.
3. **How bad is it?** Its **severity**: the consequence for users and how many are affected. This is a property of the problem, assessed against a rubric.
4. **When will we deal with it?** Its **priority**: a decision about ordering, made by an owner, which takes severity as one input among several.
5. **Who owns it, and what does the reporter hear?**

The word comes from emergency medicine, where the point of triage is that the loudest patient isn't necessarily the sickest one. The same is true of bug reports.

Separating **severity** from **priority** is the core idea. Mozilla's bug process, for example, uses severity for a bug's scope of effect and priority for when it will be worked on, as separate fields set for different reasons. Severity should be the same no matter who reported the problem. Priority can legitimately depend on who's affected, what else is going on, and what it costs to fix, but those reasons should be visible, not hidden in an email from the CEO.

This article is about the inflow: sorting, sizing and routing reports after launch. Module 27 covers the tools for collecting them (help desks, feedback widgets, feature request tracking). How bugs, features and technical debt then compete for the same engineering time is 35.4b.

## Recognizing the Problem

- More than a third of open issues are marked high or urgent.
- Reporters set the severity: the customer, the support agent or the sales rep.
- Requests from big accounts or executives skip the queue with no record of why.
- The same problem appears as many tickets, described differently, and nobody counts them together.
- Error-tracker issues and support tickets live in separate tools with no links between them.
- "Cannot reproduce" is a reason to close a ticket.
- The backlog has grown for weeks and nobody would be surprised or upset if half of it disappeared.
- Feature requests, usability complaints and bugs share one queue and one priority scale.
- A triage meeting with many people is the only place triage happens.
- Customers learn a bug was fixed by noticing, not by being told.

## How It Works

### One intake, many channels

Reports will keep arriving through many channels, and they should. Customers shouldn't need to find the right form. But everything should end up in one place, with its source attached:

```
 support tickets ─┐
 in-app feedback ─┤
 error tracker ───┤                  ┌──▶ bug ──────────────┐
 app store ───────┼──▶ report ──▶ classify ──▶ usability ────────┼──▶ problem record ──▶ severity, priority, owner
 CSM / sales ─────┤   (source,       ├──▶ feature request ──▶ discovery (35.4b)
 internal users ──┘    account,      ├──▶ question ──▶ docs / support
                       evidence)     └──▶ duplicate ──▶ link to existing problem

 security reports ──▶ private channel, separate process (Module 20)
```

Two notes on the diagram. First, the unit of work is a **problem record**, not a ticket. A ticket is one person's report. A problem is the underlying thing that's wrong, and it may have any number of reports, error-tracker issues and affected accounts linked to it. Most triage failures come from treating each report as if it were its own problem. Second, security reports go through a separate, private path, because a public issue describing an unfixed vulnerability is a problem of its own.

### Classify before you size

Before asking how bad something is, ask what it is. The categories need different handling:

| Category | Example | Where it goes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bug** | Notes lost when two devices sync the same job | Problem record, severity rubric |
| **Usability problem** | "Can't find the export button" | Problem record, usually low severity, but count it: many reports mean a design problem |
| **Feature request** | "Invoice part of a job" | Product discovery (35.4b), recorded as the need, not the proposed solution |
| **Question / setup** | "App logged me out" (phone setting) | Support answer; if frequent, a docs or product fix |
| **Duplicate** | Another "notes disappeared" | Link to the existing problem; this adds to its reach |

"Working as designed, but confusing" is a real category and worth taking seriously. The fix might be a label or a help article rather than code, but twenty people confused by the same screen is a product defect, even if no line of code is wrong.

For feature requests, record the problem the customer has, not only the solution they propose. "Add a button to invoice half the job" is one solution. The underlying need, "we get paid in stages on long jobs," may have a better one, and it's the need you'll want to count across customers.

### Severity: a rubric based on consequence and reach

Severity should be set by a rubric that anyone on the triage rotation would apply the same way. Two things determine it: **consequence** (what happens to an affected user) and **reach** (how many are affected, or how central the affected journey is).

| | Few users or a rare path | Many users or a core journey |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Data lost or corrupted; money wrong; security or privacy exposed** | **S1** | **S1** |
| **Blocked, no workaround** | S2 | **S1** |
| **Degraded, with a workaround** | S3 | S2 |
| **Cosmetic, or a minor annoyance** | S4 | S3 |

The top row is the exception that makes the rubric work: **data loss, wrong money and security problems are S1 regardless of reach.** They're usually underreported, because users don't know they've lost data until they need it, and they're usually worse than they look. The notes-overwriting bug belonged there from its first report, whatever the ticket said. The logo, however loudly it was reported, is S4 or S3: cosmetic, with a very limited reach.

Two practical rules make severity honest:

- **The triager sets severity, not the reporter.** Customers and support agents describe what happened; they shouldn't have to argue for a level. Remove the priority field from the support form, or keep it only as "how upset is the customer," which is useful information but a different field.
- **Severity can change as evidence arrives.** A problem starts at S3 with one report, and becomes S2 when thirty more link to it. Reach should be recomputed from the linked evidence, not remembered from the day of the first report.

### Priority: a decision, with reasons

Severity says how bad a problem is. Priority says when you'll deal with it, and it's a decision rather than a lookup. Its usual inputs:

- **Severity**, the starting point.
- **Cost of delay:** does it get worse over time? Data loss accumulates; a cosmetic bug doesn't.
- **Effort and risk of the fix.**
- **Who's affected.** It's legitimate that a problem affecting your largest customer matters more to the business. What matters is that it's recorded as a reason ("account: 400 technicians, renewal in 60 days") rather than happening invisibly through an email to the CEO.
- **Timing:** a cosmetic bug on the pricing page during a marketing campaign may deserve a quick fix.

Each severity level should come with a **response target**, so that lower levels still mean something:

| Severity | Response target |
| --- | --- |
| S1 | Treat as an incident (Module 24.3); work starts now |
| S2 | Fix planned within the current or next sprint |
| S3 | Considered in regular prioritization (35.4b) |
| S4 | May be closed as won't-fix, with a note, if not addressed within 90 days |

When priority differs from what severity would suggest, write down why. That's the check against the logo problem. A week of two engineers on an S4 is sometimes the right call, but "the CEO forwarded it" should be visible as the reason, so everyone can see what it cost.

### Count reach instead of guessing it

The sync bug was invisible because its evidence was scattered: two dozen tickets in different words, a few app store reviews, and an error-tracker issue with 9,000 events. Linking them is most of the work of triage, and it's what reveals reach:

- **Link error-tracker issues to problem records,** and show event counts, affected users and affected accounts on the problem. An error that affects 700 devices is not an S4, whatever its tickets say.
- **Search before creating.** When a report arrives, the triager searches existing problems first. Tools can help here by suggesting similar reports, but a person makes the link.
- **Watch for vague data-loss language.** Words like "disappeared," "missing," "gone," "reverted," "wrong amount" or "old price" in a report should trigger a specific question: could this be data loss or corruption? Users rarely describe data loss accurately; they describe its symptoms.
- **Treat "cannot reproduce" as a state, not a verdict.** Some bugs only happen under specific conditions: offline, two devices, a particular phone. Keep the problem open, gather logs and device details from each new report, and let the count of linked reports rise. Five unreproducible reports of the same symptom are stronger evidence than one reproducible one.

### Severity inflation, and how to stop it

When only the highest level gets attention, everything gets the highest level. That's rational behavior by the people filing reports, not dishonesty, and it can't be fixed by asking people to be more careful. Three things fix it:

1. **Severity is set by the rubric at triage,** not by the reporter.
2. **Every level actually gets handled.** The response targets above have to be met, including for S3 and S4, or at least those have to get an honest answer ("won't fix," "planned for next quarter"). If lower levels mean "ignored," people will keep escalating.
3. **Watch the distribution.** If more than roughly a quarter of open problems are S1 or S2, either the rubric is being applied loosely or the product genuinely has too many serious problems. Both are worth knowing.

### A rotation, not a meeting

Eight people for 90 minutes a week is the most expensive way to triage, and it's too slow for anything urgent. A **triage rotation** works better: each week, one engineer and one person from support or product own first-pass triage, within one business day of a report arriving. Most items take two or three minutes: classify, link or create a problem, apply the rubric, route. S1 candidates go straight to on-call.

A short weekly review then covers only what needs discussion: disputed severities, S2s without an owner, problems whose reach is climbing, and a look at triage health. Rotating the role spreads knowledge of what customers are actually experiencing across the team, which has value of its own.

### Backlog hygiene: a backlog is not an archive

A 1,400-item backlog that grows every week is not a list of work anyone will do. It's an archive that costs time every time someone searches it. Keep it honest:

- **Close old low-severity items explicitly.** An S4 with no new reports for 90 days gets closed as "won't fix for now," with a note, and reopens automatically if new reports link to it. Closing isn't forgetting; the problem record keeps its evidence.
- **Declare backlog bankruptcy when needed.** If the backlog is already unmanageable, close everything older than a set date that isn't S1 or S2, and let anything real come back through new reports. It feels drastic and is usually fine: problems that matter get reported again.
- **Keep the open count bounded.** If the number of open S3s keeps rising, the team is producing problems faster than it resolves them. That's a prioritization question (35.4b), not a triage one, but triage is where it becomes visible.

### Close the loop with reporters

Every report linked to a problem is a person waiting for an answer. When the problem is fixed, or deliberately won't be fixed, tell them. Linking reports to problem records makes this almost free: one status change notifies everyone who reported it, through support. It builds trust, it reduces repeat reports, and "we fixed the sync issue you reported" is one of the best messages a customer success manager can send.

### Measuring triage itself

A few numbers tell you whether triage is working:

- **Time to first triage:** how long a report waits before someone classifies it.
- **Time to fix by severity,** compared with the response targets.
- **Severity distribution** of open problems.
- **Reports per problem:** a high number for a problem nobody's working on is a warning.
- **Reopen rate:** fixes that didn't fix it, or duplicates that weren't.

## When It's Appropriate

- **After launch,** when reports arrive faster than one person can hold in their head: roughly more than a few dozen a week, or from more than two or three channels.
- **Products where some bugs are much worse than others,** such as anything involving money, customer data, offline sync, or legally significant records like signatures and invoices.
- **B2B products with account managers and executives in the loop,** where there's pressure to prioritize by relationship and the reasons need to be visible.
- **Teams where support and engineering are different people,** since triage is where the two meet.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Very small products or teams.** Five reports a week can live in a shared list that the team reads together. A rubric in someone's head is enough.
- **Internal tools** with a handful of users who can simply message the developer.
- **Automating early.** Machine clustering of reports, automatic severity scoring and dashboards are worth it at hundreds of reports a day. At sixty a day, a person on rotation with a search box does better, and learns about the product in the process.
- **Rubrics with too many levels or dimensions.** Five-by-five matrices with weightings produce arguments, not decisions. Four levels and two dimensions cover almost everything.

## Trade-offs

- **The rotation costs engineering time.** First-pass triage takes one engineer perhaps an hour or two a day during busy periods, and the context switching is real. It usually saves more than it costs, by stopping engineers from investigating duplicates and misfiled requests, but it's visible time.
- **Rubrics simplify.** Some problems don't fit the grid, and triagers will disagree about edge cases. The rubric's job is to make most decisions quick and consistent, not to remove judgment.
- **Linking can be wrong.** Merging two different bugs into one problem hides one of them. Keep links reversible and ask reporters to confirm when unsure.
- **Closing old items upsets people.** Someone's pet issue gets closed. The honest note and automatic reopening help, but not entirely.
- **Weighting by account is uncomfortable.** Making "this affects our biggest customer" an explicit reason is more honest than hiding it, but it also puts an uncomfortable trade-off in writing. That's usually a good thing.
- **Response targets create pressure to downgrade.** If S2s have to be fixed within two sprints, there's a temptation to call them S3. Watching the distribution and occasionally reviewing downgrades is the check.

## Alternatives

**Zero-bug policy.** Some teams refuse to keep a bug backlog at all: every bug is either fixed now or closed as won't-fix. It keeps the list honest and forces real decisions. It works best for small teams with a mature product and moderate inflow. Right after a launch, with reports arriving faster than they can be fixed, it usually isn't realistic.

**Fix-it weeks and bug bashes.** Setting aside periodic time for the team to fix accumulated problems. It's a good way to reduce a backlog and improve morale, but it's a supplement to triage, not a replacement: someone still has to decide what's in the queue and how bad it is.

**Let support own priority.** In some organizations, support leads decide which customer problems engineering works on next. Support has the best view of customer pain, but a weaker view of fix cost and of problems that surface through telemetry instead of tickets, like the error tracker's 9,000 events. A pair from support and engineering on the rotation combines both.

**Error budgets for reliability work.** Service-level objectives (Module 19) can decide when reliability problems take precedence over feature work, automatically. They cover availability and latency well, but most user-reported bugs are about correctness and usability, which SLOs rarely measure.

## How to Achieve It

**Step 1: One queue, one rubric.** Route every channel into one place, classify reports before sizing them, and adopt a four-level severity rubric with the data/money/security override. Remove severity from reporters' forms. _Next-step signal:_ you notice the same problem arriving under different names.

**Step 2: Problem records and a rotation.** Link reports and error-tracker issues to problem records, and compute reach from the linked evidence. Start a weekly rotation for first-pass triage, with a short weekly review. _Next-step signal:_ the backlog keeps growing and reporters keep asking for updates.

**Step 3: Response targets, hygiene, and closing the loop.** Set response targets by severity, expire old S4s with reopen-on-new-report, and notify reporters when problems are fixed or closed. _Next-step signal:_ inflow is in the hundreds a day, or linking takes most of the rotation's time.

**Step 4: Tooling.** Automatic links between the error tracker and problem records, suggested duplicates, keyword flags for possible data loss, and a triage-health dashboard.

## Implementation

TypeScript. Three pieces: the severity rubric as a function, problem records whose severity is recomputed as evidence links to them, and a backlog-hygiene job.

### The rubric, including its overrides

```ts
type Consequence = "data-loss" | "money-wrong" | "security" | "blocked" | "degraded" | "cosmetic";
type Severity = "S1" | "S2" | "S3" | "S4";

interface Reach {
  accounts: number;          // distinct affected accounts
  users: number;             // distinct affected users or devices
  coreJourney: boolean;      // e.g. complete job, collect signature, invoice
}

interface SeverityResult { severity: Severity; why: string; }

const WIDE_REACH = { accountsFraction: 0.02, users: 100 };

function assessSeverity(consequence: Consequence, reach: Reach, totalAccounts: number): SeverityResult {
  if (consequence === "data-loss" || consequence === "money-wrong" || consequence === "security")
    return { severity: "S1", why: `${consequence}: S1 regardless of reach` };

  const wide =
    reach.coreJourney ||
    reach.accounts / totalAccounts >= WIDE_REACH.accountsFraction ||
    reach.users >= WIDE_REACH.users;
  const scope = wide ? "wide" : "narrow";

  const grid: Record<Exclude<Consequence, "data-loss" | "money-wrong" | "security">, [Severity, Severity]> = {
    blocked:  ["S2", "S1"],
    degraded: ["S3", "S2"],
    cosmetic: ["S4", "S3"],
  };
  const severity = grid[consequence][wide ? 1 : 0];
  return {
    severity,
    why: `${consequence}, ${scope} reach (${reach.accounts} accounts, ${reach.users} users${reach.coreJourney ? ", core journey" : ""})`,
  };
}
```

> **Aside: the override row is the point.** Without it, the sync bug's early reach (a few accounts, a few dozen devices) would have put it at S3 for weeks, which is exactly what happened in practice. Data loss is almost always under-counted, because users only notice when they need the lost data. So the rubric treats the consequence alone as enough. Who reported it doesn't appear anywhere in this function. Account importance belongs in priority, as a written reason, not in severity.

### Problem records that re-evaluate as evidence arrives

```ts
interface Report {
  id: string;
  source: "support" | "in-app" | "app-store" | "csm" | "internal";
  accountId?: string;
  userId?: string;
  text: string;
  receivedAt: Date;
}

interface ProblemRecord {
  id: string;
  title: string;
  consequence: Consequence;
  coreJourney: boolean;
  reportIds: string[];
  errorIssueIds: string[];   // linked error-tracker issues
  severity: Severity;
  severityWhy: string;
  priority?: { level: "now" | "next" | "later" | "wont-fix"; reason: string; decidedBy: string };
  status: "open" | "fixed" | "wont-fix";
}

async function linkReport(problem: ProblemRecord, report: Report): Promise<ProblemRecord> {
  const updated = { ...problem, reportIds: [...new Set([...problem.reportIds, report.id])] };
  return reassess(updated);
}

async function reassess(problem: ProblemRecord): Promise<ProblemRecord> {
  const reports = await reportsStore.getMany(problem.reportIds);
  const errorImpact = await errorTracker.impact(problem.errorIssueIds); // { accountIds, userIds }

  const reach: Reach = {
    accounts: new Set([...reports.flatMap(r => (r.accountId ? [r.accountId] : [])), ...errorImpact.accountIds]).size,
    users: new Set([...reports.flatMap(r => (r.userId ? [r.userId] : [])), ...errorImpact.userIds]).size,
    coreJourney: problem.coreJourney,
  };

  const { severity, why } = assessSeverity(problem.consequence, reach, await accountsStore.count());
  if (rank(severity) < rank(problem.severity)) {           // got worse
    await notifyTriage(`${problem.id} escalated ${problem.severity} → ${severity}: ${why}`);
    if (severity === "S1") await pageOnCall(problem);
  }
  return { ...problem, severity, severityWhy: why };
}

const rank = (s: Severity) => ["S1", "S2", "S3", "S4"].indexOf(s);
```

> **Aside: reach comes from evidence, not memory.** Each linked report and each linked error-tracker issue adds its accounts and users to the problem's reach, and the severity is recomputed every time. When the error tracker's 9,000 events across 700 devices are linked, the sync bug's reach jumps and triage is notified. That's why linking matters more than any other triage step: the counts only exist if the evidence is connected. `reassess` only raises the alarm when severity gets worse. A downgrade is a human decision, recorded with a reason.

A small helper can suggest when a report might describe data loss, even if it's worded as something mild. It suggests; it doesn't decide:

```ts
const DATA_LOSS_HINTS = /\b(disappear\w*|missing|gone|lost|revert\w*|overwr\w*|old (price|version)|wrong (amount|total))\b/i;

function triageHints(report: Report): string[] {
  const hints: string[] = [];
  if (DATA_LOSS_HINTS.test(report.text))
    hints.push("Possible data loss or corruption: check before assigning severity below S1");
  return hints;
}
```

### Backlog hygiene: expire S4s, reopen on new evidence, tell reporters

```ts
async function expireStaleLowSeverity(now = new Date()) {
  const stale = await problems.find({
    status: "open",
    severity: "S4",
    lastReportBefore: daysAgo(now, 90),
  });

  for (const p of stale) {
    await problems.update(p.id, {
      status: "wont-fix",
      priority: { level: "wont-fix", reason: "S4 with no new reports in 90 days; reopens on new report", decidedBy: "backlog-policy" },
    });
    await notifyReporters(p, "We've decided not to change this for now. If it keeps affecting you, reply and we'll reopen it.");
  }
}

// When a new report links to a closed problem, reopen it rather than filing a new one.
reportsStore.onLinked(async (report, problem) => {
  if (problem.status === "wont-fix") {
    await problems.update(problem.id, { status: "open" });
    await notifyTriage(`${problem.id} reopened by a new report (${report.source})`);
  }
});
```

> **Aside: closing is a message, not a deletion.** Expired problems keep their evidence and reopen automatically if someone reports them again, so the policy loses almost nothing that matters. `notifyReporters` goes through support, not an automated email from a no-reply address. A person telling a customer "we've decided not to change this" is honest; silence is not. The same function sends "fixed" messages when a problem's status changes to fixed, which is how the loop gets closed for every linked reporter at once.

## Further Reading

- **Mozilla, "Triage for Bugzilla" and "Defect Severity" (Firefox Source Docs).** A large project's working triage process, with severity describing a bug's scope of effect and priority describing when it gets worked on, kept as separate fields.
- **Google Issue Tracker documentation, "Issues."** A clear set of priority definitions (P0–P4) framed by how urgently each needs to be addressed, useful as a starting point for your own response targets.
- **Joel Spolsky, "Painless Bug Tracking," _Joel on Software_ (2000).** Old and short, still one of the best descriptions of what a useful bug report contains and why every bug needs one owner at a time.
- **Teresa Torres, _Continuous Discovery Habits_ (Product Talk, 2021).** On turning customer requests into underlying needs ("opportunities"), which is how feature requests should leave the triage queue.
- **Your error tracker's documentation on issue grouping and linking to external tickets** (Sentry, Bugsnag, Rollbar and others all support this). The mechanical basis for counting reach from telemetry.
