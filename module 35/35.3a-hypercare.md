# Hypercare: Watching a System That Has No Baseline Yet

_Module 35 · Launch Checklist & Go-Live · 35.3a_

## The Problem

Suppose you've launched a rent-payment product for small landlords. Tenants pay rent in the app, by bank transfer or card. Landlords see who's paid and get payouts. Reminders go out three days before rent is due, and late fees are applied automatically according to each lease's terms. Launch was on the 12th of the month: 3,000 landlords and about 9,000 tenants moved over from checks and spreadsheets.

The team runs a "launch watch" for the first ten days. Engineers take turns watching dashboards, a channel collects anything unusual, and there's a quick sync each morning. Error rates are low, latency is fine, the payment dashboard shows a 99.2% success rate, and support tickets are manageable. On the 22nd the team declares the launch a success and goes back to roadmap work.

Then the calendar turns.

**The 28th.** Rent reminders should go out to every tenant with rent due on the 1st, which is nearly all of them. None do. The reminder job has run daily since launch, but until the 28th there was almost nobody with rent due in three days, so its runs had processed a handful of leases, or none. On its first big day, it fails on a time zone boundary in its date query and processes zero. Zero processed is not an error. No alert fires.

**The 1st.** About 85% of the month's payments arrive within 36 hours, forty times the launch week's volume. The system handles the load. The dashboard shows a 99.1% success rate. That number counts payments _initiated_ successfully. Bank transfers don't settle for a few business days, and returns (insufficient funds, closed accounts) come back after that.

**The 3rd to the 5th.** Returns arrive. About 4% of bank payments fail. Is that high? Nobody knows. There's no previous month to compare it to, and nobody wrote down what to expect.

**The 6th.** The late-fee job runs for the first time with real work to do. It treats any lease without a _settled_ payment as unpaid, and charges late fees to 400 tenants whose payments were still in transit. Landlords get angry messages from their tenants. Several tenants had also forgotten to pay because they never got a reminder.

Looking back, the signs were there. In launch week, a handful of support tickets asked why bank payments were "pending for four days," but each was answered individually and nobody counted them. And the whole launch watch had covered the part of the month in which the system did almost nothing. **The riskiest things the system does — the monthly peak, reminders at scale, settlement, returns, the late-fee run — all happened for the first time after the team had stopped watching.**

## What It Is

**Hypercare** is a period of heightened attention right after launch, lasting until the system has proven itself through its real operating cycles. The term comes from enterprise software go-lives, where a new ERP isn't considered stable until it has survived a month-end close. It combines extra staffing, a closer loop between support and engineering, and monitoring designed for a system with no history.

That last part is the technical core. Ordinary monitoring (Module 19) relies on a baseline. You alert when this Tuesday looks different from recent Tuesdays. After launch there are no previous Tuesdays. So hypercare has to replace the baseline with other things: a **calendar of firsts** that tells you when to look, **written predictions** that tell you what to expect, **outcome and absence checks** that catch failures that don't produce errors, and **invariants** that catch the system doing the wrong thing successfully.

Hypercare isn't incident response (Module 24.3), though incidents will happen during it. And it isn't the launch-day roles of 35.1b, which cover the hours around the cutover. Hypercare covers the days and weeks after.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The post-launch watch ends on a date ("two weeks") rather than after specific events have happened.
- The system has monthly, weekly or yearly behavior (billing, renewals, month-end, payroll, reports), and it hasn't gone through a full cycle yet.
- Nobody can say what number to expect for key metrics, so nobody can tell whether 4% is bad.
- Dashboards show technical health (errors, latency) but not business outcomes (payments settled, orders delivered).
- Outcomes are measured at the moment an action starts, not when its result is actually known.
- Scheduled jobs report errors but not how much work they did.
- Support tickets are answered one by one, and nobody counts themes.
- Alerts for the new system are either copied from another system or absent, and the team isn't sure what thresholds would make sense.

## How It Works

### Build the calendar of firsts

A launched system hasn't really been tested until each of its cycles has run once for real. So before launch, list everything that will happen for the first time, and when:

```
Launch ─── day 1 ──────── day 16 ──── day 19 ─── day 20 ─── day 22-24 ── day 25 ─── day 40 ──── ...
(12th)                    (28th)      (1st)      (2nd)      (3rd-5th)    (6th)      (22nd)
  │                         │           │          │           │            │           │
  first logins,             first big   first      first       first        first      first
  first payments            reminder    monthly    payout      returns      late-fee   card
  (small numbers)           run         peak       batch       arrive       run        disputes
                                                                                        arrive
  ◄──────── the original launch watch ───────►
```

Things that commonly belong on this calendar:

- **Cycles:** the first peak day, first billing or renewal run, first month-end, first quarterly or annual job.
- **Delayed outcomes:** settlements, returns, chargebacks, deliveries, refunds — anything whose result arrives days after the action.
- **Scheduled jobs at real volume:** reminders, digests, cleanups, retention purges. They often run from day one but do negligible work at first.
- **Expiries:** trial ends, tokens and certificates, promotional pricing, cached data with long lifetimes.
- **Calendar events:** month lengths, daylight saving time changes, holidays that shift bank days.

Hypercare then ends when the critical items on this calendar have happened and been verified, not after a fixed number of days. For the rent product that meant at least one full month, and realistically two, because the second month tests things the first can't: tenants who paid late last month, leases with changes, and the late-fee job with realistic data.

One way to shorten this is to run the calendar _before_ launch. With a controllable clock in a production-like environment, you can fast-forward through a month-end and watch the reminder, settlement and late-fee jobs do their work. That's covered under Alternatives. It doesn't remove the need for hypercare, but it moves some of the surprises earlier.

### Write predictions: they're your baseline

With no history, the only thing you can compare real numbers to is what you expected. So before launch, write down expected ranges for the metrics that matter, with a sentence on where each estimate comes from:

| Metric | Expected | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reminders sent on the 28th | ≈ tenants with rent due on the 1st (~8,500) | Lease data |
| Bank payment returns | 1–5% of bank payments | Payment provider's guidance for similar merchants |
| Card decline rate | 3–8% | Provider's guidance |
| Share of rent paid by the 3rd | 70–90% | Landlord survey, old check data |
| Support tickets per landlord, week 1 | 0.1–0.3 | Beta |

Predictions don't need to be accurate to be useful. Their job is to turn every number into a question. A number inside the range is probably fine. A number outside it is worth an explanation, in _either_ direction. Surprisingly good numbers are often broken instrumentation. Zero failed logins in a day usually means failed logins aren't being recorded.

### Watch outcomes, and measure them when they're known

The launch watch looked at whether requests succeeded. What the business needs to know is whether rent got paid. These can diverge for days. A payment can be initiated successfully, then return a week later.

So measure outcomes at the point they're actually known, and follow each group of actions through to its result. Group the payments by the day they were initiated, and watch what happens to that group over the following days:

```
Payments initiated on the 1st (bank transfers, n = 5,200)
                     day 0    day 1    day 2    day 3    day 4    day 5
initiated            100%
pending               99%      96%      61%      18%       6%       2%
settled                        3%       35%      78%      89%      94%
returned                                3%       3.5%     4.1%     4.2%   ← compare to prediction: 1–5%
```

Reading the table this way answers questions that a single "success rate" hides. How long does settlement actually take? That's what the "pending for four days" tickets were about. Are returns within range? When is it safe to treat a payment as final? That last question is exactly the one the late-fee job got wrong.

### Detect absence

The reminder failure produced no errors. Nothing happened, and systems don't emit events for things that didn't happen. Most monitoring is built to notice bad events, so it's blind to missing ones.

Two techniques catch absence:

- **Heartbeats for scheduled work.** Every scheduled job reports that it completed, when, and how much it did. A separate checker alerts when an expected report doesn't arrive (sometimes called a dead man's switch). This catches jobs that don't run at all.
- **Expected counts.** For jobs whose workload can be computed independently, compare what the job did with what it should have done. On the 28th, the leases table says roughly 8,500 tenants have rent due in three days. The reminder job reports zero. That comparison is the whole check, and it catches the job that runs, succeeds and does nothing.

The expected count should come from a different query than the one the job itself uses. Otherwise both will share the same bug and happily agree.

### Check invariants: correctness, not performance

Some failures aren't about the system being slow or down, but about it doing the wrong thing successfully. The late-fee job ran on time, without errors, and charged 400 people it shouldn't have. For these, write down statements that must always be true, and check them continuously:

- No tenant has a late fee while they have a payment pending for that period.
- Every settled payment has exactly one payout to the landlord.
- Payouts equal settled payments minus fees, per landlord per period.
- No reminder is sent to a tenant who has already paid.

These invariants are cheap queries. During hypercare, run them every few minutes, and especially right after the jobs they concern. After hypercare, most of them are worth keeping forever.

### Alerting when you have no history

Static thresholds for a new system tend to fail in both directions. Set them tight and they page constantly about the normal noise of a new product; set them loose and they miss real problems. During hypercare, a different arrangement works better:

- **Page only on clear, user-visible failure:** journeys failing outright, invariants violated, expected jobs missing. These don't need a baseline, because they're wrong at any volume. Where you have explicit service-level objectives (Module 19), their burn-rate alerts work from the start too, because the target is chosen rather than learned.
- **Send everything else to a watch channel** that humans review daily: metrics outside predicted ranges, unusual ratios, new error types. A person looking at "returns 4.2%, predicted 1–5%, provider says typical is 2–3%" makes a better decision than a threshold would.
- **Compare segments against each other,** not against history. If one bank's returns are three times the others', or one landlord's tenants all fail, that's a signal that doesn't need a baseline.
- **Graduate signals into real alerts** as history accumulates. After two or three cycles, you know what a normal 1st of the month looks like and can alert on deviations from it.

### The human side: rota, daily review, support loop

Hypercare also changes how people work for a while:

- **A named rota** with a short escalation path, including someone who understands the business outcome, not only the infrastructure. For payments, that includes someone who can explain what a return code means.
- **A daily review, short and fixed:** metrics against predictions, new clusters in support tickets, open issues, and what's coming up next on the calendar of firsts. "Tomorrow is the first late-fee run. Who's watching it, and what invariant tells us it worked?"
- **Support tickets as telemetry.** Tag every ticket with a theme and count them daily. Three tickets on the same theme within a couple of days is a pattern, not three customers. The "pending for four days" tickets would have become a question on day 3 instead of an explanation after the 6th.
- **Reach out before customers do.** Some signals point at specific customers: a landlord whose tenants have all failed payment, an account with no activity since migrating. Contacting them first often turns a complaint into goodwill, and it's how you learn about problems your metrics can't see.

### Ending hypercare: exit criteria

Like the launch stages in 35.2a, hypercare ends on criteria, written in advance:

- Every critical item on the calendar of firsts has happened and been verified by its invariants.
- Key metrics have stayed within predicted (or revised) ranges for at least one full cycle, better two.
- Alerts have been set from real data, and the watch channel's recurring items have either become alerts or been dismissed.
- Ticket volume per customer is down to the level support planned for.
- Runbooks have been updated with what the team learned, and ownership has moved to normal on-call.
- Every open issue has an owner in normal triage (35.4a).

Then hold a short retrospective, and feed its findings into the readiness checklist (35.1a) for next time. The late-fee lesson becomes a readiness claim: _"Every job that acts on payment status has been run against payments in every state, including pending."_

## When It's Appropriate

- **New products and platform migrations,** especially after a cutover (35.1b), when every number is new and the team's intuitions come from the old system.
- **Systems with cycles:** billing, subscriptions, payroll, rent, invoicing, reporting, anything with a month-end or renewal date. The first cycle is the real launch.
- **Delayed outcomes:** payments, deliveries, shipments, background checks, anything whose success is only known days after the action.
- **High-stakes correctness,** like money, compliance or healthcare, where a wrong result is worse than a slow one.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Features that closely resemble existing ones,** using the same paths and metrics, launched behind progressive delivery (30.2b). The existing baseline already works, and a comparison against the control group is better than predictions.
- **Stateless or cosmetic changes** with no cycles and no delayed outcomes. A day of attention is enough.
- **Hypercare that never ends.** If a "launch watch" channel is still going six months later, it's substituting for alerting and ownership that should exist. The exit criteria are what stop this.

## Trade-offs

- **People's time.** A rota and daily reviews over a month or two take real capacity from the next project, and the people best suited to hypercare are usually the ones most wanted elsewhere.
- **Attention is finite.** A watch channel full of "outside predicted range" items becomes noise quickly. Predictions that are too narrow, or too many metrics, produce the same fatigue that static alerts do.
- **Predictions can anchor.** Once a range is written down, a number inside it tends to be accepted without thought, even when it's the wrong metric. The 99.2% initiation success rate would have been "inside predictions" too.
- **Invariants and expected counts are code.** They need maintaining as the product changes, and a stale invariant produces either false alarms or false comfort.
- **Proactive outreach exposes problems.** Contacting customers first means telling them something went wrong before they noticed. This is usually the right call, but it's a real decision, and support and account teams need to agree on it.

## Alternatives

**Run the calendar before launch.** With a controllable clock (an injected clock in code, or a time-shifted environment), you can simulate a month in hours: create leases, advance to the 28th, check reminders, advance to the 1st, simulate settlements and returns, advance to the 6th, check late fees. This finds the logic errors, like the pending-payment late fee, before any real tenant sees them. It can't find what depends on real-world behavior: actual return rates, how long real banks take, what real tenants do. It shrinks hypercare; it doesn't replace it.

**Rely on standard observability and progressive delivery.** Where a meaningful control group exists, comparing the new version against the old (30.2b) gives you a baseline on day one. That's the best case, and for changes to existing products it's usually enough. For a genuinely new product there's no control group.

**Extended beta instead of hypercare.** Keeping the product in a small beta (35.2a) through a full cycle before general availability means the first month-end happens with thirty landlords, not three thousand. That's often the best choice for products with long cycles, at the cost of slower launch.

## How to Achieve It

**Step 1: Calendar, predictions, daily review.** Before launch, write the calendar of firsts and a one-page prediction table. After launch, hold a fifteen-minute daily review against them, and tag support tickets by theme. This costs very little and would have caught the scenario's problems a few days earlier. _Next-step signal:_ you realize some failures wouldn't show up in any of the numbers you're looking at.

**Step 2: Outcome and absence checks.** Make scheduled jobs report their workload, and compare it to independently computed expectations. Measure outcomes when they're known, following each group of actions through to its result. _Next-step signal:_ the system's riskiest failures are wrong results, not missing ones.

**Step 3: Invariants.** Write down what must always be true about the system's state, and check it continuously, especially right after the jobs that change it.

**Step 4: Graduate and hand over.** As cycles complete, turn watch-channel items into real alerts with thresholds from real data, and end hypercare against the exit criteria.

## Implementation

TypeScript. Three pieces: predictions as data, an expected-count check for scheduled jobs, and an invariant check.

### Predictions as data, reviewed daily

```ts
interface Prediction {
  metric: string;
  low: number;
  high: number;
  basis: string;                     // where the estimate came from
  query: () => Promise<number | null>; // null = not yet measurable (e.g. returns before the 3rd)
}

type Finding = { metric: string; value: number; low: number; high: number; direction: "low" | "high" };

async function dailyReview(predictions: Prediction[]): Promise<{ findings: Finding[]; unmeasured: string[] }> {
  const findings: Finding[] = [];
  const unmeasured: string[] = [];
  for (const p of predictions) {
    const value = await p.query();
    if (value === null) { unmeasured.push(p.metric); continue; }
    if (value < p.low) findings.push({ metric: p.metric, value, low: p.low, high: p.high, direction: "low" });
    if (value > p.high) findings.push({ metric: p.metric, value, low: p.low, high: p.high, direction: "high" });
  }
  return { findings, unmeasured };   // posted to the watch channel, not paged
}
```

> **Aside: "low" is a finding too.** Reviewers tend to skim past good news, so the report treats both directions the same way. A card decline rate of 0.2%, predicted 3–8%, is more likely to mean declines aren't being recorded than that your customers have unusually good cards. The `unmeasured` list is also worth reading. A metric that's still unmeasurable a week after the data should exist is itself a finding.

### Expected counts for scheduled jobs

```ts
interface JobRun { job: string; ranAt: Date; processed: number; }

interface ExpectedWork {
  job: string;
  schedule: "daily";
  // Computed independently of the job's own query, so they can't share a bug.
  expected: (day: Date) => Promise<number>;
  tolerance: number;   // fraction; e.g. 0.05 = within 5%
}

const reminders: ExpectedWork = {
  job: "rent-reminders",
  schedule: "daily",
  expected: async (day) => {
    // Rent due three days after `day`, in each property's local time zone,
    // counted directly from leases — not by calling the reminder job's selector.
    return db.count(
      `SELECT count(*) FROM leases l JOIN properties p ON p.id = l.property_id
       WHERE l.status = 'active'
         AND l.due_day = extract(day FROM ($1::timestamptz AT TIME ZONE p.timezone) + interval '3 days')`,
      [day],
    );
  },
  tolerance: 0.05,
};

async function checkExpectedWork(spec: ExpectedWork, day: Date): Promise<string | null> {
  const run: JobRun | undefined = await jobRuns.latest(spec.job, { on: day });
  const expected = await spec.expected(day);
  if (!run) return `${spec.job}: no completion report for ${day.toDateString()} (expected ~${expected})`;
  const allowed = Math.max(1, expected * spec.tolerance);
  if (Math.abs(run.processed - expected) > allowed)
    return `${spec.job}: processed ${run.processed}, expected ~${expected}`;
  return null;
}
```

> **Aside: this catches both halves of the reminder failure.** If the job doesn't run, there's no completion report. If it runs and does nothing, `processed: 0` against `expected: 8,500` fails. On the launch week's quiet days, the expected count was also near zero, so the check passed, correctly, and the first real test came on the 28th. That's the calendar of firsts in action: the check was there all along, but it only mattered on the day that was already marked as important. Note also that `expected` computes the date in each property's own time zone, independently of however the job does it. If both used the same helper, they'd agree on the same wrong answer.

### An invariant: no late fee while a payment is in flight

```ts
async function lateFeeWhilePending(): Promise<string[]> {
  const rows = await db.query<{ lease_id: string; period: string; payment_id: string }>(
    `SELECT f.lease_id, f.period, p.id AS payment_id
     FROM late_fees f
     JOIN payments p ON p.lease_id = f.lease_id AND p.period = f.period
     WHERE f.status = 'charged'
       AND p.status IN ('initiated', 'pending')
       AND p.initiated_at <= f.charged_at`,
  );
  return rows.map(r => `lease ${r.lease_id} (${r.period}): late fee charged while payment ${r.payment_id} was in flight`);
}

// During hypercare: run every 10 minutes, and immediately after the late-fee job finishes.
scheduler.every(minutes(10), () => runInvariant("late-fee-while-pending", lateFeeWhilePending));
jobs.onComplete("late-fees", () => runInvariant("late-fee-while-pending", lateFeeWhilePending));
```

> **Aside: invariants page, predictions don't.** A late fee on a pending payment is wrong at any volume, on any day. It doesn't need a baseline, so it's safe to page on from the first minute. That's the difference between the two kinds of checks. Predictions describe what's _likely_, and a human decides what a deviation means. Invariants describe what's _required_, and a violation is a bug. Running the check right after the job completes means the first run on the 6th would have been caught within minutes, before most tenants had seen a charge, rather than by angry landlords.

## Further Reading

- **Google, _Site Reliability Engineering_ (O'Reilly, 2016), Chapter 6, "Monitoring Distributed Systems."** Symptoms vs causes, and why monitoring should start from what users experience. Free at sre.google/books.
- **Google, _The Site Reliability Workbook_ (O'Reilly, 2018), Chapter 5, "Alerting on SLOs."** Burn-rate alerting, which works from day one because the target is chosen rather than learned.
- **Charity Majors, Liz Fong-Jones and George Miranda, _Observability Engineering_ (O'Reilly, 2022).** On asking new questions of a system you don't understand yet, which is most of what hypercare is.
- **Heartbeat monitoring tools such as Healthchecks.io or Cronitor (docs).** Simple, concrete implementations of "alert when an expected check-in doesn't arrive."
- **Your payment provider's documentation on settlement timing and return or dispute codes.** Specific to your provider, but the fastest way to set realistic predictions for payment outcomes and their delays.

---

The habit to take from this: **for any system you launch, ask what it will do for the first time next week, next month and next quarter, and whether you'll be watching when it does.** A system hasn't been launched until each of its cycles has run once for real.
