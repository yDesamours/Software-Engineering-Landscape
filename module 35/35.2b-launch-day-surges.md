# Launch-Day Surges: Waitlists, Admission Control and Communicating Under Load

_Module 35 · Launch Checklist & Go-Live · 35.2b_

## The Problem

Suppose you're launching a collaborative whiteboard tool. For five months it's been invite-only, and 120,000 people have joined the waitlist. Launch is set for Tuesday at 09:00 Pacific. The plan: at 09:00, email everyone on the waitlist a "You're in!" link, post on a couple of launch sites and social media, and a popular YouTube channel publishes a review the same hour. Normal traffic, from the invite-only users, peaks at about 40 requests per second. The team load-tested to 1,000 requests per second, "25× normal," and autoscaling is on.

Here's the first hour.

**09:00–09:04.** The email provider delivers 120,000 emails in four minutes.

**09:06.** Signups are arriving at around 400 a minute. Before launch the rate was about 2 a minute. Each signup creates a workspace, copies a sample board (a 2 MB template with images) into it, creates a customer and trial in the billing provider, and sends a verification email. All synchronously, in the request.

**09:09.** Signup requests take 20 seconds. The database connection pool is exhausted. Autoscaling notices high CPU and starts adding instances, which take four minutes to become healthy. Each new instance opens its own connection pool, pushing the database toward its connection limit.

**09:12.** Users stare at a spinner, then click the link again, or open it in a second tab. Each click is a new signup attempt. Some attempts half-succeed, creating a workspace but timing out before the billing call, so the retry fails with "workspace already exists."

**09:15.** The "You're in!" links expire after 15 minutes, a security default from the invite-only period. Everyone who hasn't gotten through yet sees "Link expired" and clicks "Send me a new link." The email provider starts throttling.

**09:20.** The landing page goes down too. It's server-rendered by the same app, so the people who only wanted to _read_ about the product, including everyone arriving from the YouTube video, get a 502. The status page is on the same cluster, so it's down as well. The first launch-site comment reads "hug of death?", and it gets upvoted.

**09:45.** The team turns off new signups entirely to let the system recover. By then the load test's 1,000 requests per second has been exceeded several times over, mostly by retries. Of the people who clicked in the first hour, about a fifth got an account. Most of the others don't come back.

The load test had been honest about the wrong thing. It measured a normal day's mix of requests, scaled up gradually, with warm caches. Launch was a different shape entirely: a step, not a ramp; almost entirely the most expensive path in the app; arriving in synchronized waves; and amplified by the system's own failures.

```
 requests/s                                         capacity:
    ▲                                               ┄┄┄ autoscaling (minutes behind)
    │        ╭╮ retries                             ─── actual demand
    │   ╭────╯╰───╮╭╮  link-expired resends
    │   │         ╰╯╰──╮
    │   │  ┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄┄
    │   │┄┄┘                            ╰───────
    │┄┄┄│ load-tested ramp (never happened)
    │───┘
    └───┬─────────────────────────────────────▶ time
      09:00
```

## What It Is

A **launch surge** is a spike in demand that's sudden, synchronized and concentrated on the new-user path. Handling one well is a combination of six things:

1. **Shaping the demand you control,** such as when emails go out and how many at once.
2. **Preparing capacity before the spike,** because reactive scaling is too slow.
3. **Making the first request cheap,** and deferring the expensive work to a queue.
4. **Admission control at the front door,** often a virtual waiting room, when demand still exceeds what the system can do.
5. **Deliberate degradation,** switching off what isn't essential.
6. **Communicating honestly while under load,** through channels that don't depend on the thing that's overloaded.

It's related to rate limiting (Module 18), but it isn't the same thing. A rate limit protects the system from _individual clients_ exceeding their quota. Admission control protects the system as a whole from _everyone together_, and has to decide, fairly, who gets in first. On launch day, each individual user is well within any reasonable rate limit. There are just a hundred thousand of them.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The launch plan says "email the whole list at 09:00" and schedules every channel for the same hour.
- The load test used the normal request mix, ramped gradually, with warm caches.
- Autoscaling is the capacity plan.
- Signup does a lot of work synchronously: provisioning, sample data, third-party calls, emails.
- The landing page, docs or pricing page are served by the application rather than from a CDN.
- The status page runs on the same infrastructure, domain or provider as the product.
- Error pages say "Something went wrong. Please try again."
- Invite links, magic links or verification codes expire in minutes.
- Nobody knows the rate limits of the billing, email or auth providers.

## How It Works

### Why a launch spike is different

It helps to do the arithmetic before launch, because it's rarely what intuition expects. Say a quarter of the 120,000 waitlisted people click within the first 20 minutes. That's 30,000 signups in 20 minutes, 25 per second, about 750 times the normal signup rate, all landing on the code path that was least exercised during the invite-only period. On top of that:

- **Shape:** arrival is a step, not a ramp. A four-minute email send, a post hitting a front page, and a video going live all deliver their audience within minutes. Autoscaling is designed for ramps.
- **Mix:** a normal day is mostly returning users doing cheap reads with warm caches. Launch day is mostly new users doing the most expensive thing the product does, against cold caches.
- **Synchronization:** arrivals cluster around the moments you created. Separate channels scheduled for the same hour add their peaks together.
- **Amplification:** every failure creates more load. Users retry, open second tabs, click "resend link." Clients retry automatically. Expiring tokens turn waiting users into new requests. A system can end up stuck in overload even after the original spike passes, because retries alone keep it there. Researchers call this a _metastable failure_.
- **Fixed limits you don't control:** the billing, email and identity providers have rate limits that don't autoscale. They're often the real ceiling, and you only find out when you hit them.

### Shape the demand you control

The cheapest capacity is demand you don't create all at once. Much of launch-day traffic is traffic _you_ triggered, so you can decide its shape:

- **Release the waitlist in batches.** Send 10,000 invitations, watch the system, then send the next batch. Batches can be sized by how the system is actually coping, not by a fixed schedule (the implementation below does this). Emailing a waitlist in batches _is_ admission control, just done by email.
- **Stagger channels.** Don't put the email, the launch post and the video in the same hour. If you control the timing of any of them, spread them out.
- **Stagger by time zone** where it makes sense, so each region's morning is its own smaller wave.
- **Don't create synchronized clients.** A mobile push notification to every user at once produces an instant spike of app opens. Push providers and your own senders can spread sends over minutes.

Invite codes are the viral version. Gmail famously launched invite-only in 2004, with each user getting a few invites to give out. That caps growth at the rate existing users hand out invites, which is roughly the rate the system has proved it can handle. It also creates scarcity, which is sometimes marketing and sometimes resentment, and invites end up being resold.

### Prepare capacity before the spike

Autoscaling reacts to load that's already there, and new capacity usually takes minutes to become healthy. For a step change, that means the system is overloaded for exactly the minutes that matter most. So:

- **Scale up ahead of time** to the expected peak, with margin, and scale back down once things settle. It's cheaper than it sounds for a few hours.
- **Warm what's cold.** Pre-populate caches, pre-establish connection pools, and run a burst of synthetic requests through new code paths so JIT compilation and lazy initialization happen before real users arrive.
- **Watch for scaling that makes things worse.** Adding app instances adds connection pools, and the database has a connection limit. Know which layer is the real bottleneck (Module 23) before assuming more servers help.
- **Raise third-party limits** in advance, and confirm the new limits in writing. This belongs in the readiness review (35.1a), because vendor quota increases can take days.
- **Serve everything that can be static from a CDN** (Module 13): the landing page, docs, pricing, blog, even the signup form itself. Visitors who only want to read about the product are usually the majority, and they shouldn't touch your application servers at all.

### Make the first request cheap, and defer the rest

The single most effective structural change is to split signup into two steps:

```
Before:   click ──▶ [ create workspace + copy template + billing + email ] ──▶ response
                     (all of it in the request; 1–20 s under load)

After:    click ──▶ [ record signup (idempotent) + enqueue ] ──▶ "Setting up your space…"
                                   │
                                   ▼
                          provisioning queue ──▶ workers at a fixed rate
                          (template copy, billing, email)
```

**Accepting** a signup is one database write, cheap and fast, so the system can accept far more signups per second than it can fully provision. The **provisioning** runs from a queue (Module 10) at whatever rate the workers, the database and the billing provider can sustain. The queue absorbs the spike; its depth rises during the surge and drains afterward. The user sees an honest "setting up your workspace" page instead of a spinner that times out.

Two refinements help further:

- **Provision lazily.** Does every account need a copied sample board at signup, or only when they first open it? Deferring work until it's needed spreads it out naturally, and some of it never happens at all.
- **Make acceptance idempotent.** Keying the signup on the invitation (or email) means the second tab, the double-click and the retry all land on the same signup instead of creating duplicates or half-states.

This split also changes what the user experiences during a surge, from "the site is down" to "it's busy, I'm in line." The second one is much easier to forgive.

### Admission control: the virtual waiting room

Sometimes demand exceeds what the system can do even after all of the above. Then someone has to wait, and the question is whether the waiting is orderly or chaotic. Without admission control, everyone gets in at once and everyone gets a bad experience. With it, some people get a good experience now and the rest get a clear wait.

A **virtual waiting room** puts a queue in front of the application, usually at the edge (the CDN), where serving a waiting page is almost free:

```
   visitor ──▶ edge
               │
               ├── has valid admission token? ──yes──▶ application
               │
               └── no ──▶ take a ticket number (atomic counter)
                          │
                          ├── ticket ≤ admitted-up-to ──▶ issue signed token ──▶ application
                          │
                          └── otherwise ──▶ static waiting page: "You're #8,412.
                                            About 6 minutes. Keep this tab open;
                                            don't refresh. It will continue automatically."
```

A controller raises "admitted up to" at a rate the application can handle. It's best driven by measured health (latency, error rate, queue depth) rather than a fixed guess. Several details make the difference between a good waiting room and an irritating one:

- **Once admitted, stay admitted.** The token lasts long enough to complete signup, so nobody gets bounced back into the queue halfway through.
- **Refreshing doesn't help and doesn't hurt.** The ticket lives in a cookie, so refreshing keeps your place. Say so on the page, because otherwise people will refresh anyway.
- **Honest positions and estimates.** An ETA that keeps growing is worse than none.
- **Decide fairness in advance.** First come, first served rewards those who arrived early. Some ticketing sites randomize the order of everyone who arrived before the opening time, so that arriving at 08:55 isn't better than arriving at 08:59. For a software launch, first-come is usually fine.

Managed waiting rooms exist from CDN providers and specialist vendors, and cloud providers publish reference implementations. They're usually a better choice than building one on launch week.

### Degrade deliberately, with switches prepared in advance

Make a list, before launch, of what the product can live without for a few hours, and put each item behind a flag you've tested:

- Thumbnail generation for new boards
- Search indexing of new content
- Analytics and tracking events
- Recommendations, "what's new" feeds, activity digests
- Expensive exports and imports (queue them with a notice)
- Real-time presence indicators, which fan out to every collaborator

The key word is _prepared_. During a surge, nobody should be writing code to turn features off. Flipping a switch takes seconds; shipping a change through an overloaded pipeline to an overloaded system can take an hour.

### Stop the system from amplifying its own load

Much of the scenario's load came from the system itself. Each amplifier has a fix:

- **Retries:** idempotent operations make retries harmless. Server responses under overload should say `503` with a `Retry-After` header, and clients should back off with jitter (the AWS Builders' Library article in Further Reading explains why jitter matters).
- **Expiring links:** on launch day, a link that expires in 15 minutes turns every delay into a new request. Extend token lifetimes for the launch window, or make the link lead to a page that issues a fresh session rather than one that consumes a single-use token.
- **"Resend" buttons:** collapse repeated resend requests within a few minutes into one.
- **The UI:** a spinner invites refreshing. A page that says "you're in line, this tab will continue automatically" doesn't.

### Communicate while under load

Silence during an outage reads as incompetence; a clear message reads as popularity. The difference is mostly preparation:

- **The status page must not share fate with the product.** Different hosting provider, different domain, different DNS. A status page on the same cluster is down precisely when it's needed.
- **Error and waiting pages are static, served from the edge, and say something real:** what's happening, what the user should do, and what they shouldn't (refresh).
- **Messages written in advance:** "We're seeing far more signups than expected. You're in line and your place is saved," for the status page, social channels and support replies. The communications owner from 35.1b posts them; nobody drafts copy during an incident.
- **Afterward, follow up with those who didn't get in:** an email saying their spot is saved and when to come back recovers many who would otherwise never return.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Announcing to a large list at once:** a waitlist, a newsletter, an existing user base getting a new product.
- **Launches tied to a public moment:** a keynote, a launch-site post, press coverage, a popular creator's video.
- **Consumer products,** where individual users arrive unpredictably and can't be onboarded one by one.
- **Scarcity events:** ticket sales, limited drops, anything where everyone arrives at the same second by design.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Launches you're already staging** (35.2a). If you control who gets access and when, keep controlling it. The waves are your admission control, and the surge never happens.
- **Small audiences.** A launch to a 2,000-person list doesn't need a waiting room. Idempotent signup and a static landing page are enough.
- **Building your own waiting room for a one-off launch.** Use a managed one, or batch your emails and accept some queueing.
- **Massive pre-scaling for a launch that may get a tenth of its forecast.** Forecasts are uncertain. Prepare cheap levers (batching, static pages, degradation flags, deferred provisioning) generously, and expensive ones (large reserved capacity) with more care.

## Trade-offs

- **Waiting rooms are visible.** Some people will read a queue as "they couldn't handle it," although many read it as "it's popular." Either way, some visitors leave rather than wait.
- **Deferred provisioning adds a state.** "Account exists but isn't ready" has to be handled everywhere: login, billing, support tools, emails. It's a permanent complexity bought for a one-day problem, though it usually proves useful later too.
- **Degradation means a worse first impression.** New users see a reduced product on the day that matters most. Choose what to degrade with that in mind.
- **Batching delays people.** Someone who's waited five months gets their invite three hours later than their friend, and some will notice.
- **Pre-scaling costs money,** especially when the forecast was optimistic.
- **Every added layer is a dependency.** A waiting room at the edge is another thing that can fail, and it needs its own testing and its own bypass.
- **Forecasting is guesswork.** Plan for demand anywhere from a third to ten times the estimate, and make sure the cheap levers work across that whole range.

## Alternatives

**Brute-force capacity.** Provision for the worst case and hope the dependencies keep up. It can work for stateless read traffic served from a CDN. It rarely works for signup, where the database, the billing provider and the email provider have limits that money can't raise on launch morning.

**Avoid the spike entirely.** Staged launches (35.2a) replace one surge with many small, controlled waves. For most B2B products this is simply the better choice. The surge techniques here are for launches where a public moment is the point, or where arrivals are outside your control.

**Accept failure gracefully.** For a very small team, a legitimate strategy is: static landing page, email capture on overload ("We're overwhelmed. Leave your email and we'll let you in as soon as we can"), and batch invitations from that list over the following days. This is a waiting room made from a form and a spreadsheet, and it's much better than a 502.

## How to Achieve It

**Step 1: Do the arithmetic and use the free levers.** Estimate arrivals per minute from list size, expected click-through and channel timing. Batch the emails. Put the landing page on a CDN. Move the status page to independent infrastructure. Request third-party quota increases. _Next-step signal:_ the estimate is more than the signup path has ever been tested at.

**Step 2: Load test the launch shape.** Test a step, not a ramp, with the launch mix (mostly signups, cold caches) and third-party calls mocked at their real rate limits. _Next-step signal:_ signup is the bottleneck.

**Step 3: Split signup and prepare degradation.** Idempotent acceptance, provisioning through a queue, lazy sample data, and tested flags for non-essential features. Fix the amplifiers: token lifetimes, resend collapsing, `Retry-After`. _Next-step signal:_ even the cheap acceptance path can be overwhelmed by the expected peak.

**Step 4: Admission control at the edge.** A managed waiting room, driven by the application's health signals, with messages written in advance.

## Implementation

TypeScript. One health-driven controller decides how fast to let people in. It drives both the email batches and the waiting room, alongside a signup handler that accepts cheaply and defers the rest.

### One controller for "how fast can we admit people?"

```ts
interface Health {
  signupP95Ms: number;
  errorRate: number;          // fraction of 5xx on the signup path
  provisioningQueueDepth: number;
}

interface AdmissionConfig {
  minPerMinute: number;
  maxPerMinute: number;
  targets: { p95Ms: number; errorRate: number; queueDepth: number };
}

// Additive increase, multiplicative decrease: creep up while healthy, back off hard when not.
class AdmissionController {
  constructor(private cfg: AdmissionConfig, public perMinute = cfg.minPerMinute) {}

  update(h: Health): number {
    const t = this.cfg.targets;
    const overloaded =
      h.signupP95Ms > t.p95Ms || h.errorRate > t.errorRate || h.provisioningQueueDepth > t.queueDepth;

    this.perMinute = overloaded
      ? Math.max(this.cfg.minPerMinute, Math.floor(this.perMinute * 0.5))
      : Math.min(this.cfg.maxPerMinute, this.perMinute + 50);
    return this.perMinute;
  }
}
```

The email release uses it directly: instead of sending 120,000 invitations at 09:00, it sends in small batches sized to what the system is absorbing right now.

```ts
async function releaseWaitlist(controller: AdmissionController, clickThrough = 0.3) {
  while (await waitlist.remaining() > 0) {
    const rate = controller.update(await metrics.health());
    // Expected signups ≈ invites × clickThrough, spread over the next ~20 minutes.
    const batch = Math.floor((rate * 20) / clickThrough);
    await invitations.send(await waitlist.next(batch));
    await sleep(minutes(5));
  }
}
```

> **Aside: the queue depth is the signal that matters.** Latency and error rate tell you the system is hurting _now_. Provisioning queue depth tells you it's falling behind before anyone feels it, because acceptance is cheap and the backlog builds silently. Including it in the controller's inputs is what keeps the deferred work from becoming a slow-motion version of the original outage.

### Signup: accept cheaply, provision later

```ts
async function acceptSignup(req: { inviteToken: string; name: string; password: string }) {
  const invite = await invitations.verify(req.inviteToken);   // launch window: long-lived tokens
  if (!invite) return { status: 400, body: { error: "invalid-invite" } };

  // Idempotent on the invite: second tabs, double-clicks and retries all land here.
  const { signup, created } = await signups.upsert({
    key: invite.id,
    email: invite.email,
    name: req.name,
    passwordHash: await hash(req.password),
    state: "accepted",
  });

  if (created) await provisioningQueue.enqueue({ signupId: signup.id }, { dedupeKey: signup.id });

  return {
    status: 202,
    body: { signupId: signup.id, state: signup.state, next: `/setting-up/${signup.id}` },
  };
}
```

> **Aside: "accepted" is a real state now.** Login, billing, support tools and the "Setting up your space" page all have to handle an account whose workspace doesn't exist yet. That's the permanent cost of this split. The provisioning worker should also be idempotent per step (workspace, template, billing customer, email), because a worker that dies halfway will run again, and the billing provider will happily create two customers for the same person if asked twice. Most billing APIs accept an idempotency key; pass the signup ID.

### The waiting room at the edge

```ts
// Runs at the CDN edge, in front of /signup and the app.
async function waitingRoom(request: Request): Promise<Response> {
  const token = readCookie(request, "admit");
  if (token && (await verifyAdmission(token))) return fetch(request);   // admitted: pass through

  let ticket = Number(readCookie(request, "ticket"));
  if (!ticket) ticket = await counters.increment("launch-tickets");    // keep your place across refreshes

  const admittedUpTo = await counters.get("admitted-up-to");           // raised by the controller
  if (ticket <= admittedUpTo) {
    const res = await fetch(request);
    return withCookies(res, { admit: await signAdmission({ ticket, ttl: minutes(30) }) });
  }

  const ahead = ticket - admittedUpTo;
  const perMinute = await counters.get("admission-rate");
  return withCookies(
    staticWaitingPage({ position: ahead, etaMinutes: Math.ceil(ahead / Math.max(perMinute, 1)) }),
    { ticket: String(ticket) },
    { status: 200, headers: { "Cache-Control": "no-store", "Retry-After": "30" } },
  );
}
```

> **Aside: admitted tokens outlive the queue.** The 30-minute admission token is what keeps someone from being sent back into the queue halfway through signup. Keep it signed and short-lived; a token that's valid forever is a pass that can be shared. And give the waiting room itself a bypass switch. If the counter store at the edge has a problem, you need to be able to let everyone through or no one, without deploying.

## Further Reading

- **Google, _Site Reliability Engineering_ (O'Reilly, 2016), Chapters 21 "Handling Overload" and 22 "Addressing Cascading Failures."** Load shedding, client-side throttling, and how retries turn overload into cascading failure. Free at sre.google/books.
- **AWS Builders' Library: "Using load shedding to avoid overload" (David Yanacek) and "Timeouts, retries, and backoff with jitter" (Marc Brooker).** Practical and specific; the second explains exactly why synchronized retries make things worse.
- **Nathan Bronson et al., "Metastable Failures in Distributed Systems," HotOS 2021.** Why systems stay broken after the trigger passes, and why fixing the amplifier matters more than the trigger.
- **Cloudflare Waiting Room docs, and AWS "Virtual Waiting Room on AWS" solution.** Two concrete designs for edge admission control, managed and self-hosted.
- **Michael Nygard, _Release It!_, 2nd edition (Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2018),** on stability patterns and on self-inflicted load from marketing campaigns.

---

The habit to take from this: **when you plan a launch moment, ask how many people will arrive in the same five minutes, and what each of them costs the system on their first request.** Most launch-day outages are decided by those two numbers, and most of the fixes are cheap if you pick them before the email goes out.
