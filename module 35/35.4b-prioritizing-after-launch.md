# Prioritizing After Launch: Features, Bugs and Debt on One Roadmap

_Module 35 · Launch Checklist & Go-Live · 35.4b_

## The Problem

Suppose you make expense-management software for mid-sized companies: employees photograph receipts, a card program issues company cards, managers approve expenses, and everything syncs to the customer's accounting system. You launched three months ago and have about 350 paying companies. Twelve engineers, one product manager, a growing sales team.

Work now arrives from three directions, and each has its own list.

- **Sales has a feature list.** To close deals, reps promised things: multi-currency cards, per-diem rates, an integration with a second accounting system, custom approval chains. Fourteen items, several with dates in emails, two written into contracts.
- **Support has a bug list.** Triage (35.4a) works well now. There are 31 open S2 and S3 problems, each with its linked reports and reach. The biggest cluster: the accounting sync occasionally duplicates or drops transactions, and customers find out at month-end.
- **Engineering has a debt list.** The launch left shortcuts behind, each one sensible at the time. A reconciliation script, run by hand every morning, catches card transactions the webhook missed. The accounting sync polls every 15 minutes with no idempotency. The test suite is flaky enough that deploys have slowed from daily to twice a week. Receipt scanning runs on one queue shared by all customers.

Quarterly planning happens in a spreadsheet using RICE scores (reach, impact, confidence, effort). The features score highest. Sales supplies the reach and impact numbers, and every promised feature is "high impact, needed for deals." The debt items score near zero, because there's nothing to put under reach: "no direct user impact." Bugs are "handled separately, by whoever's free," which in practice means by nobody, since everyone is assigned to features. The resulting roadmap goes on a slide with nine months of dates, and sales starts quoting from it.

Two quarters later:

- **Features.** Eleven of the fourteen promised features shipped, most of them late. Usage data shows four of them are used by fewer than three companies each. The second accounting integration, the largest item, has 11 customers.
- **The reconciliation script** takes about a third of one engineer's time every day. On a morning when that engineer was sick, nobody ran it. For two days, 40 companies saw card balances that didn't match their bank.
- **Deploys** are now weekly. Every feature takes longer than estimated, partly because of the tests, partly because of the sync code everyone is afraid to touch.
- **The sync bug cluster** grew. When the team finally analyzes why customers left, the most common reason in exit interviews is "we couldn't trust the numbers in our accounting system." That problem had been on the bug list _and_ the debt list the whole time. On each list separately, it scored low.
- **The roadmap slide** has missed nearly every date. Sales has stopped believing it, and has started promising things that aren't on it at all.

No one made a bad decision on purpose. The trouble is that **three kinds of work competed for the same twelve engineers, but they were never actually compared.** Features were scored in terms of revenue, bugs in terms of severity, and debt in terms of engineering pain, and the scoring model only understood the first. The choice between them happened implicitly, by whichever list had a spreadsheet and a champion in the planning meeting.

## What It Is

**Prioritization after launch** is deciding how a fixed amount of engineering capacity is split between new features, fixing problems, and improving the system itself, and in what order the work within each happens. The **roadmap** is how those decisions are communicated: what the team is doing now, what it expects to do next, and what it's considering later.

Before launch, most of this is simple: build the product. After launch, the three kinds of work are all real, and all compete:

| Kind of work | Where it comes from | What it's usually measured in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Features** | Sales, customers, product strategy, competitors | Revenue, deals, adoption |
| **Bugs and quality** | Triage (35.4a), support, telemetry | Severity, reach, support load |
| **Debt and platform** | Engineering: launch shortcuts, toil, fragility | Engineering hours, incidents, speed |

The central problem is that these are measured in different units, so they can't be ranked against each other without translating them first. Module 31.3 covers how to identify and pay down technical debt; Module 28 covers backlogs and sprint planning. This article is about the layer above those: how the three kinds of work get their share of the team, and how the result is told to everyone who's waiting on it.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Features, bugs and debt live in three different lists, owned by three different people.
- The prioritization framework only has columns that features can fill in.
- Debt items are described as "cleanup" or "refactoring" with "no user impact."
- Bugs get fixed "when someone has time," and nobody has time.
- The same underlying problem appears on more than one list, scored differently on each.
- Roadmap dates beyond the current quarter are presented as commitments.
- Sales and customer success learn about priorities from the roadmap slide, and commit customers to its dates.
- Nobody checks whether shipped features were used, or changed what they were supposed to change.
- Lead time for changes is rising, and the explanation is "the codebase is getting bigger."

## How It Works

### Translate everything into outcomes

The first step is to describe every candidate piece of work, whatever list it came from, in terms of the business outcomes it affects. A short list of outcomes covers almost everything:

- **Acquisition and expansion:** new customers, deals, upsells.
- **Retention:** customers staying, and staying satisfied.
- **Cost to serve:** support load, manual operations, infrastructure spend.
- **Delivery speed:** how fast the team can ship anything else.
- **Risk:** the chance of an incident, data loss, security or compliance failure, and how bad it would be.

Now the three lists can speak the same language. Some examples from the expense product:

| Item | Came from | Outcome it affects, and how much |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Per-diem rates | Sales | Acquisition: 3 open deals (≈ $90k ARR) cite it |
| Sync duplicates and drops | Bugs (S2, 60 accounts) | Retention: top reason in exit interviews; also cost to serve (≈ 25 tickets a week) |
| Idempotent sync with webhooks | Debt | Retention (removes the bug cluster's cause), delivery speed (sync changes stop being scary) |
| Automate reconciliation | Debt | Cost to serve (≈ 13 engineer-hours a week), risk (2-day balance error already happened once) |
| Fix flaky tests | Debt | Delivery speed: lead time from ~3 days back toward same-day, for every future item |

Two things stand out once the table exists. First, "no user impact" was false for every debt item. Debt reaches users through reliability, through speed, and through what the team doesn't have time to build. The work is to trace that path and state it. Second, the sync bug and the sync debt were the same problem. Together, they were the single biggest retention risk in the product.

### Debt is interest, not principal

Engineers tend to describe debt by what it would take to fix ("the sync needs a rewrite, about six weeks"), which is the _principal_. Decision-makers need to know what it costs to leave it alone, which is the _interest_. Ward Cunningham's original debt metaphor was about exactly this: the cost of debt is the interest you keep paying on it.

Interest usually shows up in four forms, and each can be estimated:

- **Toil:** manual work to keep things running. The reconciliation script costs about 13 hours a week, every week.
- **Drag:** extra time on every change that touches the area. If every sync-related feature takes 40% longer, and a third of the roadmap touches sync, that's a measurable tax on the roadmap.
- **Incidents:** how often it causes one, and how much each costs.
- **Risk:** the chance of a severe failure. A shared receipt-scanning queue where one large customer's month-end upload blocks everyone else is a risk, even if it hasn't happened yet.

Debt with high interest is worth paying down early. Debt with low interest, like ugly code that's rarely touched, can often wait indefinitely, and that's fine. Martin Fowler's writing on internal quality makes the related point that low quality slows teams down within weeks, not years, which is why "we'll clean it up after the next launch" rarely pays off.

### Scoring models: useful for arguing, dangerous for deciding

Scoring frameworks are useful because they force people to write down estimates and assumptions that can then be argued about. The common ones:

- **RICE** (Intercom): Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. Simple and widely used. Its weakness is Impact, which is a guess on a small scale and the easiest number to inflate.
- **ICE:** Impact × Confidence × Ease. A lighter version of the same idea.
- **Cost of Delay and CD3** (Don Reinertsen; Joshua Arnold): estimate the value lost per week that an item isn't done, and divide by how long it takes. The highest ratio goes first. It puts features, bugs and debt on the same scale (money per week) and makes timing explicit.
- **WSJF** (Weighted Shortest Job First), as used in SAFe: a relative-scoring version of Cost of Delay divided by job size.
- **The Kano model:** separates features customers take for granted (their absence loses deals, their presence doesn't win them), ones where more is better, and ones that delight. Useful for recognizing "table stakes" requests, which score badly on impact but lose deals when missing.

Their shared failure modes are worth knowing:

- **False precision.** A RICE score of 412 versus 389 means nothing when every input was a guess. Treat scores as rough bands.
- **Gaming.** Whoever supplies the numbers controls the ranking. Ask for the evidence behind reach and impact ("3 open deals cite it," "60 accounts affected"), not a number on a scale.
- **Missing urgency profiles.** Not everything loses value at the same rate over time. Kanban's _classes of service_ (David Anderson) describe four common shapes:

```
value lost
by waiting      Expedite          Fixed date          Standard           Intangible
    ▲          ┌──────           │      ┌────       │    ╱              │            ╱
    │          │                 │      │           │   ╱               │           ╱
    │          │                 │      │           │  ╱                │         ╱
    │          │                 │      │           │ ╱                 │ ______╱
    └──────────┴───▶ time        └──────┴──▶        └╱─────▶            └──────────▶
     (S1 bugs, outages)   (contract, regulation)   (most features)   (most debt: cheap to
                                                                      delay now, expensive
                                                                      later)
```

  Most debt has the intangible shape: waiting costs little this quarter, and a lot later. Any model that scores by value _right now_ will rank it last, every quarter, until it becomes an expedite item in the form of an incident. That's what happened with the sync.

### Capacity allocation: decide the split before ranking

Because comparing a feature against a debt item is genuinely hard, even with outcomes stated, many teams separate the decision into two steps:

1. **Decide how capacity is split** among kinds of work, for this quarter.
2. **Rank within each kind,** using whatever scoring suits it.

```
  Quarter capacity: 12 engineers ≈ 130 engineer-weeks after overhead

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────┬──────────────────┐
  │ Product outcomes            55%     │ Quality   20%   │ Platform/debt 25%│
  │ (features, ranked by CD3            │ (S2/S3 problems │ (ranked by       │
  │  with evidence)                     │  by reach)      │  interest)       │
  └─────────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────┴──────────────────┘
```

The split isn't fixed. It changes with the product's situation, and it should change for stated reasons, driven by signals the whole team can see:

- **Right after launch,** quality usually needs more than people expect, because real users find real problems.
- **When lead time is rising or deploys are slowing,** shift toward platform work, since everything else is getting more expensive.
- **When S2 problems are over their response targets** (35.4a), or churn reasons point at reliability, shift toward quality.
- **When reliability is inside its service-level objectives** and quality signals are healthy, shift toward features. Google's error budgets (Module 19) formalize one version of this: when a service has used up its budget of unreliability, reliability work takes priority until it recovers.

A common rule of thumb is "20% of time for tech debt." A fixed percentage is better than nothing, but it has two known problems. It gets raided whenever a deadline looms, and it becomes a place where engineers do whatever they like rather than paying down the debt with the highest interest. An allocation driven by visible signals, with debt ranked by interest, avoids both.

### Feature requests: count needs, not asks

After launch, feature requests arrive through triage (35.4a) and sales. Handled well, they become some of the best evidence you have. Handled badly, they turn the roadmap into a queue of whatever the last big customer asked for.

- **Group requests by the underlying need,** not the proposed solution. Three customers asking for "per-diem rates," "travel allowances" and "fixed daily meal budgets" have the same need.
- **Count by segment and revenue,** not just by volume. Twenty requests from free-trial accounts and two from your largest customers say different things. Both are information; neither decides alone.
- **Check fit with strategy.** A request that only makes sense for a kind of customer you're not trying to win should be declined, kindly, even from a big account.
- **Make sales commitments a visible, limited class.** Promising a feature with a date to close a deal is sometimes worth it. It should be an explicit decision with the roadmap owner's approval, with a cap on how much capacity such commitments can take. Each one should then be tracked like a fixed-date item.

### Roadmaps: commit to the near, signal the far

A roadmap with dates for every item nine months out is a list of promises that the team will probably break, and the people who quote from it will pay for that. A structure that matches how certainty actually works communicates better. The **now / next / later** format (popularized by Janna Bastow of ProdPad) is one common version:

| Column | Meaning | Certainty | Stated as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Now** | In progress this cycle | Committed | Specific work, often with dates |
| **Next** | Likely next cycle | Planned, may change | Problems to solve, rough scope |
| **Later** | Being considered | Directional | Themes and outcomes |

Some practices that make roadmaps trustworthy:

- **Put outcomes on the roadmap, not just features.** "Accounting sync you can trust" is a roadmap item. The work under it (idempotent sync, webhook migration, fixing the drop bug) is how it gets done. This also gives debt and bugs a place on the roadmap, instead of being invisible work that makes features seem late.
- **Dates only where they're real:** a contract, a regulation, an event. Those items get protected scope and are tracked as fixed-date work.
- **Different views for different audiences.** Customers and sales need themes and "now." Engineering needs the detail. The executive team needs the allocation and the reasons.
- **Show what you're not doing,** and why. An explicit "not now" list prevents the same argument every week and gives sales something honest to tell customers.

### Close the loop: did it work?

The last step is the one teams skip most often: after shipping, check whether the work changed the outcome it was chosen for. Did per-diem rates close the three deals? Did the sync rewrite reduce sync tickets and remove "trust in numbers" from exit interviews? Did fixing the tests bring lead time down?

Module 16 covers feature adoption and product metrics. The use here is specific: results feed back into the next round of estimates. If features that sales rated "high impact" routinely get used by three companies, the confidence placed on those ratings should go down. Features that nobody uses are also a kind of debt. Each one has to be maintained, tested and supported, and removing them is legitimate roadmap work.

### Cadence

Plan quarterly, and check monthly. Signals that should trigger an earlier re-plan include a major incident, a large deal won or lost on a specific capability, a dramatic change in a quality signal, and discovering that an estimate was off by more than half. Replanning constantly is its own failure, since the team never finishes anything, but a plan that ignores new evidence for three months is worse.

## When It's Appropriate

- **After launch,** when bugs, debt and features all have real claims on the team for the first time.
- **When several groups have their own lists:** sales, support, engineering and product each with priorities that nobody reconciles.
- **When delivery is visibly slowing,** and the cause is debt that never wins a place in the plan.
- **When a roadmap is shown to customers or sales,** and its reliability affects revenue.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Before product-market fit.** When the product might change direction next month, one short list and a weekly conversation beat any framework. Debt can reasonably be ignored until you know the code will survive.
- **Very small teams,** where three people can hold every candidate item in their heads and discuss trade-offs directly.
- **Heavy scoring machinery.** Spreadsheets with weighted formulas and many columns tend to produce confident-looking numbers from guesses. Stated outcomes, rough estimates and an explicit allocation cover most of the value.
- **Precise cost-of-delay estimates for everything.** Estimate carefully for the large or contested items. For the rest, rough bands are enough.

## Trade-offs

- **Translation takes effort.** Stating every debt item's interest, or every feature's evidence, is real work for engineers and product managers. It's worth it for the large items and often not for small ones.
- **Outcome estimates are still guesses.** Cost of delay in dollars per week looks precise and usually isn't. The value is in comparing and challenging assumptions, not in the numbers themselves.
- **Allocation can become politics.** Deciding the split between product, quality and platform is a negotiation between groups. Tying it to visible signals helps, but someone still has to make the call.
- **Honest roadmaps disappoint.** Sales loses the comfort of dates, and customers hear "not now." In the short term, a now/next/later roadmap can feel like less commitment. Over time it's the only kind people believe.
- **Measuring outcomes delays conclusions.** Some effects take months to appear, and many things change at once. Attributing a churn decrease to one fix is usually approximate.
- **Saying no to big customers costs something.** Declining a request that doesn't fit the strategy can cost a deal. That's sometimes the right trade, but it's a real one.

## Alternatives

**Shape Up** (Basecamp). Instead of a ranked backlog, leadership places "bets" on shaped pitches for fixed six-week cycles, followed by a two-week cool-down that teams use for bugs and cleanup. Ideas that aren't bet on are simply dropped, not kept in a backlog. It handles debt and bugs through the cool-down rather than allocation, and works well for small, senior teams. It's a harder fit when sales has contractual dates.

**Dedicated teams per kind of work.** A platform team owns debt and infrastructure; product teams own features; a support-engineering rotation owns bugs. This makes allocation structural, which is stable and clear. It also risks product teams creating debt that someone else pays for, and platform teams building things product teams didn't need.

**Error-budget-driven policy.** For reliability specifically, SLOs and error budgets (Module 19) decide automatically when reliability work comes first. This is excellent for availability and latency. It says nothing about correctness bugs, usability or delivery speed, so it covers one part of the allocation, not all of it.

**Sales-driven roadmap.** Letting the sales pipeline set priorities is common in early B2B companies, and in the short term it's often right: you need the revenue. It becomes a problem when the product turns into a collection of single-customer features, and when nothing but features ever gets built.

## How to Achieve It

**Step 1: One list, stated in outcomes.** Put features, bug clusters (from triage) and debt items in a single list, each with the outcome it affects and the evidence for it. State debt as interest. Look for items that appear on more than one list. _Next-step signal:_ it's still hard to compare items of different kinds.

**Step 2: An explicit allocation.** Decide the product/quality/platform split for the quarter, with written reasons, and rank within each part. Choose the signals (lead time, S2 backlog, SLOs, churn reasons) that will shift it next time. _Next-step signal:_ people outside engineering are making plans based on the roadmap.

**Step 3: A now/next/later roadmap.** Commit only to "now," put outcomes rather than just features on it, and publish views for different audiences, including a "not now" list. Make sales commitments an explicit, capped class. _Next-step signal:_ you're unsure whether what you shipped made a difference.

**Step 4: Close the loop.** For each significant item, record the expected effect when prioritizing it, and check it a month or a quarter after shipping. Use the results to adjust confidence in future estimates, and to remove features that aren't earning their keep.

## Implementation

TypeScript. Three pieces: work items expressed in a common unit (cost of delay per week), a capacity allocation that shifts based on signals, and a planner that fills each part of the allocation and explains what it left out.

### Common units: cost of delay, including debt's interest

```ts
type Kind = "product" | "quality" | "platform";
type Urgency = "expedite" | "fixed-date" | "standard" | "intangible";

interface WorkItem {
  id: string;
  title: string;
  kind: Kind;
  outcome: "acquisition" | "retention" | "cost-to-serve" | "delivery-speed" | "risk";
  evidence: string[];          // "3 open deals cite it", "60 accounts, S2", "13 h/week toil"
  costOfDelayPerWeek: number;  // value lost for each week it isn't done, in currency
  urgency: Urgency;
  deadline?: Date;             // fixed-date items only
  weeks: number;               // engineer-weeks, rough
  confidence: number;          // 0..1, lowered for sources whose past estimates missed
}

// Debt: turn interest into cost of delay, so it can be compared with everything else.
interface DebtInterest {
  toilHoursPerWeek: number;
  dragOnOtherWork: { fraction: number; affectedWeeksPerWeek: number }; // e.g. +40% on 1/3 of work
  incidentsPerQuarter: number;
  costPerIncident: number;
}

function debtCostOfDelay(i: DebtInterest, costPerEngineerHour: number): number {
  const toil = i.toilHoursPerWeek * costPerEngineerHour;
  const drag = i.dragOnOtherWork.fraction * i.dragOnOtherWork.affectedWeeksPerWeek * 40 * costPerEngineerHour;
  const incidents = (i.incidentsPerQuarter * i.costPerIncident) / 13;
  return toil + drag + incidents;
}

const automateReconciliation: WorkItem = {
  id: "plat-12",
  title: "Automate card reconciliation",
  kind: "platform",
  outcome: "cost-to-serve",
  evidence: ["≈13 h/week manual toil", "2-day balance mismatch for 40 companies in August"],
  costOfDelayPerWeek: debtCostOfDelay(
    { toilHoursPerWeek: 13, dragOnOtherWork: { fraction: 0, affectedWeeksPerWeek: 0 },
      incidentsPerQuarter: 1, costPerIncident: 30_000 },
    120,
  ),
  urgency: "intangible",
  weeks: 3,
  confidence: 0.8,
};
```

> **Aside: the point is the comparison, not the number.** `debtCostOfDelay` produces a figure in the same unit as a feature's "value of three open deals per week," which is what lets them sit on one list. Neither figure is accurate. But writing down "13 hours a week plus one incident a quarter" makes the estimate something people can challenge with evidence, where "important cleanup" was only something to agree or disagree with. The `confidence` field is where the loop closes: if a source's estimates keep missing, its items get less weight.

### Allocation that shifts with signals

```ts
interface Signals {
  leadTimeDays: number;         // commit to production, median
  s2OverTarget: number;         // S2 problems past their response target (35.4a)
  errorBudgetRemaining: number; // 0..1 across key SLOs
  reliabilityChurnShare: number;// share of churned accounts citing reliability or data trust
}

type Allocation = Record<Kind, number>;

function allocate(base: Allocation, s: Signals): { allocation: Allocation; reasons: string[] } {
  const a = { ...base };
  const reasons: string[] = [];
  const shift = (from: Kind, to: Kind, amount: number, why: string) => {
    const moved = Math.min(amount, a[from] - 0.3 * base[from]);  // never starve a kind entirely
    if (moved <= 0) return;
    a[from] -= moved; a[to] += moved; reasons.push(`${why}: ${Math.round(moved * 100)}% ${from} → ${to}`);
  };

  if (s.leadTimeDays > 2) shift("product", "platform", 0.10, `lead time ${s.leadTimeDays}d > 2d`);
  if (s.s2OverTarget > 3) shift("product", "quality", 0.10, `${s.s2OverTarget} S2s past target`);
  if (s.errorBudgetRemaining < 0.2) shift("product", "quality", 0.10, "error budget nearly spent");
  if (s.reliabilityChurnShare > 0.25) shift("product", "platform", 0.05, "reliability is a leading churn reason");

  return { allocation: a, reasons };
}

// allocate({ product: 0.6, quality: 0.2, platform: 0.2 }, currentSignals)
```

> **Aside: the reasons are the output.** The allocation itself is a starting proposal for the planning conversation, not a decision a function should make. What the function guarantees is that any shift away from features comes with a stated signal behind it, and that no kind of work drops below 30% of its base share. That makes the planning conversation about whether the signals are right, which is a much better argument than "engineering always wants more time for cleanup." The thresholds are illustrative; pick yours from your own history.

### The planner: fill each part, and say what didn't fit

```ts
interface Plan {
  byKind: Record<Kind, WorkItem[]>;
  notNow: Array<{ item: WorkItem; reason: string }>;
  warnings: string[];
}

function plan(items: WorkItem[], capacityWeeks: number, allocation: Allocation, quarterEnd: Date): Plan {
  const result: Plan = { byKind: { product: [], quality: [], platform: [] }, notNow: [], warnings: [] };

  // Fixed-date and expedite items are placed first, against their own kind's budget.
  const score = (i: WorkItem) => (i.costOfDelayPerWeek * i.confidence) / i.weeks;   // CD3, confidence-weighted
  const ordered = [...items].sort((x, y) =>
    Number(y.urgency === "expedite") - Number(x.urgency === "expedite") ||
    Number(y.urgency === "fixed-date" && !!y.deadline && y.deadline <= quarterEnd) -
      Number(x.urgency === "fixed-date" && !!x.deadline && x.deadline <= quarterEnd) ||
    score(y) - score(x));

  const remaining: Record<Kind, number> = {
    product: capacityWeeks * allocation.product,
    quality: capacityWeeks * allocation.quality,
    platform: capacityWeeks * allocation.platform,
  };

  for (const item of ordered) {
    if (item.weeks <= remaining[item.kind]) {
      result.byKind[item.kind].push(item);
      remaining[item.kind] -= item.weeks;
    } else {
      result.notNow.push({ item, reason: `${item.kind} capacity left: ${remaining[item.kind].toFixed(1)} of ${item.weeks} weeks needed` });
      if (item.urgency === "fixed-date" && item.deadline && item.deadline <= quarterEnd)
        result.warnings.push(`Fixed-date item "${item.title}" doesn't fit: renegotiate scope or date now, not in week 11`);
    }
  }

  const intangibleLeftOut = result.notNow.filter(n => n.item.urgency === "intangible");
  if (intangibleLeftOut.length)
    result.warnings.push(`${intangibleLeftOut.length} intangible-urgency items deferred again; check how many quarters each has waited`);
  return result;
}
```

> **Aside: `notNow` is part of the roadmap.** Every item that didn't fit comes out with the specific reason it didn't, which is the "not now" list the roadmap should publish. The warnings target the two ways this scenario went wrong. A contractual fixed-date item that doesn't fit is flagged at planning time, when renegotiating is still cheap. And intangible items, mostly debt, are flagged when they're deferred yet again, because repeated deferral is precisely how the sync problem grew into the top churn reason. The planner can't make those calls. It makes sure nobody can miss that a call is being made.

## Further Reading

- **Donald Reinertsen, _The Principles of Product Development Flow_ (Celeritas, 2009).** The source of cost of delay as the unit for comparing work, and of weighted-shortest-job-first sequencing. Dense but foundational.
- **Joshua Arnold, Black Swan Farming (blackswanfarming.com), on Cost of Delay and CD3.** A practical, less theoretical treatment, including urgency profiles and worked examples.
- **David J. Anderson, _Kanban_ (Blue Hole Press, 2010).** Classes of service: expedite, fixed date, standard and intangible, and why they need different handling.
- **Sean McBride, "RICE: Simple prioritization for product managers," Intercom blog (2016).** The origin of RICE, with its intended use as a way to structure thinking.
- **Melissa Perri, _Escaping the Build Trap_ (O'Reilly, 2018).** On outcome-oriented product management, and what happens when a roadmap becomes a list of features to deliver.
- **Martin Fowler, "Is High Quality Software Worth the Cost?" (martinfowler.com, 2019),** and **"TechnicalDebt."** The case that internal quality pays for itself within weeks, and the interest-and-principal framing, credited to Ward Cunningham.
- **Ryan Singer, _Shape Up_ (Basecamp, 2019).** Free online. Betting, fixed cycles, cool-down periods, and the case against keeping a backlog.
- **Janna Bastow, on now/next/later roadmaps (ProdPad).** The origin and reasoning of the format.
- **Within this collection:** Module 16 (feature adoption and product metrics), Module 19 (SLOs and error budgets), Module 28 (backlogs and planning), Module 31.3 (identifying and paying down debt), 35.4a (triage, which feeds the quality part of the plan).

---

The habit to take from this: **when two kinds of work compete for the same people, make them speak the same language before choosing between them.** If one side is measured in revenue and the other in "cleanup," the choice has been made before the meeting starts.
