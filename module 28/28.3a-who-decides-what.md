# Who Decides What: Scrum's Roles as Decision Rights

_Module 28.3a — Agile/Scrum Methodology_

## The Problem

Suppose you're on the in-house digital team at a regional bus company. Seven people build the journey planner and the mobile ticketing app used by about 200,000 passengers a month. On paper the team has every Scrum role. In practice, nobody can tell you who decides what.

**A product owner who can't say no.** The product owner is a business analyst eighteen months into the job. She writes tickets and keeps the backlog tidy. The real priorities come from a monthly steering committee and from the head of marketing, who has edit access to the tracker and reorders the backlog himself. In one sprint, the goal (paying for tickets with Apple Pay) was replaced on day four by a banner for a summer promotion, then partly restored on day seven when finance asked why Apple Pay had slipped.

**Two product owners, one team.** Operations wanted tools for the control room; marketing wanted features in the app. Each got "their" product owner and half the team's time. Their items conflicted in shared code, and when they did, the team resolved it in favor of whichever product owner had messaged more recently.

**A Scrum Master who schedules meetings.** The Scrum Master is also a half-time developer. Her visible work is booking rooms, taking notes and administering the tracker. Her impediment list has 30 items. The oldest, getting access to the ticketing vendor's test environment, took seven weeks to resolve, during which two developers tested payment changes against production with real cards.

**An engineering manager who assigns the work.** In standup, the engineering manager hands out tickets to individuals. Performance reviews cite each person's story points. The tech lead makes every technical decision and is the only person who understands the payment code. When he went on holiday, a payment bug waited ten days.

**Stakeholders who go around everyone.** Customer services, marketing and the accessibility officer message developers directly: "Can you just add a line to the FAQ screen?" About a fifth of the team's work never appears on the board.

**Decisions nobody owns.** The app fails a screen-reader check on the ticket purchase flow. Is that a release blocker? The product owner thinks the developers decide. The developers think the product owner does. It ships, and a passenger's complaint reaches the transport regulator.

Every one of these is a question about **decision rights**: who is allowed to decide, who must be consulted, and who is accountable when it goes wrong. The team had the titles and none of the answers.

## What It Is

Scrum defines three **accountabilities** within the Scrum team. The 2020 Scrum Guide deliberately calls them accountabilities rather than roles or job titles, because they describe what someone answers for, not what their business card says.

- **The product owner** is accountable for the value of what the team builds: the product goal, the contents and order of the product backlog, and making both clear. It's one person, not a committee, and the organization is expected to respect their decisions.
- **The developers** are accountable for how the work gets done: planning the sprint, meeting the definition of done, and adapting their plan daily. "Developers" means everyone doing the work, including testers, designers and analysts.
- **The Scrum Master** is accountable for the team's effectiveness: helping the team, the product owner and the wider organization understand and use Scrum well, and getting impediments removed.

Two groups sit outside the Scrum team but matter just as much:

- **Stakeholders** (customers, users, other departments, leadership) who have needs and constraints and influence what's built through the product owner and the review.
- **Line management**, such as an engineering manager, which Scrum doesn't define at all. Managers are usually responsible for people: hiring, careers, capability and the organization around the team.

The common confusion is between **product owner** and **product manager**. Many product companies use "product manager" for a broader job: discovery, strategy, market and pricing, often in a trio with a design lead and a tech lead. A product owner in the Scrum sense is the accountability for the backlog, which a product manager may hold. Problems start when the title "product owner" is given to someone who holds none of the product manager's authority, as happened here.

## Recognizing the Problem

- People outside the team reorder the backlog, or change the sprint's content, without going through the product owner.
- The product owner can't say no to a senior stakeholder, or has to check with someone before every priority decision.
- Several people are called product owner for the same team.
- Work reaches developers directly, and a significant share never appears on the board.
- A manager assigns tasks to individuals in the standup.
- Individual story points or velocity appear in performance reviews.
- One person makes all technical decisions, and work stops when they're away.
- The Scrum Master's work is mostly scheduling and note-taking, and impediments sit for weeks.
- When something goes wrong, people disagree about who was supposed to decide.

## How It Works

### 1. Start from decisions, not titles

List the decisions that actually come up, and give each one owner. Others may be consulted or informed, but one person or group decides:

```
 decision                                      decides          consulted                 informed
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 product goal; what's in the backlog, order    product owner    stakeholders, developers  everyone
 how much fits in a sprint                     developers       product owner             stakeholders
 how to build it, who does which task          developers       tech lead as advisor      —
 whether an item meets the definition of done  developers       (standards set with org)  product owner
 what goes into the definition of done         Scrum team       compliance, security, a11y everyone
 whether to release                            product owner    developers                stakeholders
 how the team works (process changes)          Scrum team       Scrum Master facilitates  —
 who's on the team; hiring; careers            eng. manager     team                      —
 budget, legal and regulatory constraints      organization     product owner             team
```

The accessibility failure falls out of this table. The screen-reader check belongs in the **definition of done** (28.4), which the team sets together with organizational standards. An item that fails it isn't done, so the question "is it a blocker?" never arises. The product owner decides whether to release; developers decide whether something is done.

Different organizations can reasonably draw some lines differently. What matters is that each line is drawn, written down and agreed, so it doesn't get redrawn in every disagreement. A useful refinement is **delegation levels**, from "I decide and tell you" through "we decide together" to "you decide and don't need to tell me," agreed for each kind of decision.

### 2. The product owner: real authority, one voice

A product owner is only useful if they can make the call. That requires:

- **Authority that the organization backs.** Stakeholders influence the backlog by talking to the product owner, bringing evidence, and taking part in reviews, not by editing it. If leadership overrules the product owner routinely, the real product owner is leadership, and the team should work with them directly.
- **One person per product.** Two product owners for one team means the team resolves their disagreements, badly. The alternative is one product owner with explicit capacity allocations: for example, 60% passenger app, 25% control-room tools, 15% technical health, set with leadership and revisited quarterly. Stakeholder groups then compete within their allocation rather than against each other in the sprint.
- **Enough knowledge to decide.** The product owner needs direct contact with users and stakeholders, and evidence like the weighted needs from 27.3a. A product owner who only relays other people's decisions is a **proxy**, which adds a delay without adding judgment.
- **Time.** Product ownership is a full job for most products. A product owner who is also three other things will default to whatever the loudest stakeholder asked for.

Making trade-offs visible is a large part of the job: "If the summer promotion banner goes in this sprint, Apple Pay moves two weeks. Do you want that?" is a decision the head of marketing can make in the open, instead of by quietly reordering the tracker.

### 3. The developers: self-management with shared knowledge

Developers decide how to do the work and who does which part. That's not a courtesy; it's where the knowledge is. A manager assigning tickets to individuals optimizes for keeping everyone busy, which increases work in progress (28.1a) and concentrates knowledge in whoever always gets that kind of ticket.

Self-management works when:

- **The team is cross-functional**, with the skills to finish work without handing it off: testing, design, accessibility and operations included, or reachable without waiting weeks.
- **Knowledge is spread deliberately.** Pairing, rotating who works on the payment code, and reviews by someone other than the expert. The goal is that no area of the code has only one person who understands it.
- **The tech lead advises rather than approves.** An experienced engineer guiding design, reviewing risky changes and mentoring is valuable. One who must make every decision becomes a bottleneck and a single point of failure.

### 4. The Scrum Master: impediments and the system around the team

The Scrum Master's most valuable work is usually outside the meetings:

- **Removing impediments the team can't remove itself**, especially organizational ones: the seven-week wait for a test environment, a stakeholder who keeps bypassing the product owner, a policy that forces every release through a monthly committee. The measure is how long impediments stay open, the same aging idea as in 28.1a.
- **Coaching the team and the product owner:** helping them slice work, run events that produce decisions (28.2a), and notice when they're drifting into routine.
- **Helping the organization work with the team:** explaining to leadership why the sprint goal matters, and what reordering mid-sprint costs.

Facilitating events is part of the job, but booking rooms and taking notes isn't the point of it. A Scrum Master who is also a half-time developer will usually lose the Scrum Master half whenever there's a deadline. That's workable for a mature team with few impediments; it isn't for a team in the situation above.

### 5. The engineering manager: people and capability

Scrum doesn't define line managers, but most organizations have them, and they have a clear job that doesn't overlap with the others: hiring, careers and feedback, building the team's skills, making sure the team has what it needs, and working on organizational problems with the Scrum Master.

Two things are outside the manager's lane in a self-managing team. **Assigning daily work** belongs to the developers. **Judging people by story points or velocity** is harmful: points are a planning tool for the team (28.5), and once they measure individuals, people inflate estimates and avoid helping others.

### 6. Stakeholders: a front door and a regular window

Stakeholders need a way in and a way to see what's happening:

- **One front door for requests**, owned by the product owner: a request form or intake queue, with a promise of a response within a set time. Requests that reach developers directly get redirected there, politely and every time.
- **A small, explicit lane for genuinely small, urgent requests** (a typo on a fares page) if the team wants one, with its own WIP limit, rather than invisible work.
- **The review as the regular window.** Stakeholders see what was built and influence what's next there (28.2a).
- **A visible roadmap** that shows direction beyond the current sprint, so stakeholders don't feel they have to fight for every item every two weeks.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A team serving several stakeholder groups** that compete for its time. Clear product ownership and capacity allocations replace negotiation by volume.
- **Organizations where people outside the team can reach in** and change priorities or assign work.
- **Recurring disputes about whether something is done, ready or allowed to ship,** which are almost always missing decision rights.
- **A team that depends on one or two people** for key decisions or key code.
- **Regulated or public-facing products,** where accountability for quality and compliance decisions must be explicit.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A small startup where the founder is the product owner** and talks to the whole team daily. The decision rights are obvious; writing a table adds nothing.
- **A dedicated full-time Scrum Master for one mature, stable team** with few impediments. The accountability can be held part-time by someone on the team, or shared with a coach across teams.
- **Strict separation of roles in a very small team.** A four-person team may reasonably have its product owner also doing some analysis and testing. What matters is that the product owner's decisions are still clearly theirs.
- **Formal decision matrices for every small choice.** Use them for decisions that cause disagreements, not for which library to use for date formatting.

## Trade-offs

- **A single product owner is a bottleneck.** Every priority question goes through one person. If they're unavailable or overloaded, the team waits. Delegating smaller decisions to developers helps; so does a product owner with enough time.
- **Concentrated authority can be wrong confidently.** One person deciding means one person's blind spots. Evidence (27.3a), reviews with real users and the team's challenge are the counterweights.
- **Leadership gives up direct control.** Executives who used to reorder backlogs must now work through the product owner. That's slower for them, and some will resist it.
- **Self-management takes skill and time to build.** A team used to being assigned work may struggle at first with choosing, swarming and spreading knowledge. The manager's job changes from directing to developing.
- **The Scrum Master role is easy to see as overhead.** Its value shows up as impediments removed and problems prevented, which are hard to count. Under budget pressure it's often the first role cut.
- **Clear lines create friction at the edges.** "That's the product owner's decision" can become a way to avoid responsibility. Decision rights say who decides, not that others stop caring.

## Alternatives

**The product trio (product manager, design lead, tech lead).** Common in product companies. The three share discovery and decisions about what to build, with the product manager holding final say on priorities. It spreads product thinking across disciplines more naturally than a single product owner, and it often fits better where discovery (deciding what's worth building) is a large part of the work.

**Kanban's lighter roles.** The Kanban Method doesn't require specific roles. Where it names any, they're a service request manager (who understands customers and orders incoming work, similar to a product owner) and a service delivery manager (who looks after flow, similar to a Scrum Master's focus on impediments).

**Team structures beyond one team.** When several teams work on related products, how teams are organized matters as much as roles within them. _Team Topologies_ describes stream-aligned teams, platform teams and the ways they interact, which often resolves stakeholder conflicts that look like role problems.

**Manager-led teams.** A team where the engineering manager also holds product ownership and assigns work can function, especially when small. It depends heavily on one person's judgment and availability, and it usually concentrates knowledge rather than spreading it.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Write down the decisions.** List the decisions that caused disagreement or delay in the last few months, and who actually made each one. *Signal to move on:* decisions made by people outside the team, or by nobody.

**L1 — One product owner, with authority confirmed by leadership.** Remove edit access to the backlog from people who aren't on the team. Replace multiple product owners with one, plus capacity allocations. *Signal:* stakeholders still reaching developers directly.

**L2 — A front door for requests** and, if needed, a small visible lane for urgent small work. Everything on the board. *Signal:* standup still run as task assignment.

**L3 — Hand "how" to the developers.** The manager stops assigning tasks; the team plans its own work and deliberately spreads knowledge of critical areas. Remove story points from performance reviews. *Signal:* impediments sitting for weeks.

**L4 — Make impediments the Scrum Master's visible work,** with an aging list reviewed with the manager and leadership. *Signal:* the roles are clear, but the backlog they decide over is still a long list of vague tickets, which is 28.4.
