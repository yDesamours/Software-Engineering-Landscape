# "When Will It Be Done?": Estimation, Velocity, and Forecasting

_Module 28.5a — Agile/Scrum Methodology_

## The Problem

Suppose you're on a team of six building donor-management software for charities: donor records, recurring donations, campaigns and, every January, the annual tax receipts that donors need for their returns. Several hundred charities use it. Every year, the receipts must go out by the end of January. There is no moving that date.

It's mid-October. The big project this year is rebuilding receipts: multiple currencies, combined receipts for donors who give to several funds, and templates each charity can customize. The director asks the question every director asks: **when will it be done?**

What happened last time this team was asked, and what they do now, shows most of what goes wrong with estimation.

**An estimate becomes a promise.** Last spring, asked about the recurring-donations rework, the tech lead said "about three weeks" in a corridor conversation. By Friday it was a date on a board presentation. It took nine weeks. Nobody lied; "three weeks" was a guess about work nobody had broken down, and it was heard as a commitment.

**Points that measure nothing.** The team estimates in story points. A year ago, velocity (points finished per sprint) was about 30. Leadership started showing velocity per team on a dashboard and asked for "10% improvement." Velocity is now 52. The number of items finished per sprint hasn't changed. The same work simply gets more points. Meanwhile a new manager asked for "points in hours" so he could plan capacity, and "1 point = 1 day" crept into planning.

**Planning poker as a two-hour ritual.** Every week, the team estimates about 40 items one by one. The senior developer usually speaks first, and everyone converges on her number. When estimates differ widely (a 2 and a 13), the team averages them and moves on, instead of finding out what the 13 knows.

**A single date from a division.** For receipts, the product owner adds up the backlog: 120 points, velocity 30, so four sprints, eight weeks. The date goes to the director. It ignores that velocity varies from 18 to 40 between sprints, that about a quarter of each sprint goes to support escalations, and that the last big project's backlog grew by a fifth while it was being built.

The director doesn't need a number. She needs to know whether receipts will be ready for January, how confident the team is, and what to cut if not.

## What It Is

**Estimation** is predicting how much effort or time work will take. **Forecasting** is predicting when a body of work will be done, or how much will be done by a date, usually from historical data. They're related but not the same, and much of the trouble comes from using an estimate where a forecast was needed.

A few common terms:

- **Story points** are a relative measure of an item's size (effort, complexity and uncertainty together), meaningful only to the team that uses them: a 5 is roughly more than a 3 and less than an 8, usually on a Fibonacci-like scale.
- **Planning poker** is a technique for estimating in points: everyone chooses an estimate privately, all reveal at once, and the people with the highest and lowest explain their reasoning.
- **T-shirt sizing** (S, M, L, XL) is a coarser version for larger items or early ideas.
- **Velocity** is the number of points a team finishes per sprint. It's a planning aid for that team.
- **Throughput** is the number of items finished per period (28.1a), without points.
- **Monte Carlo forecasting** uses historical throughput to simulate many possible futures and gives a range of outcomes with probabilities, instead of one date.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Estimates given in conversation turn into dates in presentations.
- Big projects regularly take two or three times their estimate.
- Velocity has gone up over time while the amount of work actually finished hasn't.
- Velocity is compared between teams, or used to judge individuals or teams.
- Points are converted into hours or days.
- Estimation meetings take hours, and widely different estimates are averaged rather than discussed.
- Forecasts are a single date with no statement of confidence.
- Nobody accounts for unplanned work or scope growth when forecasting.
- Teams are asked "when will it be done?" for a fixed-date commitment, when the real question is "what can we have by then?"

## How It Works

### 1. Start from the decision

Estimates are for decisions, and different decisions need very different precision:

```
 decision                                   needs                           use
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 Is this worth doing at all?                order of magnitude              T-shirt size, reference
                                            (days, weeks, months)           to similar past work
 What fits in this sprint?                  rough relative size             points or "does it fit?"
 Do we understand this item the same way?   a conversation                  planning poker's reveal
 When will this project be done?            a range with probabilities      throughput forecast
 What can we have by a fixed date?          a range of item counts          throughput forecast
```

The team's problems came from answering the bottom two rows with tools meant for the middle ones, and from a corridor estimate answering a question nobody had asked yet.

### 2. Points and planning poker: useful for the conversation

The most valuable thing planning poker produces isn't the number. It's the moment when one person says 2 and another says 13. That gap almost always means they understand the item differently: the 13 knows about the old currency-conversion code; the 2 knows there's a library that handles it. Talking it through is refinement (28.2a) at its best.

Three habits keep it useful:

- **Reveal simultaneously.** Otherwise the first or most senior voice anchors everyone else.
- **Discuss the outliers,** then re-estimate. Don't average.
- **Keep it quick.** If the team agrees within a minute, move on. If an item is too big to estimate, it's too big to build; slice it (28.4a).

Points are relative and belong to one team. They aren't hours, and they aren't comparable between teams. Converting them to time defeats the point of relative estimation, which is that people are better at comparing sizes than predicting durations.

### 3. Velocity: a planning aid that breaks when targeted

Velocity helps a team decide roughly how much to take into a sprint. It stops helping the moment it becomes a target. This is **Goodhart's law**: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. If leadership rewards higher velocity, estimates grow. That's not dishonesty so much as drift under pressure: the borderline 3 becomes a 5 every time.

So:

- **Never compare velocity between teams.** Their points mean different things.
- **Never use it to evaluate people** (28.3a).
- **Don't set velocity targets.** If you want to know whether the team is improving, look at cycle time and throughput, which are harder to inflate (though not impossible: 28.1a).

### 4. Forecasting from throughput

For "when will it be done?", a surprisingly effective approach skips points entirely: **count items.** If items are sliced reasonably small (28.4a), the variation in their size averages out over dozens of them, and in many teams' experience, item counts forecast about as well as points, with no estimation effort.

Then, instead of dividing, simulate:

1. Take the team's recent weekly throughput (say, the last 12 weeks): `[3, 5, 4, 6, 2, 5, 4, 7, 3, 5, 4, 6]`, an average of 4.5 items a week.
2. Take the remaining items and adjust for growth. The receipts project has 48 items; past projects grew by about 20% while in progress (items split, new edge cases appeared), so plan for about 58.
3. Simulate the future thousands of times: each simulated week, pick a random week from history as that week's throughput, until the 58 items are done. Record how many weeks it took.
4. Read the results as percentiles.

For this team:

```
 naive: 48 items ÷ 4.5/week                  ≈ 11 weeks, one date, no confidence
 simulated, 48 items (no growth)             50%: 11 wks · 85%: 12 wks · 95%: 13 wks
 simulated, 58 items (20% growth)            50%: 13 wks · 85%: 14 wks · 95%: 15 wks
```

The honest answer to the director is: "About 13 weeks if things go typically; 85% likely within 14 weeks." The growth adjustment moved the date more than the variability did, which is common. Scope growth is usually the largest thing naive forecasts leave out.

Unplanned work is already in the history, as long as throughput counts only the project's items. If the team usually spends a quarter of its time on escalations, the historical throughput for project items already reflects that.

Forecasts should be **updated every week** with actual progress and remaining items. A forecast that changes as you learn isn't a failure; that's the point of having one.

### 5. The fixed-date question

Receipts can't be late. So the useful question isn't "when will all 58 items be done?" but "**how many items can we finish by the deadline?**" With about 10 weeks until the release freeze, the same simulation, run the other way, gives:

```
 items finished in 10 weeks:  95% at least 38 · 85% at least 40 · 50% at least 45
```

With 85% confidence, about 40 items. The team and product owner order the receipt items by importance and draw a line after the 40th:

```
 1–40   ─ must be in January: combined receipts, currency handling, legal wording …
 ──────── 85% line ────────
 41–45  ─ likely: extra export formats
 46–58  ─ after January: custom templates, bulk re-issue tool
```

This changes the conversation with the director from "will it be done?" to "these parts are safe, these are likely, and these we'll do afterwards." It's a decision she can actually make.

### 6. Large unknowns: reference classes and spikes

A forecast from throughput assumes the work is like past work. For something genuinely new, the team hasn't sliced it yet, and a count of items means little. Two techniques help:

- **Reference-class forecasting:** find similar past projects (inside the organization, or in published data) and use how long they actually took, not how long they were estimated to take. People systematically underestimate their own projects (the **planning fallacy**); outcomes of comparable projects correct for that.
- **Spikes:** short, timeboxed investigations of the biggest unknowns (does the payment provider support multi-currency refunds?) before committing to a range. Early in a project, uncertainty is high; the **cone of uncertainty** describes how estimates narrow as work proceeds and unknowns are resolved.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Anyone asks "when will it be done?"** or "what can we have by then?" and the answer affects a decision: a launch date, a contract, a campaign, a regulatory deadline.
- **Teams with a few months of throughput history** and reasonably small items. Monte Carlo needs data, and the method works better as items get more consistent.
- **Fixed-date commitments,** where the forecast of what fits drives scope decisions.
- **Planning poker** when items are often misunderstood between team members, as a way to surface that.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Estimating every item in points when nothing depends on it.** If sprint planning works by asking "does this fit?" and forecasts come from counts, points may add little. Many teams drop them.
- **Monte Carlo for a two-week piece of work.** Look at the items and decide.
- **Precise estimates for "is it worth doing?"** An order of magnitude settles that.
- **Detailed forecasting early in a genuinely new project** with no sliced items. Use reference classes and spikes first; simulations of made-up items produce made-up confidence.
- **Re-estimating finished work** to "calibrate" points. Look at throughput and cycle time instead.

## Trade-offs

- **Forecasts assume the future resembles the past.** A new team member, a change in how work is sliced, or a holiday season breaks that. Rolling history (the last 10 to 15 weeks) and weekly updates help; nothing makes it perfect.
- **Ranges are less satisfying than dates.** Stakeholders often want one date. Giving "85% by week 14" means explaining what that means, repeatedly, until it becomes normal.
- **Counting items depends on slicing.** If some items are a day and others a month, counts forecast badly. Throughput forecasting quietly requires the discipline from 28.4a.
- **Dropping points loses the conversation** unless you keep it some other way. Refinement still needs the "why do you think this is big?" discussion.
- **Growth factors are guesses too.** "20% growth" comes from past projects and may not apply. Forecasts should say what they assume.
- **Probabilistic forecasts can be misused as well.** "85%" can become the new promise. When it slips, the question should be what changed, not who failed.

## Alternatives

**Expert judgment.** An experienced person's estimate, informed by knowledge of the codebase and similar work. Often good for small things. For larger ones it suffers from the planning fallacy and anchoring, and it's hard to update as work proceeds.

**Bottom-up task estimates in hours.** Breaking work into tasks and adding up hours. It feels rigorous and is useful for fine-grained sprint planning, but it systematically misses integration, waiting, rework and unknowns, which is why totals usually come out short.

**#NoEstimates.** A movement arguing that estimation effort is often wasted and that small, similar-sized items plus throughput give better answers. In practice, it's close to the approach in this article: slice well, count, forecast from data, and estimate only when a decision needs it.

**Fixed time, variable scope as a rule.** Shape Up's appetite (28.1a) inverts the question entirely: decide how much time something is worth, then shape the work to fit. Forecasting becomes unnecessary for the cycle, at the cost of accepting whatever scope fits.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Stop turning estimates into promises.** Any estimate comes with its purpose and its uncertainty ("rough guess, not broken down, could be two to eight weeks"). *Signal to move on:* estimation meetings long and contentious.

**L1 — Planning poker for the conversation.** Simultaneous reveal, discuss outliers, move on quickly. Remove velocity from dashboards and performance reviews. *Signal:* stakeholders asking when a multi-sprint piece of work will be done.

**L2 — Measure throughput** (28.1a) and track how much projects grow while in progress. *Signal:* a few months of history.

**L3 — Monte Carlo forecasts** for projects and fixed dates, updated weekly and presented as ranges with confidence. *Signal:* points no longer used for anything the forecast doesn't already do better.

**L4 — Decide whether to keep points at all.** Many teams keep planning-poker conversations and drop the numbers. *Signal:* the process runs well within one team, and the harder questions are about collaboration between people and teams, which is Module 29 onward.

## Implementation

TypeScript, one piece: the Monte Carlo forecast, both ways round. It's small because the idea is small.

```ts
// Weekly throughput history: items finished per week, most recent ~12 weeks.
const pick = (xs: number[]) => xs[Math.floor(Math.random() * xs.length)];
const pct = (sorted: number[], p: number) => sorted[Math.floor(p * (sorted.length - 1))];

// "When will N items be done?"
export function weeksToFinish(history: number[], remaining: number, growth = 1, runs = 10_000) {
  const target = Math.round(remaining * growth);
  const results: number[] = [];
  for (let r = 0; r < runs; r++) {
    let left = target, weeks = 0;
    while (left > 0) { left -= pick(history); weeks++; }
    results.push(weeks);
  }
  results.sort((a, b) => a - b);
  return { p50: pct(results, 0.5), p85: pct(results, 0.85), p95: pct(results, 0.95) };
}

// "How many items by the deadline?" Read low percentiles: "at least X, with 85% confidence."
export function itemsByDeadline(history: number[], weeks: number, runs = 10_000) {
  const results: number[] = [];
  for (let r = 0; r < runs; r++) {
    let done = 0;
    for (let w = 0; w < weeks; w++) done += pick(history);
    results.push(done);
  }
  results.sort((a, b) => a - b);
  return { atLeast95: pct(results, 0.05), atLeast85: pct(results, 0.15), atLeast50: pct(results, 0.5) };
}

// weeksToFinish([3,5,4,6,2,5,4,7,3,5,4,6], 48, 1.2) → { p50: 13, p85: 14, p95: 15 }
// itemsByDeadline([3,5,4,6,2,5,4,7,3,5,4,6], 10)    → { atLeast95: 38, atLeast85: 40, atLeast50: 45 }
```

> **Aside — why the range is probably too narrow.** Picking each week independently assumes good and bad weeks are random. In reality they cluster: a production incident, a holiday, or a sick team member produces several slow weeks in a row. Independent sampling averages those out and makes the range look tighter than it is. Two practical responses: sample from history in blocks of two or three consecutive weeks, and treat holidays explicitly (the week between Christmas and New Year isn't a typical week). Also note the direction of the fixed-date reading: for "at least 40 items with 85% confidence," you take the 15th percentile of items done, not the 85th. Getting that backwards turns a safe commitment into a coin toss.

## Further Reading

- **Mike Cohn, _Agile Estimating and Planning_** (Prentice Hall, 2005), the standard reference on story points, planning poker and velocity, including their intended limits.
- **Steve McConnell, _Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art_** (Microsoft Press, 2006), on the cone of uncertainty, the difference between estimates, targets and commitments, and counting instead of guessing.
- **Daniel Vacanti, _When Will It Be Done?_** (2020), a thorough treatment of Monte Carlo forecasting from throughput, including its assumptions.
- **Troy Magennis's forecasting spreadsheets** (Focused Objective, freely available), practical Monte Carlo tools that include scope growth and split rates.
- **Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner, _How Big Things Get Done_** (Currency, 2023), on the planning fallacy and reference-class forecasting, drawn from thousands of large projects.
- **Vasco Duarte, _NoEstimates_** (2016), the argument for forecasting from counts and dropping most estimation.

---

The habit to take from this: **when someone asks "when?", ask what they'll decide with the answer, then give them a range and a confidence, not a date.** A single date is a guess dressed as a promise; a range with a probability is something people can plan around.
