# Meetings That Can Change Something: Scrum's Events as Feedback Loops

_Module 28.2a — Agile/Scrum Methodology_

## The Problem

Suppose you're on a team of eight building timetabling and attendance software for secondary schools. Teachers take registers on it, office staff build the timetable each summer, and head teachers see attendance trends. The team works in two-week sprints and holds every Scrum meeting faithfully. A year in, each meeting has quietly stopped doing its job.

**The daily standup.** It takes 30 to 35 minutes. People go round in a circle and tell the engineering manager what they did yesterday and what they'll do today. Almost everyone says "no blockers." Real blockers come up afterwards in private messages, if at all. One item, the export for the government attendance return, waited four days on a question nobody asked out loud. Most people check their phones until it's their turn.

**Sprint planning.** Three hours. The product owner arrives with 30 tickets, half of which nobody has seen before. The team estimates each one from scratch, argues about what several mean, and runs out of time. The sprint goal is written as "Complete tickets 412 to 431." Nobody checks that two developers are on leave for half the sprint.

**The sprint review.** Stakeholders used to come. Now it's the team showing slides of finished tickets to each other. Nothing is shown in a real environment. The deputy head who asked for the parent-notification feature hasn't attended in four months. In the last six reviews, nothing said changed the backlog.

**The retrospective.** The same three complaints every time: too many meetings, flaky tests, unclear requirements. Action items get written on a board and never mentioned again. When a sprint is busy, the retro is the first thing cancelled. Once, it turned into an argument about whose change broke the timetable import, and people have been careful to say nothing since.

**Refinement.** In theory, weekly. In practice, it's the whole team for two hours going through 80 backlog items, getting through about 12.

The team spends about 14% of its time in these meetings. That's not unusual, and not the real problem. The problem is that none of them changes anything: not the plan, not the backlog, not how the team works.

## What It Is

Scrum defines a small set of **events** (often called ceremonies). The Scrum Guide lists five, with the sprint itself as the container for the other four:

- **Sprint planning**, at the start: what the sprint will achieve and how.
- **The daily scrum**, every day: progress toward the goal, and what to adjust today.
- **The sprint review**, at the end: what was built, with stakeholders, and what to do next.
- **The sprint retrospective**, after the review: how the team worked, and what to change.

**Backlog refinement** is an ongoing activity rather than a formal event, but it has the same character.

Each event is a **feedback loop**: it inspects something specific and exists to allow a specific decision. That's the frame that makes them work. An event where no decision is possible, or where the inputs needed for the decision aren't in the room, is a status meeting wearing a Scrum name.

```
 event           inspects                         can change                  must be present
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 planning        goal, ready items, capacity      what's in the sprint, how   team, product owner
 daily           progress toward the goal         today's plan, who helps     the team (developers)
 review          the increment, in use            the backlog, priorities     team, stakeholders, users
 retrospective   how we worked, with data         our process, one or two     the team
                                                  experiments
 refinement      upcoming items                   their size, clarity, order  PO + a few developers
```

## Recognizing the Problem

- The daily standup is a round of individual status reports to one person.
- Blockers are rarely raised in the standup and usually resolved outside it.
- Planning is mostly estimating tickets the team is seeing for the first time.
- The sprint goal is a list of ticket numbers.
- Stakeholders have stopped attending the review, or the review shows slides rather than software.
- Nothing in the backlog changes as a result of the review.
- Retrospectives produce the same complaints and action items that nobody follows up.
- The retrospective is the first meeting cancelled in a busy sprint.
- People describe the meetings as overhead and can't name a decision one of them produced recently.

## How It Works

### 1. The daily scrum: walk the board, not the room

The daily exists so the team can adjust today's plan to meet the sprint goal. A round of "yesterday, today, blockers" answers a different question (is everyone busy?) and is answered for a manager rather than for the team.

The more useful form **walks the board from right to left**: start with items closest to done, then work back. For each: what does it need to finish? Is anyone blocked? Has it been in progress longer than usual (28.1a's aging items)? People speak when their item comes up, not in turn.

```
 Testing        Review          In progress       → walk this way ←
   #512 (3d)      #518 (5d) ⚠     #521 (1d)
                  #509 (2d)       #530 (6d) ⚠ blocked: waiting on DfE schema answer
```

Two habits keep it at 15 minutes:

- **Problems are identified in the meeting and solved after it.** Whoever is needed stays for the follow-up; everyone else leaves.
- **The manager doesn't run it.** If people are reporting to someone, they'll report activity. If the team is planning its own day, they'll talk about the work.

Distributed teams across several time zones often do better with a short written check-in against the board plus a brief call only when something is blocked (Module 32).

### 2. Sprint planning: goal, capacity, ready work

Planning answers three questions in order.

**Why is this sprint valuable?** The sprint goal is a single outcome: "Office staff can produce the termly attendance return without a spreadsheet." It lets the team decide, mid-sprint, what to drop without breaking the point of the sprint.

**What can be done?** That depends on capacity and on history. Capacity means actual available days: leave, on-call, the share of unplanned work measured in 28.1a. History means what the team has actually finished in recent sprints, not what it hoped to (forecasting is 28.5).

**How will it be done?** A rough plan for the first few days is enough. The rest emerges.

Planning is short when the items arrive **ready**: understood, small enough to finish in a few days, with open questions answered. That work happens in refinement, before planning, so planning isn't where the team sees tickets for the first time.

### 3. The sprint review: show working software to people who can change the plan

The review inspects the increment, the working software built so far, and adapts the backlog. It needs three things the team's review had lost:

- **Real software, in a real environment,** ideally used by the people it's for. A deputy head trying the parent-notification flow for five minutes produces more feedback than twenty slides.
- **The people who decide priorities, and people who use the product.** If they've stopped coming, it's usually because nothing they said ever changed anything. Fix that first, and invite fewer people who matter more.
- **Evidence beyond the demo:** usage of what shipped last sprint, relevant feedback and support themes (27.3a), what changed in the market or the school calendar.

The output is a changed backlog: something reordered, added, dropped or split. A useful test: if the last three reviews changed nothing, the review isn't a feedback loop.

### 4. The retrospective: experiments, not complaints

The retrospective inspects how the team worked (people, process, tools, the definition of done) and chooses changes. Recurring complaints with no follow-up usually mean one of three things:

- **The actions are too big.** "Fix flaky tests" is a project. "Quarantine the five flakiest tests this sprint and track reruns" is an experiment.
- **Nobody owns them.** Each change needs an owner and a check at the next retrospective: did we do it, and did it help?
- **The problem is outside the team's control.** "Unclear requirements" may come from how the organization works. Then the action is to take it to whoever can change it, with evidence, and report back.

Aim for one or two experiments per retrospective, not a list of ten. Ground the conversation in data where you can: the flow report from 28.1a, carried-over work, escaped defects.

Retros only work if people can say what happened without fear. The long-standing norm (from Norm Kerth's work on retrospectives) is to assume everyone did the best they could with what they knew at the time, and to examine the system rather than individuals. The same principle underlies blameless postmortems (24.3). Varying the format helps too; the same questions every two weeks produce the same answers.

The retrospective is also the worst meeting to cancel when things are busy. A busy sprint is when the process is most visibly failing, and the retro is where that gets addressed.

### 5. Refinement: a few people, a little ahead

Refinement keeps the next one or two sprints' worth of items ready. The whole team going through 80 items is expensive and tiring. A better pattern is the product owner with two or three developers (rotating), working on the top of the backlog a few days before planning, then a short session where the whole team sees what's coming and raises concerns.

"Ready" doesn't mean fully specified. It means the team understands it well enough to start and finish it in a few days, with questions answered by the people who can answer them. Slicing work that small is covered in 28.4.

### 6. The total cost

The Scrum Guide sets maximum durations for a one-month sprint (planning 8 hours, review 4, retrospective 3), usually scaled down for shorter sprints. For this team's two-week sprint:

```
 planning   ~4 h │ review ~2 h │ retro ~1.5 h │ dailies 10 × 15 min = 2.5 h
 ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
 ≈ 10 h of an 80 h sprint ≈ 12.5%, plus refinement for a few people
```

That's close to what the team was already spending. The difference isn't the time; it's whether each of those hours can change something.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any team using sprints.** The events are what make the sprint a feedback loop rather than a two-week deadline.
- **Teams where meetings have become routine and nobody remembers a decision from them.** The table in "What It Is" is a quick audit.
- **Products with real users available** (school staff, in this case) who can take part in reviews.
- **Teams whose problems keep recurring,** which is what a working retrospective is for.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Flow-based teams (28.1a) running sprint events on no sprint.** They still need feedback loops, just different ones: a regular replenishment meeting to choose what enters the queue, a delivery review, and a retrospective. Copying sprint planning onto continuous flow adds ceremony with no timebox to plan.
- **Two or three people working together all day.** The daily scrum is the conversation they're already having.
- **Full-length events for short sprints.** A one-week sprint doesn't need a four-hour review.
- **Adding more meetings to fix meetings.** A "pre-planning" before planning, or a "retro on the retro," usually means an existing event isn't doing its job.

## Trade-offs

- **The events cost real time,** about an eighth of a team's capacity. That's only worth it if they produce decisions; otherwise it's pure overhead.
- **They fragment the day.** A daily at 10:00 breaks a morning of focused work. Many teams put it at the start of the day or just after lunch for that reason.
- **Reviews depend on people outside the team.** Stakeholders and users are busy. Keeping them engaged means making every review worth their time, and sometimes going to them instead.
- **Retrospectives need trust that takes time to build** and can be lost in one bad session. Facilitation matters, and a manager's presence can change what people are willing to say.
- **Fixed cadence can lag reality.** A decision that's needed on day three shouldn't wait for day ten's review. The events are minimum feedback points, not the only ones.
- **Ritual drift is the default.** Every event slowly becomes a routine unless someone keeps asking what it's for.

## Alternatives

**Written, asynchronous updates.** Daily check-ins, planning proposals and review notes in writing, with meetings only for disagreements and decisions. It works well for distributed teams and people who think better in writing. It loses the quick back-and-forth that resolves ambiguity in minutes (Module 32).

**Kanban cadences.** Flow-based teams usually hold replenishment (what enters the queue), delivery planning, a service delivery review (are we meeting customers' expectations?), and retrospectives, each on its own schedule. Same feedback-loop idea, decoupled from a sprint.

**Shape Up's betting table and cooldown.** Instead of sprint planning every two weeks, a small group decides what to commit to for six weeks, with a two-week cooldown for fixes and exploration. Fewer planning events, more at stake in each.

**Continuous improvement without formal retros.** Some teams keep a visible board of improvement ideas and act on them as they come. It works when improvement genuinely happens continuously; for most teams, a fixed moment to stop and reflect is what makes it happen at all.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Audit.** For each event, write down what it inspects and the last decision it produced. *Signal to move on:* one or more events with no recent decision.

**L1 — Fix the daily first.** Walk the board right to left, flag blocked and aging items, solve problems afterwards with the right people. It's the most frequent event, so it's the cheapest to experiment with. *Signal:* planning still mostly estimating unfamiliar tickets.

**L2 — Move understanding into refinement.** A small rotating group gets the top items ready before planning; planning starts from a goal and real capacity. *Signal:* stakeholders absent from the review.

**L3 — Make the review worth attending.** Working software, the right few people, evidence from real use, and a visible backlog change at the end. *Signal:* the same complaints in every retro.

**L4 — Retrospectives as experiments.** One or two changes, each with an owner and a check at the next retro, grounded in flow data. *Signal:* the events work but decisions still stall, often because it's unclear who decides what, which is 28.3.

## Implementation

TypeScript, one small piece: generating the daily's agenda from the board, so the meeting starts from the work rather than the people. Most trackers can provide this data.

```ts
type Stage = "ready" | "in_progress" | "review" | "testing" | "done";
type Card = { id: string; title: string; stage: Stage; enteredStageAt: Date;
              startedAt?: Date; blockedReason?: string; assignees: string[] };

const ORDER: Stage[] = ["testing", "review", "in_progress"];   // right to left
const DAY = 86_400_000;

export function dailyAgenda(cards: Card[], p85CycleDays: number, now = new Date()) {
  return ORDER.flatMap((stage) =>
    cards
      .filter((c) => c.stage === stage)
      .map((c) => {
        const ageDays = c.startedAt ? (+now - +c.startedAt) / DAY : 0;
        return {
          id: c.id, stage, title: c.title, assignees: c.assignees,
          ageDays: Math.round(ageDays),
          flags: [
            c.blockedReason && `blocked: ${c.blockedReason}`,
            ageDays > p85CycleDays && "older than 85% of finished items",
            stage === "review" && (+now - +c.enteredStageAt) / DAY > 1 && "waiting for review > 1 day",
          ].filter(Boolean) as string[],
        };
      })
      // Within a stage, oldest first: the item closest to done and most stuck leads.
      .sort((a, b) => b.ageDays - a.ageDays),
  );
}
```

> **Aside — what the agenda leaves out.** It lists work, not people, on purpose. Someone with no card on the board doesn't come up, which is either fine (they're on support this week) or a sign that their work isn't visible (28.1a's L0). And the flags are prompts, not verdicts: an item older than the 85th percentile may be old for a good reason. The point is that it gets mentioned, rather than sitting unnoticed for four days like the attendance export.
