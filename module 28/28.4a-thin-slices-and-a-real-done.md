# Thin Slices and a Real "Done": The Backlog and the Definition of Done

_Module 28.4a — Agile/Scrum Methodology_

## The Problem

Suppose you're on a team of six building software for small landlords: rent collection, maintenance requests from tenants, and documents like tenancy agreements. About 9,000 landlords use it, most with between one and ten properties.

The team has a backlog, a definition of done and a definition of ready. All three are getting in the way.

**A backlog nobody can read.** It has 640 items. The oldest is four years old. The product owner looks at the top 30; nobody looks below 100. Stakeholders who asked for something are told "it's on the backlog," which they hear as a promise. Twenty-two items are some version of "better rent reports." Each refinement session starts by scrolling.

**Items cut into layers.** The top priority is rent reminders: tenants get a message before rent is due. The team splits it the way the code is organized:

```
 #801 Reminder schedule: database schema
 #802 Reminder API endpoints
 #803 Reminder settings screen
 #804 Reminder email sending job
 #805 Reminder SMS
```

Each is "done" in a different sprint. After three sprints, no landlord has received anything. When the settings screen is finally built, it needs something the API doesn't provide, and #802 is reopened.

**Done means "merged."** The definition of done is "code reviewed and merged." Testing happens the following sprint in a shared staging environment; releases go out monthly. Translations for the Welsh-language version, help articles (27.2a), analytics events and accessibility checks are "follow-ups." About 30% of each sprint goes to fixing things marked done in earlier sprints. There are 70 open follow-ups.

**A definition of ready that works as a wall.** To stop unclear items entering sprints, the team adopted a 12-point definition of ready: final designs, all edge cases listed, estimates, dependencies resolved, sign-off from the stakeholder, and more. Items now bounce back from planning for missing points. The product owner spends most of her week filling in templates, and designs are "final" before anyone has built anything. The team has reinvented a small waterfall at the front of each sprint.

Each problem is about the shape of work: how big items are, what they cut across, and what it means to finish one.

## What It Is

The **product backlog** is the ordered list of what the team might do next to reach the product goal. It isn't a record of every idea or a set of promises. It's the product owner's current best guess at the order of valuable work, detailed at the top and coarse further down.

A **vertical slice** is an item that cuts through every layer needed to deliver something usable (interface, logic, data, delivery), but only a thin portion of each. The opposite, a **horizontal** item, completes one layer and delivers nothing on its own.

The **definition of done** is the quality standard every item must meet before it counts as finished. When an item meets it, it becomes part of the **increment**, the usable, releasable product the team inspects at the review (28.2a).

Two nearby ideas are often confused with these:

- **Acceptance criteria** belong to one item: what this particular change must do. The definition of done applies to all items: the quality every change must have. "Reminders are sent three days before the due date" is an acceptance criterion; "works with a screen reader and is translated" is part of the definition of done.
- **A definition of ready** is a team convention, not part of Scrum, for when an item is understood well enough to start. It's useful as a short checklist and harmful as a gate, as the landlord team found.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The backlog has hundreds of items, many older than a year, and nobody reads past the top few dozen.
- Stakeholders treat "it's on the backlog" as a commitment.
- Items are named after layers or components ("API for…", "schema for…"), and a feature is usable only when all of them are done.
- Single items regularly span more than one sprint.
- "Done" items are reopened, or a large share of each sprint fixes last sprint's work.
- There's a growing list of "follow-ups": tests, translations, docs, accessibility, monitoring.
- The increment can't be released without a separate hardening or testing phase.
- Items are routinely rejected at planning for failing a readiness checklist.
- User stories follow the template but don't say anything ("As a landlord I want reminders so that reminders are sent").

## How It Works

### 1. The backlog as a short, ordered list of options

A useful backlog has a shape:

```
 top      │ next 2–3 sprints   small, sliced, understood, ordered precisely
          │ next 1–3 months    medium items, roughly sized, ordered roughly
          │ later              a handful of broad themes or needs
 bottom   │ (not here)         ideas and requests → the feedback system (27.3a)
```

The rule that keeps it that way: **the backlog holds what the product owner intends to do, not everything anyone has suggested.** Ideas, requests and needs live in the feedback system, with their evidence and weights (27.3a). An item moves into the backlog when the product owner decides it's worth doing soon. Stakeholders can still see their request, and whether it's been accepted or declined.

Two habits prevent regrowth:

- **Expiry.** Items untouched for, say, six months are archived automatically. If they matter, they'll come back with fresh evidence.
- **A size limit.** Some teams cap the backlog at a few months of work. If it's full, something must leave before something enters, which forces the ordering decision rather than deferring it.

The product goal sits above the backlog. It's the reason for the ordering: "Landlords collect rent on time without chasing tenants" makes reminders, payment links and late-fee handling obviously relevant, and a tenancy-agreement template redesign obviously not, this quarter.

### 2. Vertical slicing

A vertical slice is small enough to finish in a few days, valuable or informative on its own, and usable by someone. Rent reminders sliced vertically:

```
 1. One email reminder, 3 days before the due date, fixed text, only for tenants
    with an outstanding balance at send time, for landlords who switch it on.
 2. Landlords choose how many days before (1, 3 or 7).
 3. A second reminder on the due date if still unpaid.
 4. SMS as well as email.
 5. Landlords edit the reminder text.
```

Slice 1 is thin but real: it touches the schema, a job, a template, a setting and delivery, and landlords can use it. After one sprint the team learns things the layered plan would have found in month three: how many landlords switch it on, whether tenants complain, and whether payment-on-time moves. Slice 5 might never be needed.

Note what slice 1 does not leave out: checking the balance at send time. A reminder to a tenant who has already paid is worse than no reminder. **Thin doesn't mean unsafe;** a slice must still be correct for what it does.

Patterns that help find slices:

- **Workflow steps:** do the first step end to end, then the next.
- **Simple rules first:** a fixed three days before configurable schedules.
- **One variation first:** email before SMS, one currency before several, one property type before all.
- **Happy path first,** as long as the unhappy paths fail safely.
- **Manual first:** the first version of a report can be generated by a script someone runs weekly, before building the screen.
- **Spikes separately:** if something is truly unknown (can our SMS provider handle 10,000 messages at 09:00?), a short timeboxed investigation answers that, and the answer shapes the slices.

A widely used check for items is **INVEST**: independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, testable. "Valuable" and "small" are the two that layered items fail.

### 3. Items that say something

The user-story template ("As a …, I want …, so that …") is a prompt for a conversation, not a specification. The idea behind stories is often summarized as **card, conversation, confirmation**: a short card to remember the item, a conversation to understand it, and confirmation in the form of acceptance criteria that say how you'll know it works.

Acceptance criteria are most useful as **examples**:

```
Given a tenant whose rent of £850 is due on 1 June and who has paid £0,
when the reminder job runs on 29 May,
then they receive the reminder email with the amount and a payment link.

Given the same tenant has paid £850 by 28 May,
when the job runs on 29 May,
then no reminder is sent.
```

Examples make edge cases concrete, turn directly into tests, and get developers, testers and the product owner to agree on the same thing in a few minutes.

### 4. The definition of done

The definition of done is a checklist every item passes before it counts as done. For the landlord team, a reasonable version might be:

```
 □ acceptance criteria met, with automated tests
 □ reviewed by someone other than the author
 □ passes accessibility checks (automated + screen-reader check for new UI)
 □ Welsh and English text present
 □ help article updated if behavior changed (27.2a)
 □ analytics events defined for new user actions
 □ monitoring/alerts for new jobs or integrations
 □ deployed to production (behind a flag if not yet released)
```

Two things make it work.

**Undone work doesn't disappear.** Anything left out of the definition of done still has to happen, later, in bulk, by people who've forgotten the context. The 70 follow-ups and the 30% of each sprint spent on fixes are the undone work from "merged" being called done. Moving those items into the definition makes each item bigger and the team's measured throughput lower at first; it also stops the hidden backlog growing.

**It should grow with the team's capability.** A team with a monthly manual release can't say "deployed to production" on day one. It can start with "deployed to staging and tested there," invest in its pipeline (Module 30), and tighten the definition as release gets cheaper. With feature flags (26.2b), "in production behind a flag" becomes achievable, which separates **done** (the team's standard) from **released** (a product decision, 28.3a).

Organizations with several teams usually set a baseline (security, accessibility, legal) that every team's definition includes, and let teams add to it.

### 5. Readiness without a wall

Items do need to be understood before a sprint, and refinement (28.2a) is where that happens. A **short** readiness check helps: the team understands what it's for, it looks small enough for a few days, and anything it depends on is known.

It becomes a gate when it requires things that can only be known by building: final designs, every edge case, full sign-off. Then items wait at the front of the sprint while the product owner fills in fields, and the team is back to specifying everything in advance. If items keep arriving unclear, the fix is usually more and earlier conversation between the product owner and developers, not a longer checklist.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Backlogs that have grown past what anyone reads,** or that stakeholders treat as a promise list.
- **Features that take several sprints before anyone can use them,** because work is split by layer.
- **Teams where "done" items keep coming back,** or where a hardening phase is needed before release.
- **Products with quality obligations** (accessibility, localization, regulatory standards) that are currently treated as follow-ups.
- **Teams arguing at planning** about whether items are ready.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Expiry rules and size caps on a backlog of 40 items.** Just read it.
- **Formal user-story templates** for a team that talks to the product owner every day. A clear title and a few examples are enough.
- **A definition of done the team can't meet yet.** Writing "deployed to production" when releases are monthly and manual just means every item fails it. Start with what's achievable and improve the pipeline.
- **Slicing work that is already small.** A one-day change doesn't need decomposing into three.
- **A definition of ready at all,** for teams where refinement already makes items clear.

## Trade-offs

- **Vertical slices can mean revisiting code.** Building reminders in five slices touches the job and template five times. Some of that is rework a big design might have avoided; the trade is that you learn which of the five you need.
- **Thin slices tempt teams to ship incomplete experiences.** A reminder feature with no way to turn it off is thin and bad. Slices need product judgment, not just technical cutting.
- **A strong definition of done lowers apparent speed.** Each item takes longer, and velocity or throughput drops when the definition tightens. Leadership sees a slowdown; the actual effect is that hidden work becomes visible.
- **Pruning the backlog upsets people.** Archiving a stakeholder's four-year-old request feels like rejection. Being clear that it's declined, and why, is kinder than keeping it forever, but it's still a conversation.
- **Keeping the definition of done current takes effort.** It's easy to write once and forget, or to let items quietly skip parts under pressure. It needs checking at retrospectives.
- **Examples take time to write.** Acceptance criteria as concrete scenarios are more work up front than one-line descriptions, and they pay back only if they're used as tests.

## Alternatives

**Story mapping.** Laying out user activities left to right and slices top to bottom (Jeff Patton's method) gives a two-dimensional view that a flat list can't: the whole journey, and which thin slice across it ships first. Useful when planning a larger feature or a new product.

**Job stories.** "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]" replaces the persona with the situation. Useful when the same person needs very different things in different contexts.

**Impact mapping and opportunity trees.** Starting from a goal and working down through the people who affect it, what they need to do differently, and what could help. They connect backlog items to outcomes more explicitly than a list does.

**Shape Up's pitches.** Instead of a backlog, a small set of shaped proposals with a time budget, bet on each cycle. Anything not chosen is discarded rather than kept. It removes backlog maintenance entirely, at the cost of losing a standing list.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Prune the backlog.** Move ideas and requests to the feedback system (27.3a), archive anything untouched for six months, and keep what the product owner intends to do soon. *Signal to move on:* features that take several sprints before anyone can use them.

**L1 — Slice vertically.** For the next feature, find a first slice that's usable in one sprint, and write acceptance criteria as examples. *Signal:* "done" items coming back.

**L2 — Write the definition of done honestly,** including everything currently treated as a follow-up that the team can realistically do now. Put the rest on a list for improving the pipeline. *Signal:* the definition can't include release because releasing is too expensive.

**L3 — Tighten the definition as delivery improves:** staging, then production behind flags. Separate done from released. *Signal:* planning blocked by readiness arguments.

**L4 — Replace any readiness gate with earlier conversation:** refinement a few days ahead, a three-point check at most. *Signal:* items are small and truly done, but forecasting when a larger feature will finish is still guesswork, which is 28.5.

## Implementation

TypeScript, one small piece: automating the parts of the definition of done a machine can check, as a pull-request check. Everything else stays a checklist in the pull request template, because a checklist is the right tool for judgment calls.

```ts
// Runs in CI on each pull request. Each check returns a problem or null.
type Change = { files: string[]; newStrings: string[]; newRoutes: string[];
                newJobs: string[]; newEvents: string[] };

const checks: Array<(c: Change, repo: Repo) => Promise<string | null>> = [
  async (c) => (c.files.some(isSource) && !c.files.some(isTest))
    ? "Source changed but no tests changed" : null,

  async (c, repo) => {
    const missing = c.newStrings.filter((k) => !repo.translations.cy.has(k));
    return missing.length ? `Missing Welsh text for: ${missing.join(", ")}` : null;
  },

  async (c, repo) => {
    const untracked = c.newRoutes.filter((r) => !repo.helpTopics.coversRoute(r));
    return untracked.length ? `New screens without a help topic (27.2a): ${untracked.join(", ")}` : null;
  },

  async (c, repo) => {
    const unwatched = c.newJobs.filter((j) => !repo.alerts.has(j));
    return unwatched.length ? `New jobs without an alert: ${unwatched.join(", ")}` : null;
  },

  async (c, repo) => (await repo.a11y.run(c.newRoutes)).violations > 0
    ? "Automated accessibility checks failed" : null,
];

export async function definitionOfDone(change: Change, repo: Repo) {
  const problems = (await Promise.all(checks.map((f) => f(change, repo)))).filter(Boolean);
  return { pass: problems.length === 0, problems };
}
```

> **Aside — what automation can't check.** Automated accessibility tools catch a fraction of real problems (missing labels, low contrast), not whether a screen-reader user can actually buy a ticket or pay rent. "Tests changed" doesn't mean the right tests exist. The automated checks catch forgetting; the human parts of the checklist catch judgment, and the check should say which is which, so a green build isn't read as "done." Also watch for the check becoming the target: a trivial test added to pass "tests changed" satisfies the machine and defeats the point.

The habit to take from this: **before starting any piece of work, ask what the smallest version someone could actually use looks like, and what "finished" will mean for it.** Most late projects were big from the first item and "done" long before they were finished.
