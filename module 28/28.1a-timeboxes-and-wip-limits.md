# Timeboxes and WIP Limits: The Two Levers Behind Scrum and Kanban

_Module 28.1a — Agile/Scrum Methodology_

## The Problem

Suppose you're on a team of seven building maintenance software for companies that run fleets of delivery vans. Mechanics log inspections on tablets, fleet managers schedule services, and the system warns when a van is due for brakes or tyres.

**Year one: the big plan.** The team spends six weeks writing a specification for a "fleet health" release: a dashboard, predictive service intervals, parts ordering, and an integration with two telematics providers. Then five months of building. It ships in November. Within a month:

- Fleet managers use the dashboard, but the predicted intervals are ignored because they don't match how their mechanics already work.
- Parts ordering is used by two customers. Everyone else orders parts by phone from a local supplier and always will.
- One telematics integration turns out to be what most customers wanted. It was the last thing built, and it's half finished because the schedule ran out.

Nothing was built badly. The team learned what customers valued in December, about something decided in June. Every decision in between was made without that information.

**Year two: "we're agile now."** The team adopts two-week sprints. Six months later:

- Sprint planning packs in 40 points of work. About 22 get finished. The rest "carry over," and sprints are now the same plan, relabeled every two weeks.
- Around a third of each sprint goes to escalations from support (27.1b) and urgent customer requests nobody planned for. The sprint goal is abandoned in most sprints by the second Wednesday.
- The board shows 23 items in progress for seven people. Everyone is busy. Pull requests wait days for review, because reviewers are busy with their own work in progress.
- An item takes, on average, 19 days from someone starting it to being done. Customers are told "next sprint" about the same request three sprints running.

**Year two and a half: "let's do Kanban instead."** The team drops sprints and keeps the board. Without the sprint boundary, even more gets started. Cycle time rises to 26 days.

Both attempts changed the ceremony and kept the underlying problem: **too much work started, too little finished, and feedback arriving long after the decisions it should have shaped.**

## What It Is

**Agile** is the family of approaches that deliver software in small increments and use what each increment teaches to steer the next. The shared idea, from the 2001 Agile Manifesto onward, is that in software, requirements and understanding change while you work, so a process should make change cheap and learning frequent, rather than trying to eliminate change with a better plan.

Scrum and Kanban are the two most widely used ways of doing that, and they're easiest to understand as two different **control mechanisms** for the same problem:

- **Scrum controls work with a timebox.** Fix a short period (the sprint, usually one to four weeks), agree on a goal and a set of work that fits, finish it, inspect what you built and how you worked, and adjust. Time is fixed; scope flexes.
- **Kanban controls work with a work-in-progress (WIP) limit.** Cap how many items can be in each stage at once. New work is only pulled in when something finishes. Work flows continuously; the limit keeps it from piling up.

Both force the team to finish before starting more. The sprint does it by bounding a batch in time; the WIP limit does it by bounding concurrency. A team can use either, or both.

The rest of Scrum's structure (its events, roles and artifacts) and estimation get their own articles, 28.2 to 28.5. This one is about the two levers and how to choose.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Big releases after months of work, and the first real customer feedback arrives after they ship.
- Sprints routinely end with a large share of work "carried over."
- The board has more items in progress than people, and everyone describes themselves as busy.
- Work waits in hidden queues: "in review," "ready for QA," "blocked on another team," with no limit on any of them.
- Unplanned work regularly breaks sprint plans, and nobody measures how much of it there is.
- The team can't say how long a typical item takes from start to done, or the answer is weeks for items everyone calls small.
- Switching frameworks changed the meetings but not the delivery times.

## How It Works

### 1. Why small batches: the cost of learning late

Every item of work carries risk: that it's wrong, unwanted, or harder than it looks. That risk is only resolved when the item reaches someone who can judge it, whether a user, a reviewer or production. The larger the batch, the longer every item waits for that judgment, and the more work is built on assumptions nobody has checked.

```
Big batch:    [ spec ][────────── build all ──────────][ship]  → learn
                                                               ↑ month 7

Small batches: [build][ship]→learn [build][ship]→learn [build][ship]→learn …
                              ↑ week 2       ↑ week 4       ↑ week 6
```

Small batches don't make each decision better. They make each wrong decision cheaper, because it's found before much else depends on it. The telematics integration would have been built first if the team had shipped something in week three and watched what customers did with it.

That's why the technical practices matter as much as the process: small pull requests, trunk-based development (Module 29) and continuous delivery (Module 30) are what make small batches cheap enough to ship.

### 2. Why WIP matters: Little's Law

In a stable system, three quantities are tied together:

```
average cycle time  =  average work in progress  /  average throughput
```

This is **Little's Law**, from queueing theory. For the team above: 23 items in progress, finishing about 1.2 items per working day, gives 23 / 1.2 ≈ 19 days per item, which is exactly what they saw.

The useful part is what it says about the levers. Working harder barely moves throughput. Lowering WIP moves cycle time directly: with 8 items in progress at the same throughput, cycle time would be about 7 days. In practice throughput often **rises** when WIP falls, because less work in progress means less switching between tasks, shorter review queues, fewer merge conflicts and earlier integration.

Too much WIP also hides problems. With 23 items open, a blocked item is one of many; with 8, it's obvious, and someone swarms it.

### 3. The timebox lever (Scrum)

A sprint bounds the batch in time. At the start, the team agrees a **sprint goal**, a single outcome ("mechanics can log a tyre inspection offline"), and selects work that serves it. At the end, it shows the result to stakeholders and reflects on how it worked. The cadence creates regular points where plans meet reality.

The timebox works when:

- **Most work can be planned two weeks ahead.** If a third of capacity goes to unplanned work, a sprint commitment is fiction. Either measure and reserve that capacity, or route interrupts elsewhere.
- **The goal is an outcome, not a list.** A list of 14 tickets can be partially done; a goal is met or it isn't, and it gives the team a basis for deciding what to drop mid-sprint.
- **Unfinished work isn't simply carried over.** Carrying over is a signal the batch was too big, and the planning should change next time.

### 4. The WIP lever (Kanban)

Kanban makes the workflow explicit and limits each stage:

```
 Ready │ In progress (≤4) │ Review (≤2) │ Testing (≤2) │ Done
───────┼──────────────────┼─────────────┼──────────────┼─────
  ▢▢▢  │  ▣ ▣ ▣ ▣         │  ▣ ▣        │  ▣           │ ▣▣▣▣
       │  (full: finish   │  (full: go  │              │
       │   or help first) │   review)   │              │
```

When "In progress" is full, nobody starts anything new. They help finish or review what's there. When "Review" is full, developers review before starting their next item. The limits turn queues that were invisible into a signal about where the bottleneck is.

Kanban's measures are about flow: **cycle time** (start to done, per item), **throughput** (items finished per week), **WIP**, and **work item age** (how long items currently in progress have been there). Aging items are the early warning: an item that's been in progress for twice the usual cycle time needs attention now, not at the next meeting.

Without limits, a Kanban board is just a visualization. That's why the team's switch made things worse: they removed the sprint boundary, the only thing limiting their WIP, and added nothing in its place.

### 5. Choosing, and combining

The deciding question is **how predictable the incoming work is.**

- **Mostly planned product work, stakeholders who want a regular review, a team still learning to slice work small:** sprints. The cadence gives structure and rhythm.
- **Mostly unplanned or interrupt-driven work** (support escalations, operations, platform teams serving other teams): flow with WIP limits. Sprint commitments would be broken constantly.
- **Mixed:** keep a cadence for planning, review and retrospective, add WIP limits within the sprint, and track unplanned work as its own lane with its own capacity. Some teams call this Scrumban; the label doesn't matter.

Whichever you choose, measure cycle time and throughput. They tell you whether it's working, independent of which meetings you hold.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Product work where users' reactions change what you should build next,** which is most product work.
- **Teams of roughly 3 to 10 people** working on a shared product, where coordination and finishing are the real constraints.
- **Cycle times of weeks for work people call small,** with many items in progress. Little's Law says WIP is the first lever.
- **Stakeholders who keep asking "when?"** A regular cadence or measured throughput gives an evidence-based answer (28.5).
- **A team where a large share of work is unplanned,** which is the case for WIP limits and a separate lane for interrupts.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **One or two developers working closely with a customer.** A shared list and daily conversation provide what the framework is for.
- **Short, well-understood, fixed-scope work** (a two-week migration with a clear checklist). Just do the checklist.
- **Full Scrum ceremony for a research spike.** Timebox the spike itself and decide at the end; sprint planning and review add nothing.
- **Adopting a framework because it's expected.** Holding the meetings without limiting work or shortening feedback loops produces the year-two team: all the ritual, none of the effect.
- **Scaled frameworks for one team.** Multi-team coordination frameworks exist for real multi-team problems. A single team adopting one gets overhead and no benefit.

## Trade-offs

- **Small batches need engineering investment.** Shipping every week or two requires automated tests (Module 21), a reliable pipeline (Module 30) and the skill of slicing work into small, valuable pieces. Without those, a sprint is a small waterfall.
- **Timeboxes create artificial deadlines.** Sprint boundaries encourage rushing at the end and splitting work in unnatural places to "fit." They also add planning overhead every cycle.
- **WIP limits feel slow at first.** Being told not to start something when you're idle is uncomfortable, and the benefit (shorter cycle time) arrives a week or two later. Teams often drop limits before they see it.
- **Flow gives less natural rhythm.** Without sprints, reviews and retrospectives have to be scheduled deliberately or they stop happening.
- **Frequent change can feel like no plan.** Stakeholders used to fixed roadmaps may read iteration as indecision. You need a way to communicate direction beyond the current sprint (roadmaps, forecasts from throughput).
- **Metrics can be gamed.** Throughput rises if you split items smaller; cycle time falls if you only start easy things. Use them to understand the system, not to judge people.

## Alternatives

**Plan-driven (waterfall) delivery.** Up-front requirements and design, then build, then test. Reasonable when requirements are genuinely fixed and change is expensive: regulated systems with certification, integration with hardware on its own schedule, or contractual fixed scope. Even there, building and testing in increments inside the plan reduces risk.

**Shape Up.** Basecamp's method: six-week cycles, work "shaped" in advance with a fixed time budget ("appetite") rather than an estimate, a betting table that chooses what goes into each cycle, and a two-week cooldown between cycles. It suits teams that find two-week sprints too short for meaningful work and too heavy in planning.

**Extreme Programming (XP).** Short iterations plus technical practices: test-driven development, pair programming, continuous integration, small releases. Often combined with Scrum or Kanban, since those say little about how to write the code.

**No named framework.** A small team with a board, a WIP limit, a weekly look at cycle time and a habit of shipping small can be entirely effective. The levers matter more than the label.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Make all work visible.** One board with everything the team does, including support escalations, operational work and "quick favors." *Signal to move on:* the board shows more items in progress than people.

**L1 — Define "done" and measure flow.** Record when each item starts and finishes; compute cycle time, throughput and WIP weekly. *Signal:* cycle times in weeks for small items, or a large share of unplanned work.

**L2 — Limit WIP.** Start with a limit near the number of people, including review and testing stages, and lower it once the team is comfortable. *Signal:* items waiting in one stage, which shows the bottleneck.

**L3 — Choose the cadence.** Measure the share of unplanned work over a month. If it's small, use sprints with outcome goals. If it's large, use flow with a separate lane and reserved capacity for interrupts, and schedule reviews and retrospectives on a fixed rhythm. *Signal:* stakeholders asking when things will be done.

**L4 — Shrink the batch further.** Smaller items, smaller pull requests, more frequent releases, feedback from real use. *Signal:* the team spends its time on the right things but can't forecast delivery, which is 28.5.

## Implementation

TypeScript. One small piece: computing flow metrics from a board's history, so that decisions about WIP and cadence rest on numbers rather than impressions. Most trackers can export when each item changed state.

```ts
type Item = { id: string; startedAt?: Date; doneAt?: Date; unplanned: boolean };

const DAY = 86_400_000;

export function flowReport(items: Item[], from: Date, to: Date) {
  const done = items.filter((i) => i.startedAt && i.doneAt && i.doneAt >= from && i.doneAt < to);
  const wip = items.filter((i) => i.startedAt && !i.doneAt);

  const cycleDays = done.map((i) => (+i.doneAt! - +i.startedAt!) / DAY).sort((a, b) => a - b);
  const workingDays = workingDaysBetween(from, to);
  const throughputPerDay = done.length / workingDays;

  return {
    throughputPerWeek: +(throughputPerDay * 5).toFixed(1),
    cycleTimeP50: percentile(cycleDays, 0.5),
    cycleTimeP85: percentile(cycleDays, 0.85),        // "85% of items finish within…"
    wipNow: wip.length,
    // Little's Law estimate from current WIP, to compare with what actually happened.
    expectedCycleDays: +(wip.length / throughputPerDay).toFixed(1),
    unplannedShare: +(done.filter((i) => i.unplanned).length / done.length).toFixed(2),
    // Items already older than the 85th percentile: the ones to swarm today.
    aging: wip
      .map((i) => ({ id: i.id, ageDays: (+to - +i.startedAt!) / DAY }))
      .filter((a) => a.ageDays > percentile(cycleDays, 0.85))
      .sort((a, b) => b.ageDays - a.ageDays),
  };
}
```

> **Aside — where the numbers lie.** Little's Law holds for averages in a roughly stable system. During a big change in WIP, the estimate and the measured cycle time will disagree for a while, and that's expected. The median also hides the tail: a team whose p50 is 3 days and p85 is 20 days has a few items stuck for weeks, which is where the most useful conversations are. And `startedAt` must mean the same thing for everyone. If some people move cards when they start thinking about something and others when they open a pull request, the metrics describe card-moving habits, not the work.

Nothing here judges individuals. The report describes the system the team works in, which is the thing the team can change.
