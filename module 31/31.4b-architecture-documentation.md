# Architecture Documentation: Diagrams That Stay True

_Module 31 · Code Quality & Technical Debt · 31.4b_

## The Problem

Suppose 60 engineers in six teams build an accounts-payable platform. Companies forward supplier invoices by email or upload them. The platform extracts the data (through a third-party OCR vendor), routes each invoice through an approval workflow, and pays suppliers through a bank's payment API. It runs as 22 services, with Postgres databases, Kafka, and object storage for invoice images.

The architecture is documented in one diagram on the wiki. It was drawn in 2023 in a desktop drawing tool, and the source file was on the laptop of an engineer who has since left. It shows 11 services. Its arrows are unlabeled: some mean "calls," some mean "publishes to," and one means "is owned by the same team as." Some boxes are services, two are shared libraries, and one is a database table. Every new engineer is shown it, with the words "it's a bit out of date."

Over six months:

**An audit question takes two weeks.** An enterprise customer's security team asks which third parties receive their invoice data. The answer is assembled from Slack questions and code searches, and it's wrong: it lists the OCR vendor as receiving "extracted fields," when in fact it receives the full invoice images, including bank details printed on them. The customer's contract requires disclosure of exactly that. A correction has to be sent, with an apology.

**An incident follows an arrow nobody knew about.** The payments service goes down, and approvals stop working too. Eight months earlier, a pull request made the approvals service call payments synchronously to show a "payment scheduled" badge. The incident responder checks the diagram, finds no connection between the two, and spends forty minutes looking elsewhere.

**A planning argument about pictures.** Two teams propose different ways to split the overloaded documents service. Each brings a whiteboard photo. The pictures can't be compared, because in one a box is a deployable service and in the other it's a code module, and neither says which. A week goes to working out that they disagree about less than they thought.

**Onboarding by oral tradition.** New engineers learn how the system fits together from whoever sits nearest, and each learns a slightly different version.

The company has architecture. It has a picture of architecture. What it lacks is documentation with a consistent notation, at the right levels of detail for different questions, kept somewhere it can be reviewed and changed, and with some way to notice when the picture and the running system disagree.

## What It Is

**Architecture documentation** describes the structure of a system above the level of code: its parts, what each is responsible for, how they communicate, where they run, and what data moves between them. Its readers are people who need the whole picture: new engineers, other teams, incident responders, security reviewers and auditors, and anyone planning a significant change.

It describes the system _as it is_, and sometimes as it's intended to become. That separates it from its neighbors:

- **Architecture decision records** (Module 2.5) record _why_ individual decisions were made, one at a time, and don't change once accepted. Architecture documentation shows the current result of all those decisions and changes whenever the system does. The two link to each other.
- **Design documents and RFCs** propose a change before it's made. Once it's built, the architecture documentation should be updated to match.
- **Documentation close to code** (31.4a) explains individual functions and packages. Runbooks (24.3) explain how to operate something. API reference (7.2) describes contracts in detail.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The architecture diagram is described as "a bit out of date," and nobody knows by how much.
- Nobody can say where the diagram's source file is, or it's in a tool only one person uses.
- Diagrams have unlabeled arrows, no legend, and boxes that mean different things.
- Questions like "which services touch payment data?" or "which third parties receive customer data?" take days to answer.
- Incident responders discover dependencies during incidents.
- Design discussions stall on what a box in someone's picture means.
- Each team has its own picture of the system, and they don't agree.
- New engineers learn the architecture from conversations rather than from anything written.

## How It Works

### 1. Why box-and-arrow diagrams fail

The wiki diagram fails in ways almost every informal diagram does:

- **Ambiguous elements.** A box can be a service, a library, a database, a team or a concept, and nothing tells you which.
- **Ambiguous arrows.** An arrow can mean a synchronous call, an event published, data flowing, a code dependency, or ownership. For the incident responder, the difference between "calls synchronously" and "consumes events from" was the whole question.
- **Mixed levels of detail.** One diagram trying to show users, services, modules and tables at once shows none of them clearly.
- **One diagram for every audience.** An auditor, an incident responder and a new engineer need different pictures.
- **No source, no history.** An exported image can't be edited, compared with its previous version, or reviewed.

### 2. The C4 model: levels of zoom

The **C4 model**, by Simon Brown, addresses most of this with a simple idea: describe the system at a few fixed levels of detail, like zooming a map, and use consistent abstractions at each.

1. **System context.** The system as one box, the people who use it, and the external systems it interacts with. Readable by anyone, including non-engineers.
2. **Containers.** The separately running or deployed parts inside the system: applications, services, databases, queues, storage buckets. ("Container" here means anything that runs or stores data on its own, not specifically a Docker container.)
3. **Components.** The major parts inside one container, such as the modules of the documents service. Worth drawing for complex containers only.
4. **Code.** Classes and functions. Rarely worth drawing by hand; generate it if you need it.

A context diagram for the accounts-payable platform:

```
   [AP clerk]            [Approver]              [Company admin]
       │ uploads,            │ approves or           │ configures
       │ reviews invoices    │ rejects invoices      │ workflows
       ▼                     ▼                       ▼
 ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │                  Accounts-Payable Platform                    │
 │  Captures supplier invoices, routes approvals, pays suppliers │
 └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
       │ sends invoice images       │ sends payment           │ syncs approved
       │ (HTTPS; full images incl.  │ instructions            │ invoices
       │  bank details)             │ (HTTPS, mTLS)           │ (REST, nightly)
       ▼                            ▼                         ▼
 [OCR vendor]                 [Bank payment API]       [Customer's accounting system]
```

The notation matters less than the rules that make it unambiguous:

- **Every element has a name, a type, a technology and a one-line responsibility.**
- **Every arrow has one direction and a label** saying what it's for and how: "sends invoice images (HTTPS)," "publishes InvoiceApproved (Kafka)." Synchronous and asynchronous communication should be distinguishable at a glance.
- **Every diagram has a title, a scope, and a legend.**

Most teams need only the first two levels for most systems, and component diagrams for the few containers complex enough to need them.

### 3. Views for particular questions

C4's levels describe static structure. Some questions need a different view of the same system:

- **Dynamic views** show one important scenario as a sequence: an invoice arriving, being extracted, approved and paid. Three to five of these, for the flows that matter most, explain more than any static diagram.
- **Deployment views** show where containers run: regions, networks, clusters, managed services. They answer "what's affected if this zone fails?"
- **Data-flow views** show what data moves where, including the **trust boundaries** it crosses: from your network to a vendor's, from a customer's tenant into shared infrastructure. This is the view the auditor needed, and it's the starting point for threat modeling and for privacy records such as the processing records GDPR requires (34.1).

Beyond diagrams, a small amount of text does a lot of work: a glossary of domain terms (the ubiquitous language from 2.3), the quality requirements the architecture is meant to meet (with numbers, as in 1.2), and known risks. **arc42** is a well-known template with twelve sections for all of this. Use it as a checklist of what might be worth writing, not as a form to fill in completely.

### 4. Docs as code: diagrams from text, in the repository

The wiki diagram couldn't be updated because its source was an image file on a laptop. **Docs as code** means keeping architecture documentation in a repository, as Markdown and text-based diagram definitions, reviewed in pull requests and rendered automatically. Diagrams become diffable, reviewable and owned, like everything else in the repository.

There are two kinds of tool:

- **Diagram languages**, such as Mermaid, PlantUML (with C4 extensions) and D2. You write each diagram as text. Mermaid renders directly on GitHub and GitLab, which makes it very low-friction. Each diagram is independent, though, so a service renamed in one diagram keeps its old name in the other five.
- **Model-based tools**, such as Structurizr (with its DSL) and LikeC4. You define the elements and relationships once, as a model, and then define views that show parts of it at different levels. Renaming a service or adding a dependency updates every view. This is what makes several levels of zoom maintainable.

Auto-layout is the main cost of both: text-based diagrams are laid out by an algorithm, and the result is rarely as clear as one arranged by hand. Most tools allow hints; few match a careful drawing.

### 5. Keeping it true

Moving the diagram into a repository makes updating it possible. It doesn't make anyone do it. There are three ways to keep architecture documentation accurate, and they work best together.

**Update it in the flow of work.** When a design document or ADR is accepted, the change to the architecture model is part of the pull request that implements it. Each container has an owning team, and the model is reviewed like code. This keeps the _intent_ current, and depends on discipline.

**Generate what can be generated.** Some facts about the architecture are recorded elsewhere already:

- A **service catalog** (Backstage and similar tools) records each service's owner, APIs and declared dependencies.
- **Infrastructure as code** (30.4b) records what's deployed where.
- **Distributed tracing** (19.2) records which services actually call which, at runtime.

Generated views are accurate about _what is_, and they're often unreadable: a trace-based service map of 22 services is a tangle of lines, with no grouping, no purpose and no sense of what matters.

**Check the documentation against reality.** Compare the model's declared relationships with what's observed, and report the differences:

```
declared in the model ──┐
                        ├──► compare ──► undeclared: approvals → payments (sync, 41k calls/day)
observed in traces ─────┘                  declared but unused: documents → legacy-search
```

The first line is the dependency from the incident, found eight months earlier. The same idea applied inside a codebase is an **architecture fitness function** (a term from _Building Evolutionary Architectures_): an automated test that fails when the code violates an intended structure, for example "the approvals module must not import the payments client." Tools like `dependency-cruiser` for TypeScript and ArchUnit for Java make these straightforward.

The combination works because each covers the others' weakness. The hand-curated model carries intent and grouping; generated data supplies the facts; the comparison tells you when the model has drifted, or when the system has drifted from the model. Sometimes the right fix is updating the diagram. Sometimes it's removing the dependency.

### 6. How much is enough

For most systems: a context diagram, a container diagram, component diagrams for the one or two most complex containers, dynamic views for the three to five most important flows, a data-flow view if the system handles regulated or sensitive data, and a glossary. All in one place, linked from each service's README and from the ADRs. That's a few pages, not a 200-page architecture document that nobody reads.

## When It's Appropriate

- **More than one team, or more than a handful of services.** Past that point, nobody holds the whole picture in their head, and the picture in each head differs.
- **Security reviews, audits and enterprise sales.** Customers and auditors ask where data goes. A current data-flow view answers in minutes.
- **Regulated data:** payments, health, personal data under GDPR or similar laws, where knowing which systems touch it is a legal requirement.
- **Incident response.** A trustworthy container view with synchronous dependencies marked shortens diagnosis.
- **Planning significant change:** splitting a service, adding a region, replacing a vendor. A shared notation lets people compare proposals.
- **Onboarding more than a few people a year.**

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A single application with a small team.** A paragraph in the README and one container diagram is plenty.
- **Component diagrams for every container.** Most containers are simple enough that their README says enough.
- **Hand-drawn code-level diagrams.** They're out of date before they're finished. Generate them when needed, or skip them.
- **A complete arc42 document for a modest system.** Fill in the sections that answer questions people actually ask.
- **Enterprise architecture platforms** for a company of 50 engineers. A repository and a diagram tool cover it.
- **Drift detection before there's a model worth checking.** First get a correct container diagram; then worry about keeping it correct automatically.

## Trade-offs

**Maintenance never ends.** Every diagram is a claim about the system that has to be kept true. Fewer, well-chosen views are easier to keep than many.

**Tools take learning.** Model-based tools and their DSLs are one more thing to learn and maintain. Mermaid is easier to start with and harder to keep consistent across many diagrams.

**Auto-layout is often ugly.** Text-based diagrams trade visual control for diffability. For a diagram meant to explain a single idea to an audience, a hand-arranged drawing is sometimes better, as long as it's treated as a one-off illustration rather than the reference.

**Generated views mislead in their own way.** A runtime service map shows everything that happened, including test traffic, health checks and one-off scripts, and nothing about what's important. Unfiltered, it's accurate and useless.

**Drift checks produce noise.** Batch jobs, admin tools and rare code paths create observed dependencies that may be fine. Each needs a decision, recorded in the model or as an exception, or the report gets ignored.

**Documentation can become bureaucracy.** Mandatory diagram updates, review boards and templates can slow change without improving understanding. The measure is whether the documents get used, not whether they exist.

## Alternatives

**Diagrams only when needed.** Draw on a whiteboard for each discussion and throw it away. Good for discussion, and it leaves nothing for the next person or the next incident.

**A service catalog alone.** Ownership, APIs and dependencies in a catalog, without narrative or diagrams. Excellent for "who owns this" and "what does this depend on"; weak on how the system works as a whole, or why.

**Tracing service maps alone.** Always current, and good for incident response. They show calls, not purpose, and miss everything that doesn't go through a traced request: batch jobs, file drops, vendor integrations.

**Design documents as the record.** Every change is documented when it's proposed. Reconstructing the present from forty historical design docs, some of which were implemented differently or not at all, is impractical.

**People.** An architect or senior engineers who know the system and explain it. Fast, flexible, and it doesn't scale or survive people leaving.

## How to Achieve It

**Step 1: replace the stale diagram.** Write a context diagram and a container diagram as text in a repository, with labeled arrows, a legend, and an owner per container. Link it from every service's README. Retire the wiki image. _Signal to move on:_ people use the diagrams, and ask questions they can't answer.

**Step 2: add views for real questions.** A data-flow view with trust boundaries for sensitive data, dynamic views for the most important flows, and a glossary. _Signal:_ keeping several diagrams consistent by hand has become a chore.

**Step 3: move to a model.** Define elements and relationships once (Structurizr, LikeC4, or a typed model like the one below) and generate the views from it. Render them in CI to a documentation site. Make model changes part of the pull request for any accepted design or ADR. _Signal:_ the model is maintained, and nobody's sure it matches production.

**Step 4: detect drift.** Compare declared relationships with the service catalog and tracing data regularly, and review the differences.

**Step 5: enforce the intended structure where it matters,** with fitness functions for dependencies that must not exist, run in CI.

## Implementation

TypeScript: a small typed model of the platform, one function that generates views from it, and a drift check against tracing data. In practice, Structurizr or LikeC4 provide the model and rendering; this version shows what "model once, many views, checked against reality" means without committing to a tool.

The model defines each element and relationship once, including how each relationship communicates and what data it carries:

```ts
// architecture/model.ts
type Kind = "person" | "external" | "service" | "database" | "queue" | "storage";
export type Element = { id: string; name: string; kind: Kind; tech?: string; owner?: string; does: string };
export type Relationship = {
  from: string; to: string; purpose: string;
  style: "sync" | "async";
  data?: ("invoice-image" | "bank-details" | "invoice-fields" | "payment-instruction")[];
};

export const elements: Element[] = [
  { id: "clerk",     name: "AP clerk",      kind: "person",   does: "Uploads and reviews invoices" },
  { id: "intake",    name: "Intake",        kind: "service",  tech: "Node", owner: "capture",   does: "Receives invoices by email and upload" },
  { id: "extract",   name: "Extraction",    kind: "service",  tech: "Node", owner: "capture",   does: "Gets fields from invoice images" },
  { id: "approvals", name: "Approvals",     kind: "service",  tech: "Node", owner: "workflow",  does: "Routes invoices through approval rules" },
  { id: "payments",  name: "Payments",      kind: "service",  tech: "Go",   owner: "money",     does: "Schedules and sends supplier payments" },
  { id: "images",    name: "Invoice store", kind: "storage",  tech: "S3",   owner: "capture",   does: "Original invoice files" },
  { id: "ocr",       name: "OCR vendor",    kind: "external", does: "Text extraction from images" },
  { id: "bank",      name: "Bank API",      kind: "external", does: "Executes payments" },
  // ... 22 services in all
];

export const relationships: Relationship[] = [
  { from: "intake",    to: "images",   purpose: "stores originals",       style: "sync",  data: ["invoice-image"] },
  { from: "extract",   to: "ocr",      purpose: "sends images for OCR",   style: "sync",  data: ["invoice-image", "bank-details"] },
  { from: "extract",   to: "approvals", purpose: "InvoiceExtracted",      style: "async", data: ["invoice-fields"] },
  { from: "approvals", to: "payments", purpose: "InvoiceApproved",        style: "async", data: ["payment-instruction"] },
  { from: "payments",  to: "bank",     purpose: "sends payment instructions", style: "sync", data: ["payment-instruction", "bank-details"] },
  // ...
];
```

A view is a selection of the model, rendered here as Mermaid so it displays on GitHub and in most documentation sites. The data-flow view for the auditor's question is a filter, not a new drawing:

```ts
// architecture/views.ts
import { elements, relationships, type Relationship } from "./model";

const byId = new Map(elements.map((e) => [e.id, e]));
const shape = (id: string) => {
  const e = byId.get(id)!;
  const label = `${e.name}${e.tech ? `<br/><i>${e.tech}</i>` : ""}`;
  return e.kind === "external" ? `${id}[/"${label}"/]` : e.kind === "database" || e.kind === "storage" ? `${id}[("${label}")]` : `${id}["${label}"]`;
};

export function mermaid(title: string, rels: Relationship[]): string {
  const ids = [...new Set(rels.flatMap((r) => [r.from, r.to]))];
  const edges = rels.map((r) => {
    const arrow = r.style === "sync" ? "-->" : "-.->";            // dashed = asynchronous
    const data = r.data?.length ? ` [${r.data.join(", ")}]` : "";
    return `  ${r.from} ${arrow}|"${r.purpose}${data}"| ${r.to}`;
  });
  return ["---", `title: ${title}`, "---", "flowchart LR", ...ids.map((id) => `  ${shape(id)}`), ...edges].join("\n");
}

// "Which third parties receive customer data, and what data?"
export const thirdPartyDataFlow = mermaid(
  "Customer data sent to third parties",
  relationships.filter((r) => byId.get(r.to)?.kind === "external" && r.data?.length),
);
```

> **Why the data classes live on the relationship.** The wrong answer to the auditor came from someone remembering that the OCR integration sends "extracted fields." Recording what each connection carries, in the same pull request that creates the connection, makes the data-flow view a query rather than an investigation. It's only as accurate as those entries, which is why the drift check below matters.

Finally, the drift check. Jaeger, like most tracing backends, can report which services called which over a period; comparing that with the model's declared relationships finds the undeclared dependency from the incident:

```ts
// architecture/drift.ts
import { relationships } from "./model";

type Edge = { parent: string; child: string; callCount: number };
const IGNORE = new Set(["healthcheck", "load-test", "admin-scripts"]);   // known noise, reviewed

const lookbackMs = 7 * 24 * 3600 * 1000;
const observed: Edge[] = (await fetch(
  `${process.env.JAEGER_URL}/api/dependencies?endTs=${Date.now()}&lookback=${lookbackMs}`,
).then((r) => r.json())).data;

const declared = new Set(relationships.filter((r) => r.style === "sync").map((r) => `${r.from}→${r.to}`));
const seen = new Set(observed.map((e) => `${e.parent}→${e.child}`));

const undeclared = observed.filter((e) =>
  !declared.has(`${e.parent}→${e.child}`) && !IGNORE.has(e.parent) && e.callCount > 100);
const unused = [...declared].filter((key) => !seen.has(key));

for (const e of undeclared) console.log(`UNDECLARED  ${e.parent} → ${e.child}  (${e.callCount} calls in 7 days)`);
for (const key of unused)  console.log(`NOT SEEN    ${key}  (declared in the model, no traced calls)`);
```

> **Only synchronous edges are compared.** Traces record direct calls between services. Events through Kafka appear as calls to the broker, not between producer and consumer, unless trace context is propagated through messages and the backend links them (19.2 covers this). Checking asynchronous relationships needs different data, such as topic producer and consumer lists from the broker or the service catalog.
>
> **"Not seen" doesn't mean "not used."** A declared dependency with no traced calls might be dead, or might only happen during month-end payment runs, or might go through a path that isn't traced. The report prompts a question to the owning team; it shouldn't delete anything by itself. The same is true of undeclared edges: the fix may be to add the relationship to the model, or to remove the dependency, as the incident suggests for approvals → payments.

## Further Reading

- Simon Brown, c4model.com, and _Software Architecture for Developers, Volume 2: Visualise, Document and Explore Your Software Architecture_ (Leanpub). The C4 model, notation guidance, and diagram review checklists.
- arc42 (arc42.org) and Gernot Starke's _arc42 by Example_. A template for architecture documentation, best used selectively.
- Paul Clements et al., _Documenting Software Architectures: Views and Beyond_ (2nd ed., Addison-Wesley, 2010). The thorough academic treatment of views and their audiences.
- Neal Ford, Rebecca Parsons, Patrick Kua and Pramod Sadalage, _Building Evolutionary Architectures_ (2nd ed., O'Reilly, 2022). Architecture fitness functions.
- Gregor Hohpe, _The Software Architect Elevator_ (O'Reilly, 2020). The chapters on diagrams, on choosing what to leave out, are especially good.
- Adam Shostack, _Threat Modeling: Designing for Security_ (Wiley, 2014). Data-flow diagrams with trust boundaries.
- Tool documentation: Structurizr and its DSL, LikeC4, Mermaid, C4-PlantUML, D2; Backstage's software catalog and TechDocs; Jaeger's dependency graph; `dependency-cruiser` and ArchUnit for fitness functions.
- Within this map: ADRs in **2.5**, service boundaries in **2.1**, domain glossary in **2.3**, quality requirements in **1.2**, tracing in **19.2**, runbooks in **24.3**, infrastructure as code in **30.4b**, documentation close to code in **31.4a**, and privacy obligations in **34.1**.

A sibling in **Java** would be worth writing, though it isn't yet. On the JVM, the component level can be derived from code rather than drawn: Spring Modulith verifies module boundaries and generates C4 component diagrams from the application's module structure, and Structurizr's Java library can build models from annotated code. That changes where the source of truth sits, from a hand-written model that gets checked against the code, to code that produces its own documentation, and shows which parts of the architecture still need a human to write them down.

---

The habit to take from this: when you look at an architecture diagram, ask of every arrow _what does this mean, and how do we know it's still true?_ If the answer is "someone drew it once," treat it as a historical document. A diagram is useful only when there's a reason it can't silently stop matching the system.
