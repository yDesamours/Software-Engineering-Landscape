# Technical Debt: Interest, Not Messiness

_Module 31 · Code Quality & Technical Debt · 31.3a_

## The Problem

Suppose 25 engineers build a payroll and time-tracking product for small businesses in the United States. It's seven years old, it pays about 90,000 employees a month, and the engineers have been asking for time to deal with technical debt for three years running.

The first year, leadership said no; there were customers to sign. The second year, engineers got a "tech debt quarter." They spent it starting "Payroll v2," a rewrite of the payroll engine planned for nine months. Six months in, it was 40% done, and meanwhile the old engine still needed every change the business required, now made twice. v2 was cancelled.

The third year, the compromise was 20% of every sprint for debt. Here's where that time went:

- Rewriting the internal admin tool from AngularJS to React. Engineers hated working in it. Three support staff use it, and it changes twice a year.
- Migrating the lint configuration and renaming modules to a new convention.
- Working through the 340 tickets in the "Tech Debt" Jira epic, titled things like "Refactor PayrollCalculator" and "Clean up employee service." None of them says what goes wrong if it isn't done.

Meanwhile, these went untouched:

- **Tax rules are hard-coded** across three services: the payroll engine, the tax-filing service and the employee portal. Adding a state takes about six engineer-weeks, and two of the last four state launches caused incorrect withholding in the first month. The roadmap has eight new states next year.
- **The month-end pay run needs babysitting.** Two engineers spend about a day and a half each at every month end watching the batch job, restarting stuck steps and fixing data by hand. It pages someone at night about once a month.
- **The filing service runs on Node 16,** which stopped receiving security fixes in September 2023. Current versions of several of its dependencies no longer support it, so the security updates from 31.2b can't be applied.

Every engineer could have described these problems. None of them was in the debt epic in a form anyone outside engineering could weigh against a feature. And the question leadership kept asking, "how much will fixing debt speed us up?", never got an answer, because "debt" meant everything engineers disliked, and nothing on the list said what it cost.

## What It Is

**Technical debt** is the extra cost that past design and implementation choices add to future changes. The metaphor has two parts. **Principal** is the work it would take to remove the choice's effect: to consolidate the tax rules, or to fix the pay run. **Interest** is the extra cost you pay every time you work around it instead: five extra weeks per state, three engineer-days per month end.

Ward Cunningham coined the term in 1992, and his meaning was narrower than today's use. He described shipping code that reflected the team's current, incomplete understanding of a domain, which is fine and even useful, as long as the code is revised once understanding improves. Debt, in his sense, is the gap between the code and what you now know. Martin Fowler later sorted debt into four kinds by two questions: was it taken on deliberately or not, and prudently or recklessly? "We'll hard-code the three launch states and consolidate at the fifth" is deliberate and prudent. "What's a tax rule table?" is inadvertent and reckless. Both produce interest; only the first came with a repayment plan.

Not every flaw is debt, and treating everything as debt is how the 340-ticket epic happened:

- **A bug** is wrong now. It needs fixing on its own merits, not as debt.
- **A missing feature** isn't debt either; it's a roadmap item.
- **Code you dislike but never change** has almost no interest. The AngularJS admin tool is ugly, and it costs the team two small changes a year. It may still carry _risk_ (below), but not much interest.

Debt comes in several forms: in code, in design and architecture (the tax rules spread over three services), in tests (areas nobody dares change), in dependencies and runtimes (drift, from 31.2b), in operations (manual toil, like the pay run), and in knowledge (a system only one person understands).

## Recognizing the Problem

- "Tech debt" is a standing request, and nobody can say what it would buy.
- The debt backlog has hundreds of items, most titled with a solution ("Refactor X") rather than a consequence.
- Debt time gets spent on whatever is most annoying to work in, not whatever costs the most.
- Estimates in certain areas are routinely two or three times over, and everyone knows which areas.
- Some recurring operational work is done by hand every week or month, by the same people.
- A runtime, framework or database version is past end of life, and upgrading keeps being deferred.
- Someone has proposed a full rewrite.
- A business goal (a new market, a new product tier) is blocked or slowed by how the system is built.

## How It Works

### 1. Interest is paid per change

The interest on debt isn't a fixed rate; it's paid each time someone has to work in or around the affected code. Two factors multiply:

```
interest per period  ≈  extra cost per change  ×  number of changes
```

That's why the hotspots from 31.1 are the debt worth attention: complex code that changes often pays interest constantly, while equally complex code that never changes pays almost none. The AngularJS admin tool has a high extra cost per change and almost no changes. The tax rules have a high cost per change and eight changes planned for next year.

Paying down principal makes sense when the interest you'd pay over a reasonable horizon exceeds the principal. For the tax rules:

```
                      states launched:   1     2     3     4     8
keep as is (6 weeks per state)           6    12    18    24    48   engineer-weeks
consolidate first (12, then 1 per state) 13   14    15    16    20
                                                 ▲
                                    break-even between the 2nd and 3rd state
```

With eight states on the roadmap, consolidating saves about 28 engineer-weeks next year and fewer incidents. With one state planned, it wouldn't pay back for a long time. The numbers are rough, and don't need to be precise: a decision that holds within a factor of two is a sound one.

### 2. Debt grows while you wait

Unlike a loan, technical debt's principal tends to grow. Every new state added the old way puts more tax logic in three places, so consolidating later costs more than consolidating now. Every new service built on Node 16 makes the upgrade bigger. This is the practical meaning of "compounding" here: new work gets built on top of the old choice, and the choice becomes harder to undo. It's also why containing debt, described below, is often worth more than paying it down.

### 3. Some debt is risk, not interest

Some items cost little day to day and could cost a great deal all at once:

- **An end-of-life runtime.** Node 16 works fine, until a vulnerability appears that can't be patched on it, or a cloud provider stops supporting it.
- **Knowledge in one head.** The pay-run job is understood in depth by one engineer. If she leaves, the babysitting gets much harder.
- **A component whose vendor is gone,** or a certificate, license or contract that expires.

These are better described as probability times impact, like any risk register, than as interest. They often have a deadline, and the cheapest time to deal with them is well before it.

### 4. Where interest shows up

Interest rarely shows up labeled. It appears as:

- **Slower changes.** Lead time for work in the affected area; estimates that run over.
- **Defects and incidents.** Postmortems whose contributing factors keep naming the same component.
- **Toil.** Manual, repetitive work that exists only because the system doesn't handle something. Google's SRE practice caps toil at 50% of an SRE's time, because above that it crowds out the work that would reduce it.
- **Onboarding.** How long before a new engineer can safely change an area.
- **Lost options.** Features, markets or deals the business can't pursue, or can only pursue slowly, because of how the system is built. This is often the largest cost and the easiest one for non-engineers to understand.

### 5. Making it visible: a register, not a backlog

The 340-ticket epic failed because every item described a solution, and no item described a cost. A **debt register** turns that around. It's short, 10 to 20 items, and each entry is written as a consequence with evidence:

```
Adding a state takes ~6 engineer-weeks and has caused withholding errors
  Area:       tax rules in payroll-engine, filings, employee-portal
  Interest:   ~5 extra weeks per state; 2 incidents in the last 4 launches;
              8 states on next year's roadmap
  Principal:  ~12 engineer-weeks to move rules into one versioned rules module
  Risk:       incorrect withholding is a compliance issue for customers
  Decision:   pay down, before the next state launch
  Owner:      payroll team        Review: 2027-01-15
```

Compare "Refactor PayrollCalculator." The first can be weighed against a feature by someone who doesn't read code. The second can't be weighed by anyone.

Evidence doesn't need precision. Useful sources are cheap:

- Hotspot and change data from 31.1.
- Postmortem contributing factors.
- Hours logged on toil, or simply asking the people who do it.
- A lightweight tag on tickets that were slowed by a known item ("slowed by: tax rules"), which in a quarter produces a rough count.
- A periodic team survey of where work is hardest.

### 6. Four decisions for each item

Every entry in the register gets one of four decisions, and each is legitimate:

- **Pay down.** Remove the cause: consolidate the rules, automate the pay-run recovery, upgrade the runtime. For large items, incrementally (31.3b covers how).
- **Contain.** Stop the debt from spreading, without removing it. New states go through a new rules module while the old ones stay where they are until they need changing. A wrapper hides a bad API behind a good interface. Containment is often cheap and stops the compounding in §2.
- **Accept.** Write down that the item is known and not worth fixing now, and when to reconsider. The AngularJS admin tool: stable, rarely changed, used internally. Accept it, and revisit if its changes become frequent or it becomes a security concern.
- **Retire.** Delete the feature or component that carries the debt. It's underused more often than anyone expects, and deletion has no principal.

Deliberate new debt fits here too: when a team decides to take a shortcut, it records the decision, the reason, and the trigger for repaying it, ideally as an ADR (Module 2.5). "Hard-coded for three launch states; consolidate before the fifth" is a repayment plan. "We'll clean it up later" isn't.

### 7. Funding models

Each common way of paying for debt work fails in a predictable way:

| Model | Works well for | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fixed percentage** (e.g. 20% of each sprint) | Steady, small items | Not tied to a register: time goes to the most annoying work, not the most expensive (the AngularJS rewrite) |
| **Paying down where you're about to work** | Most debt | The expensive area isn't on the roadmap, or the item is too large for one feature |
| **Clean-up weeks** | Small irritations, morale | Large items; nothing big fits in a week |
| **Dedicated team** | Platform-wide items (runtime upgrades, shared libraries) | The team drifts from the product teams' actual pain |
| **Big-bang rewrite** | Rarely anything | Almost always: the old system keeps changing while the new one chases it (Payroll v2) |

Most teams do best with a combination: pay down debt in the area a feature is about to touch, as part of that feature's estimate. As Kent Beck put it, first make the change easy, then make the easy change. Keep a small reserved budget for the top of the register. And put the large structural items on the roadmap as projects of their own, with the business case written in the register's terms.

### 8. Talking about it outside engineering

The tax-rule entry doesn't need the word "debt" at all: "Consolidating the tax rules costs 12 engineer-weeks. It saves about 28 next year, makes each state launch take one week instead of six, and removes the cause of two withholding incidents." That's an investment proposal, and it gets judged like one.

The words that fail are the ones that ask for trust: "debt," "cleanup," "refactoring," "quality." They're not wrong, but they describe engineering's view of the problem, and the listener has to take on faith that they matter. Translate into lead time, incidents, customer impact, toil hours and blocked options, and the decision becomes one leadership knows how to make.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A codebase more than a year or two old with several teams,** where debt affects people who didn't create it and can't see its whole shape.
- **Recurring requests for "debt time"** that leadership can't evaluate.
- **A roadmap that repeatedly touches the same area.** Eight state launches in one year is the moment to decide about the tax rules.
- **Recurring toil or incidents** traced to the same components.
- **A deadline-driven risk:** an end-of-life runtime, a vendor shutting down, a contract expiring.
- **A proposed rewrite.** A register with evidence is the best test of whether a rewrite, a containment, or nothing is the right answer.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Before product–market fit.** A team of four whose product may pivot next month should take on deliberate debt freely; the code may not survive long enough for the interest to matter. A short list of known shortcuts is plenty.
- **A formal register for a team that already agrees.** If the three people working on a system know its two problems and are fixing them, the register is paperwork.
- **Code that's about to be deleted or replaced.** Its interest ends when it goes.
- **Precise interest accounting.** Tracking hours to the decimal costs more than the decisions it informs. Order of magnitude is enough.
- **Treating every flaw as debt.** Bugs, missing features and matters of taste each have their own path; putting them in the register drowns the items that matter.

## Trade-offs

**Paying down debt delays features.** That's the point of weighing it, but it's a real cost, and the business is right to ask for the case.

**Estimates of interest are guesses.** A register built on rough numbers can be wrong, and a confident-sounding number can win an argument it shouldn't. Mark uncertain figures as such.

**Refactoring introduces risk.** Payroll code changed to reduce debt can break payroll. Paying down needs the same care as any other change (31.3b).

**The metaphor can be misused.** "Debt" can become a label for any code someone else wrote, or for work engineers find more interesting than features. Used as blame, it makes people hide shortcuts instead of recording them.

**Registers take upkeep.** Items go stale, owners change teams, decisions need revisiting. An unmaintained register becomes the 340-ticket epic with a different name.

**Contained debt is still debt.** A wrapper around a bad API stops the spread, and the bad API is still there, with the wrapper as one more thing to understand.

**Accepted debt can come due.** Deciding to live with something is sound only if the review date is kept. The AngularJS tool is fine to accept until a vulnerability appears in a framework that no longer gets fixes.

## Alternatives

**Continuous refactoring, and no separate management.** In teams practicing it strictly, with good tests and small refactorings as part of every change, debt never accumulates enough to need a register. It works well, and it depends on discipline that rarely survives deadline pressure across many teams. It also doesn't cover structural items too large for one change.

**A higher quality bar up front.** Refusing deliberate shortcuts reduces debt at the cost of speed early on, which is often the wrong trade for an unproven product and the right one for, say, payment systems.

**Different framings of the same idea.** Some teams find "friction log," "toil budget" or "risk register" land better than "debt," especially with audiences for whom "debt" sounds like an admission of failure. The mechanism is the same: consequences, evidence, decisions.

**The rewrite.** Occasionally correct, when the platform itself is gone or the domain has changed completely. It should be the conclusion of the register's analysis, not a substitute for it, and even then an incremental replacement (31.3b) usually beats a big-bang one.

## How to Achieve It

**Step 1: collect consequences.** Ask each team for the five things that most slow them down or cause incidents, phrased as what goes wrong, not what should be done. Merge duplicates. Cross-check against the hotspot list from 31.1. _Signal to move on:_ you have a list, and nobody can rank it.

**Step 2: add rough evidence to the top ten.** Interest (time, incidents, toil, blocked options), principal, and any deadline-driven risk. Order-of-magnitude numbers are fine. _Signal:_ the list is ranked, and nothing has changed.

**Step 3: decide.** Pay down, contain, accept or retire, with an owner and a review date for each. Put the largest items on the roadmap with their business case. Close the old debt epic, or move anything still relevant into the register. _Signal:_ decisions exist, but they compete with features every sprint.

**Step 4: choose the funding model.** Default to paying down where features are about to go, add a small reserved budget for the register's top items, and record new deliberate debt as ADRs with repayment triggers. _Signal:_ it works, and needs keeping honest.

**Step 5: review quarterly.** Check whether paid-down items actually reduced interest: did state launches get faster, did the month-end pages stop? Re-rank, retire finished items, and revisit accepted ones.

## Implementation

A register works best as a file in the repository, reviewed like code, rather than tickets in a tracker. A small TypeScript script can then keep it honest: it attaches the change evidence from 31.1 to each item and flags entries whose review date has passed or whose area has started changing much more than when the decision was made.

```ts
// debt/register.ts
export type DebtItem = {
  id: string;
  consequence: string;                     // what goes wrong, not what to do
  paths: string[];                         // where it lives, for change evidence
  interest: string;                        // evidence, rough numbers welcome
  principalWeeks: number;
  decision: "pay-down" | "contain" | "accept" | "retire";
  owner: string;
  review: string;                          // ISO date
  changesAtDecision?: number;              // 90-day change count when decided
};

export const register: DebtItem[] = [
  {
    id: "tax-rules",
    consequence: "Adding a state takes ~6 engineer-weeks and has caused withholding errors",
    paths: ["payroll-engine/src/tax/", "filings/src/rules/", "portal/src/withholding/"],
    interest: "~5 extra weeks/state; 2 incidents in last 4 launches; 8 states planned",
    principalWeeks: 12, decision: "pay-down", owner: "payroll", review: "2027-01-15",
  },
  {
    id: "admin-angularjs",
    consequence: "Admin tool changes are slow; framework is past end of life",
    paths: ["admin/"],
    interest: "~2 changes/year, each ~3 days longer than expected",
    principalWeeks: 8, decision: "accept", owner: "support-tools", review: "2027-03-01",
    changesAtDecision: 1,
  },
];
```

```ts
// debt/check.ts
import { execFileSync } from "node:child_process";
import { register } from "./register";

const changes90d = (paths: string[]) =>
  execFileSync("git", ["log", "--since=90 days ago", "--no-merges", "--format=%H", "--", ...paths],
    { encoding: "utf8" }).split("\n").filter(Boolean).length;

const today = new Date();
for (const item of register) {
  const recent = changes90d(item.paths);
  const flags: string[] = [];
  if (new Date(item.review) < today) flags.push("review date passed");
  if (item.decision === "accept" && item.changesAtDecision !== undefined
      && recent > Math.max(3, item.changesAtDecision * 3)) {
    flags.push(`accepted at ${item.changesAtDecision} changes/90d, now ${recent}: interest is rising`);
  }
  console.log(`${item.id.padEnd(18)} ${item.decision.padEnd(9)} ${String(recent).padStart(4)} changes/90d  ${flags.join("; ")}`);
}
```

> **Why the check watches accepted items most closely.** An "accept" decision is only correct while its assumption holds, usually that the area rarely changes. If the admin tool suddenly gets a dozen changes in a quarter because support needs a new workflow, the interest has changed and the decision is out of date. Nobody notices that from the register alone; the change count does. The threshold here is deliberately crude, and it only prompts a review, never a decision.

Run it in the quarterly review, or weekly in CI as an advisory report. The register itself changes by pull request, so every new item, decision and acceptance has a reviewer and a date in the history.

- Martin Fowler, "Technical Debt" and "Technical Debt Quadrant" (bliki), and "Is High Quality Software Worth the Cost?" (2019), which argues that internal quality lowers cost within weeks, not years.
- Philippe Kruchten, Robert Nord and Ipek Ozkaya, _Managing Technical Debt: Reducing Friction in Software Development_ (Addison-Wesley, 2019). The most complete treatment, from the Software Engineering Institute.
- Neil Ernst et al., "Measure It? Manage It? Ignore It? Software Practitioners and Technical Debt," ESEC/FSE 2015. A large survey finding architectural choices to be the most significant source of debt.
- Terese Besker, Antonio Martini and Jan Bosch, "Software Developer Productivity Loss Due to Technical Debt," _Journal of Systems and Software_, 2019. A survey-based estimate of how much working time debt consumes.
- Betsy Beyer et al. (eds.), _Site Reliability Engineering_ (O'Reilly, 2016), chapter "Eliminating Toil."
- Joel Spolsky, "Things You Should Never Do, Part I" (2000), on the rewrite.

---

The habit to take from this: whenever someone says "that's technical debt," ask _what does it cost us, and how often?_ If nobody can answer, it may just be code someone dislikes. If the answer is "five weeks, eight times next year," it's no longer a complaint. It's a proposal.
