# Presence

_Module 9.3a — Real-Time Communication_

## The Problem

A live-chat support product. Visitors open a widget, conversations route to whichever agent is available, and every agent sees a roster of colleagues with a green dot.

Version one is four lines, and everyone writes it:

```sql
-- on connect
UPDATE agents SET online = true  WHERE id = $1;
-- on disconnect
UPDATE agents SET online = false WHERE id = $1;
```

**The ghost.** An agent closes her laptop at 17:58. The TCP connection isn't closed, it's abandoned — 9.1b's half-open socket — so no disconnect handler runs. The row still says `true` at 18:30, and the router keeps handing her conversations. A customer waits six minutes for someone who left the building.

**The flicker.** Another agent works from a train. His connection drops and reconnects every forty seconds. The dot blinks green-grey-green all afternoon, and worse, the router moves his active conversation to a colleague mid-sentence and then back again.

**The third tab.** An agent works with three tabs open and closes one. The disconnect handler sets `online = false`. She is now invisible to the router while actively typing in the other two. A boolean cannot represent "two of her three connections are still here."

**The pod that died.** A deploy OOMs a pod holding 3,000 connections. No sockets close gracefully, no handlers run, and 3,000 rows say `online = true` with nothing in the system that will ever correct them. The replacement pod has no idea what the dead one was holding.

So someone adds heartbeats — every client updates its row every ten seconds — and at 20,000 agents that's **2,000 writes per second** against a hot table in the primary database, each producing a dead tuple, replication lag, and an autovacuum problem. The green dot is now the highest-write feature in the product, to display information nobody reads carefully.

One more, visible only at scale: in a 200-person room, every join is broadcast to 199 people. Two hundred people arriving in the first thirty seconds of an all-hands is roughly 40,000 presence messages in half a minute, to render a row of avatars.

Six failures, one root cause. The column is being asked a question it structurally cannot answer.

## What It Is

Here's the sentence the boolean is hiding: **you can never know that someone is online. You can only know that you heard from them recently.**

Every disconnection you detect is detected *late*, after a timeout, and some are never detected at all because the peer vanished or the process holding the knowledge died with it. There is no event that means "gone." There is only the absence of events, which takes time to become meaningful.

So presence is not a flag. **Presence is a lease**: a record that expires unless something keeps renewing it. Online means "an unexpired lease exists." Offline is what happens when nobody renews. Disconnect events remain useful — they let you react in milliseconds instead of seconds — but they are an *optimization on top of expiry*, never the source of truth. Build it the other way around and the pod that OOMs leaves permanent garbage.

That reframing fixes four of the six failures by construction. The ghost expires. The dead pod's agents expire. Multiple tabs are multiple renewals of one lease. And renewal can ride a heartbeat you already send.

### Four neighbours, because they get conflated constantly

**Presence vs availability.** "Connected" and "willing to take work" are different facts from different sources — one derived from the network, one declared by a human. Routing to a connected-but-at-lunch agent is the same customer-facing failure as routing to a ghost. Separate fields, explicit merge rule.

**Presence vs activity.** Connected and idle is a normal state: a tab open overnight is fully connected and its owner is asleep. If "active" is what you mean, the signal is input events in the page, not packets on the wire.

**Presence vs typing indicators.** Superficially similar, completely different lifetime: a typing state is per-conversation, lives three to five seconds, is never persisted, and should be dropped rather than queued under load. Don't route it through your presence system.

**Presence vs last-seen.** "Active 4 minutes ago" is a durable fact about the past, written occasionally. Dramatically cheaper than live presence, and frequently what the product actually needed.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Users appear online who demonstrably are not, and the state never corrects itself.
- A status flaps between states several times a minute.
- Closing one tab logs the user out of a feature.
- After a crash or deploy, stale state persists until someone runs a script.
- Presence writes are among your top queries by volume.
- Work is assigned to people who never pick it up.
- A large room produces thousands of messages when a meeting starts.
- "How many people are online?" requires a query you're afraid to run.
- There's a "clean up stale presence" cron job. That job is the design telling you it's wrong.

## How It Works

### Leases with a lazy sweep

Store a per-user entry with a timestamp, and treat anything older than the TTL as gone:

```
presence:room:42          sorted set
   ana   1757731204000
   dev   1757731199000
   mo    1757731061000    ← older than TTL: not online, not yet deleted
```

Renew on every heartbeat. Read by discarding everything older than the cutoff, then returning the rest. The deletion needs no cron job — do it on read, immediately before the read, and the structure cleans itself in proportion to how often anyone looks.

The TTL is the design decision, and it's a straight trade: **presence is wrong for up to one TTL, every time.** Three times the heartbeat interval is the usual choice, so a 25-second heartbeat gives 75 seconds of worst-case staleness and tolerates two lost pings without evicting a healthy user.

The property worth naming: **crash recovery and normal disconnect are the same code path.** A pod that OOMs stops renewing, and its users age out on the next read like anyone else. Nothing to reconcile on startup, no scenario where state is permanently wrong.

### You already have a heartbeat — don't build a second one

9.1b's connection pings every 25 seconds and pushes out a read deadline on the pong. Renewal belongs there. No new client code, no new message type, no extra traffic, and presence becomes exactly as fresh as your liveness detection, by construction.

A separate client-driven presence heartbeat gives you two liveness signals that can disagree, which produces the genuinely confusing bug: a user who is offline for the connection manager and online for the roster.

### Hysteresis: fast up, slow down

```
socket      ▁▇▇▇▇▁▁▁▇▇▇▇▇▇▁▁▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁
lease       ▁▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▁▁▁▁▁▁▁
published   ▁▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▁▁▁▁▁▁▁
             ↑ online on the first packet     ↑ offline one TTL after the last
```

Going online should be immediate: publish on connect, don't wait for a lease to be noticed. Going offline should be delayed by a full TTL **even when you received a clean disconnect**, because a clean disconnect is frequently followed by a reconnect two seconds later during a deploy or a page navigation.

Concretely: on disconnect, schedule the offline notification rather than sending it, and cancel it if a renewal arrives first. Have the timer consult the shared store rather than a local flag, because the reconnect may have landed on a different pod (9.1c) that knows nothing about your timer.

The asymmetry is the whole trick, and it's the same shape as debouncing a UI event: being briefly wrong in one direction costs much less than in the other.

### Fan-out is the expensive part, not storage

Presence storage is trivial. Presence *notification* is where the money goes, because every change is interesting to everyone who can see that user.

**Broadcast every change to the room.** O(members) per change, O(members²) for a mass arrival. Fine up to a few dozen people. This is the all-hands problem.

**Coalesce into a window.** Presence is state, not events — only the latest value matters, which makes it the textbook case for 9.1b's coalescing policy. Batch deltas over one or two seconds and send one message containing all of them. Two hundred arrivals become a handful of updates, and nobody perceives the delay because presence is already approximate.

**Send only what's rendered.** A UI showing fifty avatars doesn't need updates about the other 4,000 members. Subscribe for the visible set, re-subscribe on scroll. More client complexity, and it's the difference between a roster that scales and one with a member limit.

For counts in large audiences, stop being exact. Ten thousand viewers means ten thousand leases to count on every read; an approximate cardinality structure or a periodically-computed estimate is cheaper by orders of magnitude, and the UI is rendering "~12k watching" anyway. Exactness the interface immediately rounds away is pure cost.

### Scope: three different questions

"Is Ana online?" (account), "which of Ana's devices are here?" (connection), and "is Ana in room 42?" (membership) are separate records. Keying the room set by **user id** rather than connection id collapses multi-tab and multi-device automatically — three tabs renew the same member, and the third opening failure becomes impossible rather than special-cased. You lose the ability to say *which* device; when you need that, nest a per-connection hash under the user and take the newest timestamp.

### This is the registry 9.1c avoided

9.1c argued against a `user → node` registry because fan-out doesn't need one. Presence is the feature that does: it's something you *query*, not merely route through. Note what's different, though — leases with TTLs are self-healing, where a routing registry that must be accurate has no such safety net. That's exactly why you build the queryable thing only when a feature demands it.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Assignment or routing depends on it** — support queues, dispatch, on-call. Wants the tightest staleness you can afford (20–30 second TTL), a declared availability layer, and the escape hatch in *Trade-offs*.
- **Collaboration affordances** — avatars on a document, call participants, "3 people viewing." A 60–90 second TTL is generous, and flicker is the failure that gets reported, not staleness.
- **Rooms up to a few hundred** — full roster with coalesced deltas, no cleverness required.
- **Large audiences** — counts only, approximate, rounded, no roster at all.
- **Typing indicators**, as a separate short-lived state: 3–5 seconds, one conversation, dropped rather than buffered when a client is behind.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Nobody acts on the dot.** Presence is very often decoration that costs a Redis cluster. If no decision — human or automated — changes based on it, a last-seen timestamp gives most of the value for none of the infrastructure.
- **Small teams.** Twenty users fetching a roster every thirty seconds is 0.7 requests a second. Ship that.
- **Declared status is what users actually want.** People frequently prefer to control how they appear, and an automatic dot can be a worse experience than a manual toggle regardless of accuracy.
- **Per-room presence when rooms are derivable.** If you already track each connection's subscriptions (9.1c), membership may fall out of data you hold.
- **Exact counts at any scale where you'd round them.**

## Trade-offs

**It is always wrong, and you're choosing by how much.** One TTL of staleness isn't a bug to fix, it's the parameter. Shrinking it multiplies heartbeats, renewals, and fan-out, and increases flicker for users on poor connections. No setting is both fresh and stable.

**Presence is a record of when people are at their desks.** This is the trade-off engineering discussions skip. It gets used for things it wasn't built for — attendance inference, performance management — and in several jurisdictions that's a works-council or data-protection matter rather than a product preference. An "appear offline" option is the minimum, and it has to be *honest*: the classic leak is a typing indicator or read receipt that fires while the user is supposedly invisible, because those paths were built separately and nobody checked them against the setting.

**Hot keys.** A single room's sorted set updated by 10,000 members is one key on one shard, and the lazy sweep makes every roster read a write — so the read path can't be served from a replica and counts against your write budget. Above a few thousand members, sweep on a timer and accept slightly staler reads.

**Two sources of truth.** Derived presence and declared status must be merged somewhere, and the rule is product policy: does "do not disturb" survive a disconnect? Is a user who set themselves Away but is actively typing Away? Write the rules down; every implementation that doesn't has three of them.

**Slow-offline hurts exactly where it matters.** The hysteresis that fixes the flicker keeps the ghost visible for a full TTL — and the ghost is the failure with a customer waiting on it. Which points at the real conclusion:

**Use presence as a hint, and make the consumer tolerant.** For anything consequential, the reliable mechanism isn't better presence, it's **acknowledgement with reassignment**: route the conversation, require the agent's client to accept within eight seconds, route it elsewhere if it doesn't. That works when presence is stale, when the agent is present but away from the keyboard, and when their browser crashed during the handoff — none of which any presence system can detect in time. Presence makes the first guess better; the ack makes being wrong survivable.

## Alternatives

- **Last-seen only.** One throttled timestamp write per user per minute, rendered as relative time. No leases, no fan-out, no flicker, no infrastructure. The right answer more often than it's chosen.
- **Declared status only.** The user says Available, Away, or Do Not Disturb. Zero distributed problems, better privacy, and honest in a way derived presence never is.
- **Acknowledgement-based assignment** instead of presence-based assignment. Solves the business problem rather than the proxy for it.
- **CRDT-based presence.** Phoenix Presence keeps replicated state per node and merges with a conflict-free replicated set, so a partition heals correctly with no shared store. More robust than a TTL store under partial partitions, at the cost of a runtime that makes it practical.
- **Managed presence** — Ably presence, Pusher presence channels, Supabase Realtime Presence. Read their documentation even if you build your own, because the sync-on-reconnect case is where homegrown implementations are usually incomplete.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — last-seen.** One timestamp written on activity, throttled to once a minute. Renders "active 3m ago."
*Next when:* a decision depends on live state rather than a relative label.

**Level 1 — leases, no fan-out.** Renew from the existing pong handler; clients fetch the roster on room open and refresh every 30 seconds. Fixes ghosts, dead pods, and multi-tab immediately, with polling in front of it.
*Next when:* the roster refresh is visible as latency, or polling cost matters.

**Level 2 — deltas over the bus, coalesced and debounced.** Publish changes through 9.1c's fan-out in 1–2 second batches; delay offline by one TTL and cancel on renewal.
*Next when:* a room is big enough that the roster itself is the payload.

**Level 3 — scope and approximate.** Per-room membership sets, presence only for the rendered set, approximate counts above a threshold.
*Next when:* users ask to control their visibility, or someone in the business asks a question about this data that makes you uncomfortable.

**Level 4 — declared status and privacy.** An explicit status field, documented merge rules, and an appear-offline mode audited across *every* path that reveals activity: typing, read receipts, cursors, last-seen.

## Implementation

Go, continuing 9.1b's connection, because the point worth showing is that presence renewal isn't new machinery — it's three lines inside a handler that already exists.

```go
const (
	pingEvery   = 25 * time.Second
	presenceTTL = 75 * time.Second        // 3 × pingEvery
)

c.ws.SetPongHandler(func(string) error {
	p.Touch(ctx, roomID, userID)                           // ← aside 1
	return c.ws.SetReadDeadline(time.Now().Add(pongWait))
})

func (p *Presence) Touch(ctx context.Context, room, user string) error {
	now := float64(time.Now().UnixMilli())
	return p.rdb.ZAdd(ctx, "presence:"+room,
		redis.Z{Score: now, Member: user}).Err()           // ← aside 2
}
```

Reading a roster, sweeping on the way past:

```go
func (p *Presence) Roster(ctx context.Context, room string) ([]string, error) {
	key := "presence:" + room
	cutoff := time.Now().Add(-presenceTTL).UnixMilli()

	pipe := p.rdb.TxPipeline()
	pipe.ZRemRangeByScore(ctx, key, "-inf", strconv.FormatInt(cutoff, 10))
	members := pipe.ZRange(ctx, key, 0, -1)                // ← aside 3
	if _, err := pipe.Exec(ctx); err != nil {
		return nil, err
	}
	return members.Result()
}
```

And the debounce, which is where most implementations are wrong:

```go
func (p *Presence) OnDisconnect(room, user string) {
	p.timers.Schedule(room+"/"+user, presenceTTL, func() {
		online, err := p.IsOnline(context.Background(), room, user)
		if err != nil || online {
			return                                          // ← aside 4
		}
		p.bus.Publish("room:"+room, Delta{User: user, Online: false})
	})
}
```

**Aside 1 — renewal rides a heartbeat that already exists.** No client change, no new message type, and presence freshness becomes identical to liveness freshness — the correct coupling, because the moment those two numbers differ you own a class of bug where the connection manager and the roster disagree about the same person. Note also what a pong proves: the peer's TCP stack answered. That's liveness, not attention. If the product means "at their desk," that's a different signal from the page and belongs in a different field.

**Aside 2 — the member is the user, not the connection.** Three tabs renew one sorted-set member, so multi-tab collapses for free and the third opening failure can't occur. The cost is that you can't tell which device; when you need that, nest a per-connection hash under the user and take the newest timestamp.

**Aside 3 — the sweep is the garbage collection, and it turns reads into writes.** Expired members are removed on the way past, so there's no cron job and no reconciliation after a crash — and Redis drops the key entirely once its last member goes, so empty rooms cost nothing. The honest cost: `Roster` is a write, it can't be served from a replica, and at high read rates you should move the sweep to a timer and read without it. Correct either way; the choice is about where the load lands.

**Aside 4 — the timer asks the store, not itself.** The user may have reconnected onto a different pod, which knows nothing about this timer and never had a chance to cancel it. The lease is the only authority, and consulting it turns a cross-node cancellation problem into a single read. It's also why the check is "is there an unexpired lease," not "did I see a reconnect."

**What's deliberately absent.** There's no declared-status field, so this code cannot express "connected but unavailable" — and every product that ships presence eventually needs to. Adding the field is easy; deciding the merge rules is not, and that belongs in the product spec rather than in this function.

## Further Reading

- **Mike Burrows, "The Chubby lock service for loosely-coupled distributed systems"** (OSDI 2006) — the sections on sessions, KeepAlives, and the grace period. The canonical treatment of lease-based liveness, and the source of the intuition that a lease turns crash recovery into ordinary expiry.
- **Phoenix Presence documentation and `phoenix_pubsub`'s CRDT implementation** — a production presence system with its conflict resolution written down, including what happens across a netsplit and how state merges when it heals.
- **Marc Shapiro et al., "A Comprehensive Study of Convergent and Commutative Replicated Data Types"** (INRIA, 2011) — the theory behind the partition-tolerant version, if a TTL store isn't enough.
- **Redis sorted set documentation**, particularly `ZADD` and `ZREMRANGEBYSCORE`, plus the rule that a sorted set is deleted when its last member is removed — which is what makes the lazy sweep self-cleaning.
- **Ably's presence documentation** and **Pusher's presence channels** — read them for the sync-on-reconnect semantics, the part homegrown implementations usually miss.
- **"Flannel: An Application-Level Edge Cache to Make Slack Scale"** (Slack engineering, 2017) — presence and user metadata at a scale where fan-out dominates, and a good example of moving the problem to the edge rather than optimizing the store.
- **Redis `PFADD`/`PFCOUNT` (HyperLogLog) documentation** — for large-audience counts, where exactness costs more than it's worth.

A TypeScript sibling would earn its place on the client side, where a different problem lives: merging a coalesced delta stream into a roster without flicker, reconciling a full sync against in-flight deltas after a reconnect, and applying the declared-versus-derived merge rules in one place instead of four components. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: any fact you learned from the network is a fact about the past. "Online" means heard from recently, "healthy" means answered the last probe, "the file exists" means it existed when you looked. The engineering question is never how to make it current — it can't be — but how long the window is, and who is harmed inside it.
