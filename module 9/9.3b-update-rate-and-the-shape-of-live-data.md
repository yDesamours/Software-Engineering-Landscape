# Update Rate and the Shape of Live Data

_Module 9.3b — Real-Time Communication_

## The Problem

A warehouse robotics platform. Autonomous units move totes around a floor, and operators watch a console: a map with the robots on it, a header with throughput numbers, and a feed of alerts down the right-hand side.

Each robot publishes its position, battery, and state ten times a second. With twenty robots in the pilot warehouse, the console is a joy — everything moves smoothly, numbers tick up, it demos beautifully.

The first real site has 900 robots.

**9,000 messages a second, and the browser stops.** Every message is a `JSON.parse`, a state update, and a re-render. The main thread is pinned at 100%, the fan spins, the map redraws at four frames a second, and clicking a robot takes two seconds to respond. Nothing is wrong with the transport — the WebSocket is delivering everything perfectly, which is the problem.

**The numbers are unreadable anyway.** The throughput counter in the header updates ten times a second. Nobody can read a number that changes ten times a second; it's a grey blur. An operator asked for it to be "less jumpy," which the team interpreted as a CSS problem.

**One operator on a VPN drags down the server.** Her connection can't absorb the rate, her outbound queue grows, and — this is the part that's specific to this article — the queue is full of *positions from thirty seconds ago*. She isn't just behind; her map is animating a history at the wrong speed. A backlog of state is a backlog of wrong answers.

**Reconnecting takes eleven seconds.** The resume logic from 9.1b faithfully replays every message she missed: 9,000 per second of disconnection, applied in order, to render a journey she doesn't care about, to arrive at a present she could have been handed directly.

**The header disagrees with the shift report.** "Units active" is computed on the client by counting robots in the store. Robots outside the viewport were never subscribed, some updates were dropped under load, and over an eight-hour shift the number drifts. It's not off by a rounding error; it's off by forty.

**And an operator missed a collision alert.** Someone, reasonably, added "keep only the newest message per robot" to fix the queue problem. Alerts were tagged with a robot id. The fix deleted one.

The transport is fine. Every problem here is about *rate and shape* — how much data, in what form, arriving how often, for whom.

## What It Is

The discipline is **rate shaping**: deliberately matching the rate and shape of what you send to what the consumer can absorb, by deciding — per stream — what is allowed to be lost.

The last clause is the whole thing, because the answer is different for different data, and the opening's final failure is what happens when one policy is applied to everything.

**Three kinds of live data, three policies:**

**State** — the current value of something. Position, battery, temperature, progress, status. Intermediate values are worthless the moment a newer one exists, so state can be sampled, coalesced, quantized, and skipped freely. Applying an update is idempotent replacement.

**Events** — discrete facts that occurred. An alert, a message, an order fill, an error. Each one is a separate truth, order matters, and dropping one is data loss, not degradation. Events may never be coalesced or sampled.

**Derived values** — counts, rates, percentiles, averages over a population. These are *computed*, not transported, and they must be computed where the whole population is visible. A derived value assembled on a client from a sampled stream will be wrong, and it will be wrong in the direction of confident and specific.

Most real screens carry all three at once, which is why "how often should we send updates?" has no single answer. The question is always "for which stream?"

One placement: 9.1b named the backpressure policies — block, drop, coalesce, disconnect — as things a connection queue does when a client falls behind. This article is about doing the shaping *before* the queue, at the source, where it's done once for everyone instead of per connection and under duress.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The browser tab is hot, the fan is on, and scrolling stutters while data arrives.
- A number on screen changes too fast to read.
- Reconnecting produces a visible replay: a progress bar racing, a map catching up.
- "Live" figures disagree with the same figures in a report.
- The feature is fine on office wifi and unusable over a VPN.
- Memory in the tab grows over hours.
- A fix for one stream broke another one that shared the code path.
- Someone added a chart with 50,000 points to an 800-pixel-wide panel.
- Your per-connection queue keeps filling, and the messages in it are already obsolete.

## How It Works

### Three budgets, and the tightest one is human

```
network      bytes/sec the worst client's link will take
client CPU   messages/sec the main thread can parse, apply, and render
human        ~4–10 meaningful changes/sec; display caps out at 60/sec regardless
```

A dashboard updating four times a second is indistinguishable from one updating a hundred times a second — except the second one is unusable and costs 25× more. **Shape to the human budget first**, then check that the other two are satisfied, which they usually are once you have.

The exceptions are real and narrow: the consumer isn't always human. Another program consuming a feed can absorb whatever you send, and latency-critical domains — competitive games, trading execution — shape aggressively but in the other direction, toward smaller payloads at higher rates. Know which case you're in before picking numbers.

### Publish on a tick, not on change

The single biggest lever. Instead of emitting when something changes, mark it dirty and flush every 200–500ms:

```
producer      9,000 msg/s  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
 ├ quantize                ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓            idle robots emit nothing
 ├ viewport filter         ▓▓▓                      40 of 900 visible
 ├ coalesce on 250ms tick  ▓
 └ to client        4 msg/s│
                           ▼
client        one render per animation frame
human         reads a few changes per second
```

Each stage multiplies with the others, and they're independent:

**Coalesce by key.** Within a tick, keep the latest per entity. Nine hundred robots at 10 Hz over a 250ms tick is at most 900 entries, not 2,250.

**Send only what's rendered.** Forty visible robots out of 900 is a 22× reduction, and it's usually the largest single win in a real system. Costs client complexity: subscriptions change on scroll and zoom, which needs debouncing and a margin of prefetch around the viewport.

**Quantize to display precision.** Position to 0.1m, battery to 1%, percentages to integers. Now "changed" means "changed visibly," and a robot sitting still emits nothing at all. Pair it with a **max-silence rule** — publish unchanged state at least every 30 seconds — so a client can distinguish "not moving" from "not reporting," and so anything missed during a gap converges on its own.

**Send fields, not documents.** A patch of what changed rather than the whole entity. Requires a base snapshot and ordering, which you need anyway.

**Aggregate at the source.** Counts, rates, and percentiles computed server-side over the whole population, published once per second as their own stream. This is the fix for the drifting header, and there's no client-side version of it that works.

**Downsample for charts.** A panel 800 pixels wide can display 800 points. Sending 50,000 is wasted bytes that render identically or worse — the extra points overplot into a smear. Bucket server-side and send min/max/average per bucket, or use a shape-preserving algorithm if the peaks matter.

### Render is not receive

The highest-value client-side change, and it needs no protocol change at all: **apply messages into a store immediately, and render on an animation frame.**

```
message → store.apply()                  (cheap, synchronous, no DOM)
requestAnimationFrame → render(store)    (at most 60/sec, usually fewer)
```

Twenty messages arriving in one frame produce one render instead of twenty. The opening's melted browser is usually fixed by this alone, before anyone touches the server — which is why it's level 0 of the adoption path.

Two client techniques worth knowing alongside it:

**Interpolation beats frequency.** Two position updates a second with smooth interpolation between them looks *better* than ten updates a second rendered as jumps. Games have known this for twenty-five years and dashboards keep rediscovering it. The same trick extends to extrapolation — send velocity with position and let the client predict — which buys smoothness at the cost of occasionally being wrong and correcting.

**Move parsing off the main thread** when payloads are large: a worker that parses and diffs, posting compact updates to the UI thread. Worth it well after the cheaper wins.

### Events get the opposite treatment

Everything above is for state. For events, all of it is wrong:

- Never coalesce, never sample, never quantize.
- Give them their own fast path, bypassing the tick — a collision alert should not wait 250ms behind a batch of positions.
- Bound the *display* rather than the data: keep the last 500 in memory, virtualize the list. Losing what's rendered is fine; losing the record is not, and the record lives in your durable store anyway.
- When a client genuinely can't keep up with events, that's a real backpressure decision from 9.1b's menu — slow, disconnect and resync, or **degrade**: switch them to a summarized mode ("47 alerts in the last minute") and make the switch visible in the UI.

The way to stop the opening's last failure from recurring is to put the classification **in the protocol**. Every message declares its kind; the queue and the client apply policy by that field rather than by a developer remembering which id belongs to which stream.

### Reconnection: snapshot, not replay

For state, replaying the messages a client missed is strictly worse than sending the current value: more bytes, more latency, and every intermediate state is worthless by definition. So a state stream's resume path is:

```
gap detected (seq jump)  →  request snapshot  →  resume deltas from snapshot's seq
```

This is the opposite of 9.2's log resume, where every missed line mattered and replay was the whole point. Same connection, same resume machinery, different answer — because the kind of data is different. That's the article's thesis in one comparison.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Producer rate exceeds roughly five updates per second per rendered entity.** Below that, send it and move on.
- **The underlying set is much larger than the visible set** — hundreds of entities, dozens on screen. Viewport subscription pays immediately.
- **Values are continuous and read approximately**: positions, temperatures, percentages, progress. Quantization is nearly free here and often removes most traffic.
- **Charts with more points than pixels.** Downsample server-side, always.
- **Any aggregate.** Compute at the source, without exception, regardless of rate.
- **Clients on variable networks**, where snapshot-and-resync beats replay every time.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A handful of updates per second in total.** Send them. Every technique here is machinery with its own bugs.
- **Event feeds** — chat, audit logs, order books, notifications. Shaping them is deleting the product.
- **When intermediate values are the point.** Trade executions, medical telemetry, sensor recordings kept for compliance, anything used to reconstruct an incident. Which yields the rule worth carrying: **shape the view, never the log.** The durable record stores raw; the screen gets the shaped stream. Systems that shape at ingest can't un-shape later.
- **Latency-critical interaction.** A 250ms tick is fatal for a game or an execution path. Those systems tick at 30–60 Hz with binary payloads and client-side prediction — the same techniques, tuned two orders of magnitude differently.
- **Small internal dashboards.** Twelve entities and five users don't need a tick loop.

## Trade-offs

**You have deliberately added latency**, up to one tick, to everything that goes through it. Acceptable for state and unacceptable for alerts, which is why the fast path isn't optional.

**Smooth rendering is a lie you're choosing to tell.** Interpolation draws a robot at a position it never occupied; extrapolation draws one where it might not be. Fine for an operator's situational awareness, wrong for anything used as evidence. If the console is ever used to reconstruct an incident, the reconstruction must come from the raw log, and the UI should make clear which mode it's in.

**Quantization hides slow drift.** A value creeping below your threshold never publishes, which is exactly what you wanted until it isn't. The max-silence rule is the safety net, and it's the thing most implementations forget until a stale value causes an incident.

**Two representations of the same data will drift.** Snapshots and deltas are separate code paths, and the classic bug is a field present in the snapshot and never emitted in a diff — correct on load, stale forever afterwards, and invisible in testing because tests load fresh. Derive both from one schema, and add a test that compares a fresh snapshot against a snapshot plus replayed deltas.

**Viewport subscriptions generate churn.** Scrolling produces subscribe/unsubscribe storms; debounce, prefetch a margin, and expect this to be the fiddliest client code in the feature.

**Per-client filtering costs server CPU.** A tick that loops clients × dirty entities is fine at 500 × 900 and isn't at 50,000 × 900. The fix is an inverted index — entity to interested clients — which is more state to maintain and keep correct.

**Debugging gets multi-layered.** "The number on my screen is wrong" now has six candidate explanations: the source, the quantizer, the coalescer, the subscription filter, the delta application, and the renderer. Being able to dump the server's authoritative state for one entity, on demand, stops being a nice-to-have.

## Alternatives

- **Poll a cached snapshot once a second** (9.1a, level 0). For dashboards specifically, this is a strong and underrated answer: one endpoint, one computation shared by every viewer, cacheable for a second at the edge, no per-client state, no tick loop, no subscription churn. A one-second granularity is more than the human budget requires. Many "real-time dashboards" should be this.
- **Hybrid: poll the aggregates, push the events.** Matches the taxonomy exactly and is what a lot of mature systems converge on.
- **Query a time-series store for charts** rather than streaming points, and let it do the bucketing — that's what it's for.
- **Client-side prediction (dead reckoning).** Send position and velocity at 2 Hz, let the client extrapolate, correct on the next update. Standard in games, applicable to any smooth-motion display.
- **Server-rendered frames.** At the extreme, the server renders the view and ships pixels or draw commands. Used for trading floors and cloud gaming; enormous bandwidth, zero client CPU, and a completely different failure profile.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — decouple render from receive.** Apply to a store on arrival, render on an animation frame. Client-only, no protocol change, and it's usually the difference between unusable and fine.
*Next when:* the network or the parse cost is the bottleneck rather than rendering.

**Level 1 — tick and coalesce on the server.** A dirty set flushed every 200–500ms, latest-per-key. One change, largest server-side win.
*Next when:* you notice a policy that's correct for one stream being applied to another.

**Level 2 — classify streams in the envelope.** `kind: state | event`, a fast path for events, and queue policy driven by the field rather than by memory.
*Next when:* clients are receiving far more entities than they display.

**Level 3 — scope to the viewport**, with debounce and a prefetch margin.
*Next when:* payloads are still large, or idle entities still generate traffic.

**Level 4 — quantize, patch, and enforce max-silence.** Send changed fields at display precision; publish unchanged state periodically anyway.
*Next when:* derived numbers disagree with reports, or charts are heavy.

**Level 5 — aggregate and downsample at the source**, and make reconnection a snapshot rather than a replay.

## Implementation

TypeScript, both ends, because the two halves only make sense together: the server's tick loop and the client's frame loop are the same idea applied at different rates, and the bug the article opens with is what happens when either is missing.

The server keeps one tick for all clients:

```ts
const world = new Map<string, Robot>();
const dirty = new Map<string, Partial<Robot>>();   // entity → merged changes
let seq = 0;

function onTelemetry(id: string, next: Robot) {
  const prev = world.get(id);
  const diff = visibleDiff(prev, next);            // ← aside 1
  if (!diff) return;

  world.set(id, next);
  dirty.set(id, { ...dirty.get(id), ...diff });    // ← aside 2
}

setInterval(() => {
  if (dirty.size === 0) return;
  seq++;

  for (const client of clients) {
    const patch: Record<string, Partial<Robot>> = {};
    for (const [id, diff] of dirty) {
      if (client.viewport.has(id)) patch[id] = diff;   // ← aside 3
    }
    if (Object.keys(patch).length > 0) {
      client.send({ kind: "state", seq, patch });
    }
  }
  dirty.clear();
}, 250);
```

Alerts don't go near any of that:

```ts
function onAlert(a: Alert) {
  for (const client of clients) {
    client.send({ kind: "event", id: a.id, alert: a });   // ← aside 4
  }
}
```

And the client:

```ts
const store = new Map<string, Robot>();
let lastSeq = 0;
let frameQueued = false;

socket.onMessage((m) => {
  if (m.kind === "event") { feed.append(m.alert); scheduleRender(); return; }

  if (m.seq !== lastSeq + 1) { requestSnapshot(); return; }   // ← aside 5
  lastSeq = m.seq;

  for (const [id, diff] of Object.entries(m.patch)) {
    store.set(id, { ...(store.get(id) ?? blank(id)), ...diff });
  }
  scheduleRender();
});

function scheduleRender() {                                    // ← aside 6
  if (frameQueued) return;
  frameQueued = true;
  requestAnimationFrame(() => { frameQueued = false; render(store); });
}
```

**Aside 1 — `visibleDiff` is where most of the traffic disappears.** It compares at display precision — position to 0.1m, battery to whole percent — so a robot parked at a charging station produces literally nothing, and a robot creeping forward produces an update every few samples instead of every sample. The companion rule isn't shown and is required: a sweep that republishes any entity silent for 30 seconds, so "stationary" and "stopped reporting" remain distinguishable and any client that missed an update converges.

**Aside 2 — coalescing happens here, once, not in 500 send queues.** 9.1b put coalescing in the per-connection queue as a survival policy for a slow client. Doing it upstream is a different act: the work is done once for everyone, before anything is serialized, whether or not any client is struggling. Shaping at the source; the queue policy stays as the last line of defence.

**Aside 3 — this loop is clients × dirty, and you should know where that stops being fine.** At 500 clients and 900 dirty entities it's 450,000 cheap set lookups every 250ms, which is nothing. At 50,000 clients it is not, and the fix is an inverted index from entity to interested clients so the loop visits only what changed. Worth knowing the shape now; not worth building until the number says so.

**Aside 4 — the fast path exists so the coalescer can't eat an alert.** This is the opening's last failure, prevented structurally rather than by a comment. Because the message declares `kind`, every downstream policy — queue behaviour, client handling, what happens on a gap — reads it from the envelope instead of inferring it from the payload.

**Aside 5 — a gap means snapshot, not replay.** For state, requesting the 9,000 missed messages is slower, larger, and arrives at the same answer as asking for the current one. Note this is precisely the opposite of 9.2's build log, where the gap had to be replayed line by line. The resume machinery is identical; the correct response depends entirely on which kind of stream it is.

**Aside 6 — the whole opening incident, solved in six lines.** Twenty patches arriving in one frame schedule one render. Without this gate, every message triggers a render, the main thread never yields, and no amount of server-side shaping fully compensates — which is why this is level 0 and the server work is level 1.

**What's not here.** The snapshot endpoint that `requestSnapshot` calls must produce exactly the shape that patches assume, and nothing in this code enforces that. A field that appears in the snapshot but is never included in a `visibleDiff` will be correct on page load and stale forever after — the bug tests don't catch, because tests load fresh. One schema for both, and a test that compares a fresh snapshot against a snapshot plus replayed patches.

## Further Reading

- **Glenn Fiedler, "Networked Physics" and the snapshot-interpolation articles** (gafferongames.com) — the best writing anywhere on state streams: snapshots, interpolation, delta encoding, and why the client's display clock runs behind the server's. Game networking solved this decades before dashboards met it.
- **"Source Multiplayer Networking"** (Valve developer wiki) — tick rate, entity interpolation, and prediction described concretely enough to copy the structure.
- **Sveinn Steinarsson, "Downsampling Time Series for Visual Representation"** (MSc thesis, University of Iceland, 2013) — the largest-triangle-three-buckets algorithm, and a clear account of why naive averaging destroys the features people are looking at.
- **Grafana's documentation on max data points and interval**, and Prometheus's `step` parameter — how mature dashboards decide how many points to ask for, which is always "about one per pixel."
- **Jakob Nielsen, "Response Times: The 3 Important Limits"** — the 0.1 / 1 / 10 second thresholds, and the source for treating human perception as a budget rather than an afterthought.
- **MDN, `requestAnimationFrame`**, plus web.dev's rendering-performance material — the frame budget the client half of this article is spending.
- **_Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, ch. 11**, on windowing and materialized aggregates — for the server-side computation of derived values, and why recomputing beats accumulating.

A Go sibling would be worth writing for the server half at a scale this one can't reach: the inverted index from aside 3, a binary encoding instead of JSON, and a tick loop that allocates nothing per frame. The mechanism is identical and the constraints are entirely different, which is exactly when a second language earns its place. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: before optimizing a live feature, ask what the consumer can actually absorb — bytes, messages, and meaning — and notice that the smallest of those three numbers is usually the person. Systems that send more than the consumer can take aren't merely wasteful; they spend real money to produce a worse experience, and every layer below them has to be built bigger to carry data that was never going to be looked at.
