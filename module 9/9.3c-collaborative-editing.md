# Collaborative Editing

_Module 9.3c — Real-Time Communication_

## The Problem

An incident-management product. During an outage, responders keep a shared runbook document open — timeline, hypotheses, actions taken — and several of them type in it at once. That's the feature: a live document, multiple cursors, no "check out."

The first implementation autosaves. Every two seconds the client PUTs the whole document.

**Work vanishes silently.** Ana adds a paragraph at 03:14:02. Dev's client, which loaded the document at 03:13:40, saves at 03:14:03 and overwrites it. There's no error, no conflict, no warning. Ana's paragraph simply isn't there when she scrolls back, and by then she isn't sure she ever wrote it. Last-write-wins doesn't fail; it succeeds at doing the wrong thing.

**The version check makes it loud instead of correct.** So the team adds optimistic concurrency — the client sends the version it loaded, the server rejects mismatches with a 409 (Module 7.4a's `If-Match`, doing exactly what it's designed to do). Now Dev gets "this document has changed, please reload," which discards his paragraph instead of Ana's. Optimistic concurrency is the right tool when conflicts are rare exceptions. Here, a conflict is what two people typing *is*.

**Locking works and kills the feature.** Check-out means one editor at a time, which for an incident runbook is worse than the bug. It also needs leases (9.3a), because responders close laptops mid-incident and a lock with no expiry is an outage inside an outage.

So the team sends operations instead of documents: `insert("L", at: 3)`, `delete(at: 0)`. Much smaller, much more precise, and now the real problem appears.

```
both replicas hold "HELO"

Ana  insert "L" at 3   → "HELLO"
Dev  delete at 0       → "ELO"          (concurrently)

Ana applies Dev's delete(0)     → "ELLO"      ✔
Dev applies Ana's insert(3, L)  → "ELOL"      ✘
```

Both replicas applied both operations. They disagree. Nothing was lost, nothing errored, and the documents are now different forever — and each user sees a plausible document, so neither reports a bug until they read each other's screens.

The cause is one sentence: **a position is not an identity.** `at: 3` means "the fourth slot of the document I was looking at," and by the time that operation arrives somewhere else, the fourth slot holds something else. Every naive collaborative editor dies here.

There's a sixth failure waiting behind it, and it surprises everyone: **Ctrl-Z undoes Dev's typing.** The undo stack is a stack of document states, and the last change to the document wasn't yours.

## What It Is

The problem class is **concurrent editing of shared mutable state**: several replicas change the same thing at the same time with no coordination, and every replica must end up identical without discarding what anyone meant.

Two properties, and it matters that they're separate:

**Convergence** — all replicas end up with the same value. Last-write-wins converges perfectly; it's convergent and useless.

**Intention preservation** — the converged value reflects what each person was trying to do. This is the hard one, it's not a theorem, and both families below can converge on something nobody wanted.

There are two established families, plus a third option that gets forgotten:

**Operational Transformation (OT).** Send operations, and when an operation arrives that was generated against an older state, *transform* it so it has the equivalent effect on the current state. `insert(3)` becomes `insert(2)` after a preceding delete. The theory is a function `transform(a, b)` that rewrites each operation against the other.

**CRDTs** — Conflict-free Replicated Data Types. Don't transform anything; design the data so operations commute by construction. For text, that means giving every character a unique immutable identity, so an operation says "insert after the character with id X" rather than "insert at slot 3." Any arrival order converges.

**Serialize through a single owner.** Route every edit for a document to one process, apply in arrival order, broadcast the result, and have clients rebase their unsent local changes on top. This isn't a merge theory; it's the observation that if there's one place where order is decided, a great many domains need no merge theory at all. It's also what makes server-based OT tractable in practice, and — this is the part worth internalizing early — it's sufficient on its own for a surprising share of "collaborative" products.

Two neighbours to keep separate. **Git-style merging** is the same word for a different problem: coarse granularity, a human resolving conflicts, at a moment of their choosing. Real-time editing merges continuously, per keystroke, with no human available. And **eventual consistency** in a database is the same family of ideas at a different granularity — here the user is watching the convergence happen, which removes most of the tolerances a backend system enjoys.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Two people edit; one person's work disappears with no error.
- "This document has changed, please reload."
- A lock exists, and users complain both about being blocked and about stale locks.
- Your cursor jumps when someone types above you.
- Undo undoes someone else's change.
- Two browsers show different text and refreshing "fixes" it.
- You are writing a `transform` function and discovering new cases every week.
- Offline edits either fail to sync or clobber on reconnect.
- Someone has proposed "just save the whole document, it's small."

## How It Works

### Operational Transformation

An operation carries the version it was generated against. Before applying an incoming operation, transform it against every operation applied since that version:

```
transform(insert(a, text), insert(b, other)):
    a <  b   → unchanged
    a >  b   → insert(a + len(other), text)
    a == b   → tie-break by site id        ← intent becomes a policy choice
```

That's the easy pair. There's also insert/delete, delete/insert, and delete/delete, each with overlap cases; add formatting attributes and the matrix grows again; add a tree structure for rich text and it grows once more. The field has a genuine history of published OT algorithms later proven incorrect, which is not a cheap shot — it's the reason server-mediated OT won. With a single server deciding the order, the problem reduces to transforming each client's operations against a linear history, which is tractable. Peer-to-peer OT with n sites is where the proofs failed.

The client half is a small state machine worth knowing even if you use a library:

```
server state:   ─────●──────────●─────────────●──────►  authoritative order
client:         acked version ──┬── in flight ──┬── buffered local edits
                                └─ one op sent, awaiting ack
```

One operation in flight, local edits buffered behind it, and the client applies its own edits immediately so typing feels instant. When the server acks, the buffer is transformed and the next one goes. Typing latency is decoupled from the network, which is the entire reason this architecture feels good — and is also why a bug in the transform shows up as text that scrambles only under latency.

### CRDTs

Stop numbering slots. Give every character an identifier that never changes, and define insertion relative to an identifier:

```
"HELO"   →   H₁  E₂  L₃  O₄

Ana:  insert "L" after L₃   → id (ana, 5)
Dev:  delete H₁             → tombstone

any application order  →  E₂ L₃ L₅ O₄  =  "ELLO"   ✔
```

The divergence from the opening is gone, and not because of a cleverer algorithm — because the operation refers to a *character* rather than a slot, and the character it refers to is still there. Concurrent insertions after the same parent are ordered by a deterministic rule on their identifiers, so every replica breaks the tie the same way.

Deletion is a **tombstone** rather than a removal, because a concurrent operation may still name that character as its anchor. That's the central cost: metadata per character, tombstones that accumulate, and garbage collection that can't run until you know every replica has seen the deletion — which, without a central authority, you may never know.

Two corrections to the usual marketing:

**CRDTs don't remove the server.** Nearly every production deployment runs one, as a relay, as persistence, and as the place authorization happens. What they remove is the need for it to be *smart*: it doesn't order, doesn't transform, and doesn't need to understand the document.

**Convergence isn't correctness.** If Ana and Dev type a word at the same position simultaneously, identifier ordering can interleave their characters into nonsense. Every replica agrees on the nonsense. Production algorithms (YATA in Yjs, Fugue) add rules to keep each author's run contiguous; it's a known class of anomaly with papers about it, not an implementation slip.

### Choose the unit of conflict before choosing an algorithm

This is the step that's usually skipped, and it's worth more than either family.

Character-level merge is necessary when two people type in the same paragraph at the same time. It is *unnecessary* when the document is made of units that concurrent editors rarely share:

- **A form or a card:** conflict per field. Two people editing different fields never conflict; last-write-wins per field plus "Dev is editing this" is enough.
- **A spreadsheet:** conflict per cell.
- **A block editor:** conflict per block, with character-level merge only inside a block.
- **A design canvas:** conflict per object property. Figma is the well-documented example — a server-authoritative design where each property has a last-writer, deliberately *not* text OT, because two designers dragging the same rectangle is a different problem from two people typing in one sentence.

Splitting a document into smaller units of conflict reduces concurrency to something a much simpler mechanism handles, and it costs nothing but schema design. Reach for OT or a CRDT for the units that genuinely need it, and not for the rest.

### The parts that are most of the work

- **Cursors and selections have the position problem too.** A remote cursor at offset 40 must move when text is inserted above it. With a CRDT, anchor it to a character id; with OT, transform it on every operation. Getting this wrong is the most-noticed defect in a collaborative editor, because everyone watches everyone's cursor.
- **Undo must be per-user and selective.** Undoing your own change means applying its inverse *transformed against everything that happened since*, which may no longer be a clean inverse. Both families make this hard; the library you choose should already have an answer and you should check what it is.
- **Offline changes.** Hours of divergence is structurally fine for a CRDT and requires the OT server to retain enough history to transform against. This is the single clearest reason to pick a CRDT.
- **Persistence and loading.** You store document state plus a log, snapshot periodically, and compact. With a CRDT, the stored form is a binary structure you must be able to read for as long as the document exists — a data-format commitment, not a JSON column.
- **Authorization is coarse.** Character-level operations can't easily express "may edit the summary but not the timeline." Access control lands at the document, occasionally at the block.
- **Server-side inspection is limited.** If your server can't interpret operations semantically, content moderation, data-loss prevention, and validation have to run on materialized state rather than at write time.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Simultaneous typing in the same paragraph**, which is documents, notes, and code. There's no way around one of the two families here.
- **Offline-first or long divergence** — a field app where someone edits for four hours without signal. CRDT, clearly.
- **No trusted central authority**, peer-to-peer or local-first. CRDT, necessarily.
- **Server-mediated live text with a normal web architecture**: both work; the modern default is a CRDT library, because the integration burden is lower and offline comes free.
- **Coarse objects with low concurrency** — cards, shapes, cells, properties. Single-owner serialization with per-field resolution, and skip the rest of this article.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **One editor at a time in practice.** Most internal tools. Optimistic concurrency plus a presence indicator ("Ana is editing") prevents nearly every real collision, and the remaining ones get an honest conflict dialog.
- **Documents where locking is correct** — a contract under legal review, a financial close. Sequential editing is sometimes the requirement, not a limitation. Use a lease, not a flag.
- **Append-only content.** Chat, comments, audit entries. There's no merge problem, only an ordering question, which 9.1 already answered.
- **Records with natural per-field ownership.** A CRM contact edited by two people is two field updates, not a merge.
- **Writing your own.** Bluntly: don't. The OT literature contains a decade of algorithms published and later disproven, and sequence CRDTs have subtle anomalies that took researchers years to characterize. Use Yjs, Automerge, ShareDB, or your editor framework's collaboration module. The interesting work in your product is the integration, not the algorithm.

## Trade-offs

**Document state grows with editing history, not with content length.** Per-character identifiers and tombstones mean a heavily-edited paragraph can carry many times its visible size in metadata. Mature libraries compress runs aggressively and it's usually fine — but "usually fine" is a property of the library, and it deserves a load test with a realistic year of edits rather than a fresh document.

**Garbage collection needs a definition of "everyone."** Tombstones can only be dropped once every replica has seen the deletion, which is easy with a server that knows all clients and impossible in the general peer-to-peer case.

**Debugging divergence is genuinely hard.** When two clients disagree, the useful artifacts are both replicas' full internal state and the operation log, and you need tooling to dump and diff them. Without it, the bug report is "the text was weird" and there is nothing to look at.

**Convergence guarantees agreement, not sense.** Interleaved words are the canonical example. Everyone agrees; nobody wanted it.

**The persisted format is a long-term commitment.** Migrating a CRDT document store is harder than migrating a table. Check that your library has a documented upgrade path before the first document is saved.

**Attribution and history cost extra.** "Who wrote this sentence" requires per-character authorship retained over time — some libraries support it, all of them charge for it in space.

**Undo will take longer than you estimated.** Every time.

**Latency compensation hides bugs until production.** Local edits apply instantly, so a transform or merge defect is invisible on a fast connection and appears as scrambled text for the one user on hotel wifi.

## Alternatives

- **Optimistic concurrency with a version check.** Correct, cheap, and appropriate whenever conflicts are genuinely rare. Pair it with presence so people avoid collisions rather than resolving them.
- **Leased locks / check-out.** Sometimes the requirement. Needs 9.3a's expiry, or abandoned locks become support tickets.
- **Per-field or per-cell last-write-wins.** The right answer for structured records, and it scales to fairly rich products before it runs out.
- **Single-owner serialization.** Route a document's edits to one node (9.1c's partitioning argument, and the payoff for it), apply in arrival order, broadcast, have clients rebase. No merge theory, and it handles coarse operations well.
- **Three-way merge at save time.** Git's model, applied at a human's pace with a human resolving. Appropriate when edits are long-lived and review is expected.
- **Domain-specific commutative operations.** Design your operations so they commute for your domain: `move_card(id, to_column, position_after: card_x)` rather than `set_position(3)`. This is the CRDT insight applied by hand to a small operation set, and for a kanban board it's a day of thought instead of a library.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — pick the unit of conflict.** Split the document into fields, cells, or blocks so that most concurrent editing doesn't touch the same unit. Largest reduction available, and it's schema design rather than distributed systems.
*Next when:* two people regularly edit the same unit.

**Level 1 — optimistic concurrency plus presence.** Version per unit, 409 on mismatch, and a live indicator of who is editing what. Prevents silent loss; makes the remaining conflicts visible and rare.
*Next when:* conflict dialogs are a normal part of the workflow rather than an exception.

**Level 2 — single-owner serialization.** All edits for a document go to one process, applied in arrival order, broadcast to the rest; clients rebase pending local changes. No merge algorithm, and it's enough for coarse operations.
*Next when:* users type into the same paragraph simultaneously and rebasing discards their keystrokes.

**Level 3 — adopt a library for the units that need it.** Yjs or Automerge, the server as relay and persistence, character-level merge only where it's warranted.
*Next when:* it works and people start noticing the surrounding features.

**Level 4 — cursors anchored to identities, per-user undo, offline sync.** The part that makes it feel finished.

**Level 5 — snapshots, compaction, attribution, and history.** Operational work that grows with document age rather than user count.

## Implementation

Go, and deliberately a toy: a minimal identity-based sequence, enough to show *why* commutativity falls out of the data design. This is a teaching artifact, not a starting point — the gap between it and Yjs is several papers wide, and the asides say where.

```go
type ID struct {
	Site string
	Seq  uint64
}

type Char struct {
	ID      ID
	Parent  ID     // "insert me after this character"
	Value   rune
	Deleted bool
}

type Doc struct {
	chars    map[ID]*Char
	children map[ID][]*Char   // parent → ordered concurrent inserts
}
```

Applying a remote insert:

```go
func (d *Doc) Apply(c Char) {
	if _, seen := d.chars[c.ID]; seen {
		return                                     // ← aside 1
	}
	if _, ok := d.chars[c.Parent]; !ok && c.Parent != rootID {
		d.pending = append(d.pending, c)           // ← aside 2
		return
	}

	d.chars[c.ID] = &c
	sibs := d.children[c.Parent]
	i := sort.Search(len(sibs), func(i int) bool {
		return idLess(c.ID, sibs[i].ID)            // ← aside 3
	})
	d.children[c.Parent] = slices.Insert(sibs, i, &c)
	d.drainPending()
}

func (d *Doc) Delete(id ID) {
	if c, ok := d.chars[id]; ok {
		c.Deleted = true                           // ← aside 4
	}
}
```

Reading the document is a walk:

```go
func (d *Doc) Value() string {
	var b strings.Builder
	var walk func(parent ID)
	walk = func(parent ID) {
		for _, c := range d.children[parent] {
			if !c.Deleted {
				b.WriteRune(c.Value)
			}
			walk(c.ID)
		}
	}
	walk(rootID)
	return b.String()
}
```

**Aside 1 — `Apply` is idempotent, and that closes a loop from 9.1a.** Every transport in this module delivers at-least-once and may reorder; a duplicate operation here is a no-op and a reordered one still converges. The delivery guarantees we settled for earlier are exactly the guarantees this data structure needs, which is not a coincidence — it's why CRDTs pair so naturally with ordinary unreliable fan-out.

**Aside 2 — causal readiness is the requirement people miss.** An insert whose parent hasn't arrived can't be placed, so it must be buffered rather than applied or dropped. Homegrown implementations that skip this appear to work over a reliable connection and corrupt documents over a lossy one, which is the worst possible test profile. Production systems carry a version vector so a replica can say precisely what it has and request the rest.

**Aside 3 — `idLess` is the entire conflict resolution, and it is arbitrary.** Two characters inserted concurrently after the same parent are ordered by a deterministic comparison of their identifiers. Every replica breaks the tie identically, which is all convergence requires. Neither order is more correct, and that's the honest answer to "what happens if we type at the same spot" — a rule, not a judgment.

**Aside 4 — deletion is a flag, not a removal.** A concurrent insert may name this character as its parent, so removing it would strand that operation. Hence tombstones, hence unbounded growth, hence the garbage-collection problem that needs a definition of "everyone has seen this." A central relay can supply that definition cheaply; a peer-to-peer deployment often can't.

**What this toy gets wrong, on purpose.** Characters typed consecutively by two people at the same position can interleave into nonsense — convergent and unusable — which is what YATA and Fugue exist to prevent. There's no run compression, so a document costs a struct per character. No rich text, no cursors, no undo, no persistence format, no version vector. Every one of those is where the real engineering is, and it's why the recommendation is a library.

## Further Reading

- **Ellis and Gibbs, "Concurrency Control in Groupware Systems"** (SIGMOD 1989) — where OT starts. Short, and the position-transform idea is clearer in the original than in most retellings.
- **Sun and Ellis, "Operational Transformation in Real-Time Group Editors: Issues, Algorithms, and Achievements"** (CSCW 1998) — the honest survey, including which published algorithms turned out to be wrong.
- **Joseph Gentle's writing on OT** (he worked on Google Wave, then wrote ShareJS) — the source of the widely-quoted advice not to implement your own, from someone with standing to say it.
- **Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero, Zawirski, "Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types"** (2011) — the foundational treatment; the sequence types are the relevant part here.
- **Nicolaescu, Jahns, Derntl, Klamma, "Near Real-Time Peer-to-Peer Shared Editing on Extensible Data Types"** (2016) — the YATA algorithm behind Yjs, and the most practical of the papers.
- **Kleppmann, Gomes, Mulligan, Beresford, "Interleaving anomalies in collaborative text editors"** (2019) — short, and it will change how you evaluate a library.
- **Kleppmann and Beresford, "A Conflict-Free Replicated JSON Datatype"** (2017), plus **"Local-first software"** (2019) — the basis of Automerge and the argument for the whole approach.
- **Evan Wallace, "How Figma's multiplayer technology works"** (Figma blog, 2019) — the best available account of deliberately *not* using text OT, choosing per-property resolution and a server-authoritative design instead. Read it alongside the "unit of conflict" section.
- **ProseMirror's collaborative editing guide and `prosemirror-collab`** — a documented, rebase-based production implementation small enough to read end to end.

A TypeScript sibling would be worth writing and would be a genuinely different article: not the algorithm, but the integration — wiring Yjs to an editor, the awareness protocol for cursors and presence, choosing a provider, persisting and compacting documents server-side, and where authorization actually attaches when the server can't read the operations. That's where the time goes on a real project. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: when two people can change the same thing, ask what the unit of conflict is before asking how to merge. Most of the difficulty in collaborative features is created by choosing a unit that's too big — a whole document, a whole record — and most of it disappears by choosing a smaller one. The merge algorithms in this article are what you need for the residue that's left after that choice is made well.
