# Choosing a Fan-Out Substrate

_Module 9.4 — Real-Time Communication_

## The Problem

An online auction platform. Lots run on countdown timers, bids arrive in bursts in the final thirty seconds, watchers see the price update live, and anyone who gets outbid needs to know immediately or they lose the lot.

Following 9.1c, the team added a backplane so that a bid landing on one pod reaches watchers connected to another. They used Redis Pub/Sub, because Redis was already running. It worked.

Then someone asks the reasonable question — "we run Kafka for the data platform, why are we running two of these?" — and nobody in the room can articulate the difference beyond preference. There are four candidates, no criteria, and the decision gets made on familiarity.

Here is what familiarity cost, over the following year.

**A garbage-collection pause lost 3,000 outbid notifications.** A pod stalled for 400ms during a peak, Redis breached the pub/sub output-buffer limit for that subscriber, and disconnected it. When it reconnected forty seconds later, the intervening messages were gone — not delayed, gone. Users found out they'd been outbid by refreshing. Pub/Sub is at-most-once and that is a documented property, not a bug; nobody had read the sentence.

**Kafka fixed that and broke something else.** The natural model was a topic per lot. With 200,000 open lots, that's 200,000 topics, which is a partition and metadata problem rather than a design. The alternative — one topic, every pod consuming everything and filtering — is exactly the N×M broadcast bus 9.1c argued against, now with a heavier operator manual. And each deploy triggered consumer-group rebalances during which delivery paused for several seconds, in a product where the last thirty seconds of a lot are the entire business.

**RabbitMQ's queue-per-connection became the broker's problem.** A queue per WebSocket, bound to a fanout exchange, is an elegant model until 40,000 users close their laptops and 40,000 queues keep accumulating messages nobody will ever read. Broker memory climbed, the memory alarm fired, and RabbitMQ did what it's designed to do: blocked the publishers. A slow consumer had stopped the auction.

**And the audit log had holes.** Somewhere along the way, the bid *record* started travelling on the same channel as the bid *notification*. A fire-and-forget broadcast was the transport for a financial fact.

Four incidents, four different properties of four different systems. None of them was the wrong technology in general; each was the wrong technology for a requirement nobody had written down.

## What It Is

The component is a **fan-out substrate**: the thing that carries a message from the process that produced it to the processes that need it. 9.1c established that you need one at N=2 and said almost nothing about which. This article is the criteria.

The most useful first distinction is not between products but between three delivery shapes:

```
bus    (pub/sub)   message → every current subscriber, then it's gone
queue  (work)      message → exactly one of the competing consumers, acked
log    (stream)    message appended; consumers hold cursors; replay possible
```

Most real confusion comes from conflating these. A work queue distributes labour; a bus distributes information; a log distributes information *and remembers it*. For a real-time fan-out plane you want a bus or a log, essentially never a work queue — if two pods compete for a message, one pod's users don't get it.

The axes that decide everything else, in rough order of how often they cause an incident:

1. **What happens to a slow consumer** — dropped, disconnected, buffered, or does the producer get blocked?
2. **Retention and replay** — gone on delivery, or retrievable by cursor?
3. **Topic cardinality** — are topics free and dynamic, or heavyweight and provisioned?
4. **Ordering scope** — per publisher, per queue, per partition, per stream, or none?
5. **Consumer state** — does the broker track who has what?
6. **Latency, including during failover** — the p99 that matters is the one during a leader election.
7. **Operational cost** — what it takes to run, upgrade, and fail over.

And the structural point the opening incidents are really about: **there are two different jobs here, and one technology rarely serves both well.**

- The **fan-out plane** (this module): ephemeral, wide, latency-critical, loss survivable because 9.1b's sequence numbers let a client notice and resync.
- The **event backbone** (Module 37): durable business facts, consumed once per service, replayable for months, where loss is unacceptable and a few hundred milliseconds is fine.

Running both is normal and correct. The auction's bid record belongs on the backbone; the price update belongs on the fan-out plane; and the fourth incident is what happens when that line is never drawn.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The broker was chosen because it was already running, and nobody can state its delivery guarantee.
- Messages disappear under load with no error on either side.
- Broker memory or storage grows with the number of *connected users*.
- A deploy produces a multi-second delivery gap.
- Topic, queue, or partition count grows with a business object, and somebody is nervous about it.
- The same channel carries "update the UI" and "record that this happened."
- Nobody can answer: what happens to a message if this consumer is down for five seconds? For five minutes?
- A slow consumer has, at some point, slowed down a producer.
- Your durable record is derived from a channel that doesn't retain anything.

## How It Works

### Slow-consumer behaviour is the axis that decides most incidents

Every substrate has a policy for "the consumer can't keep up," and it is almost always the property people discover last:

| | policy when a consumer falls behind | loss visible? |
|---|---|---|
| **Redis Pub/Sub** | buffers to `client-output-buffer-limit` (32MB hard / 8MB over 60s by default), then **disconnects the subscriber** | no — silent |
| **Redis Streams** | nothing; data sits in the stream until trimmed by `MAXLEN`/`MINID` | yes — entry ids reveal a gap |
| **NATS core** | drops messages for that subscriber, raises a *slow consumer* error | yes — the client is told |
| **NATS JetStream** | retains per consumer; acks and redelivery | yes |
| **RabbitMQ (classic)** | queue grows until a memory/disk alarm, then **publishers are blocked** | yes, loudly, in the wrong place |
| **Kafka** | consumers pull; falling behind is free until retention passes, then `OffsetOutOfRange` | yes — a hard, loud error |

Read that column as the failure you're choosing. Redis Pub/Sub is fast and it fails silently. RabbitMQ is safe and it converts a consumer problem into a producer outage. Kafka is safe and it tells you at the worst possible moment, which is still better than not telling you.

This is 9.1b's backpressure menu — block, drop, coalesce, disconnect — reappearing one layer down as an infrastructure property. You chose a policy for your WebSocket send queues; the broker has chosen one for you here, and it's worth knowing which.

### Topic cardinality decides the architecture

9.1c's per-topic subscription only works if topics are cheap:

- **Redis channels** and **NATS subjects** are free, created implicitly, and effectively unlimited. NATS additionally gives hierarchical subjects and wildcards (`lot.*.bids`), which is the best available fit for this job.
- **RabbitMQ** has cheap routing keys, but queues and bindings are broker state — one per consumer means broker state proportional to your connection count, plus binding churn on every join.
- **Kafka topics are heavyweight**: partitions are files, metadata is controller state, and a topic per business object is not viable. The correct Kafka model is few topics, key-based partitioning, and consumer-side filtering — which is the broadcast bus, with its N×M arithmetic intact.

One Redis-specific trap worth naming: in **Redis Cluster**, classic `PUBLISH` is propagated to every node in the cluster, so scaling Redis horizontally does not scale pub/sub — it multiplies it. Redis 7's sharded pub/sub (`SSUBSCRIBE`/`SPUBLISH`) fixes this by keying channels to slots, and it's a different client API.

And a Kafka-specific one: consumer groups give *competing* consumers within a group and broadcast *across* groups. To fan out to every pod, each pod needs its own group id — so ephemeral pods create ephemeral consumer groups, offsets accumulate, and every scale event triggers a rebalance. Cooperative rebalancing and static membership exist precisely to make this less bad, and they're configuration you have to know to look for.

### A log deletes two problems at once

9.1c ended with a node sequencing gap: two pods publishing to the same topic have no order between them, so the sequence numbers 9.1b's gap detection depends on can't be assigned per-pod. And 9.1b ended with a replay buffer living in a process that a reconnecting client may never reach again.

Both disappear if the substrate is a log rather than a bus. The entry id **is** the sequence number, assigned at a single point by construction, and **is** the cursor a reconnecting client resumes from, readable by any pod.

```
bus:   pod → [ broker ] → pods            order: per publisher   replay: none
log:   pod → [ append ] → pods read at own cursor
                            order: total per stream   replay: to retention limit
```

That's the strongest single argument in this article: if you're about to build a replay buffer and a shared sequence counter on top of a pub/sub bus, you're building a log badly. Redis Streams and NATS JetStream are the low-ceremony versions; Kafka is the version with a much bigger operator manual and much longer retention.

The cost is that retention is now a capacity decision with a product meaning attached: how far back can a client resume, times how many streams, equals disk or memory. And trimming is a job someone has to own.

### Persist, then notify

An ordering rule that prevents an entire class of bug: **commit the durable fact first, publish the notification second.**

If the process dies between the two, a notification is lost — survivable, because clients detect the gap and resync (9.1b), or simply reload. If you publish first and the transaction rolls back, you have told thousands of users about a bid that does not exist, and no amount of client-side cleverness recovers from that.

If losing the notification is genuinely unacceptable — a payment confirmation rather than a price tick — then you have the dual-write problem that Module 8.1b handed to Module 37, and the answer there is a transactional outbox rather than a better broker.

### Latency, and the number that actually matters

For UI fan-out, all the candidates are fast enough: Redis and NATS are sub-millisecond within an availability zone, RabbitMQ around a millisecond, Kafka in the low single digits with default producer settings. Throughput is similarly a non-issue at the scale most products operate.

The number that matters is **failover**. A Redis Sentinel or Cluster failover, a Kafka partition leader election, a RabbitMQ node restart — each is seconds during which your live features silently stall while HTTP keeps working and every health check stays green. Measure and alert on end-to-end publish-to-receive latency, not on broker uptime; it's the only signal that catches this.

## When It's Appropriate

Rather than "when to use a broker" — 9.1c settled that — this is which one, with numbers.

- **Ephemeral UI fan-out, loss tolerable, many topics, minimal ops, and you already run Redis:** Redis Pub/Sub. Correct up to a few tens of thousands of messages a second, and it's correct *only because* your clients can detect a gap and resync. Without 9.1b's sequence numbers, this choice is a silent-data-loss design.
- **Same, but you want subject hierarchies, wildcards, and visible slow-consumer errors:** NATS core. Purpose-built for exactly this shape, and the operational footprint is small.
- **Resume, ordering, or bounded replay matters:** Redis Streams or NATS JetStream. This is the sweet spot for most real-time products, and the migration from a bus is small because the cursor already exists in your protocol.
- **You already run Kafka, the real-time need is coarse** (dozens of topics, high volume, per-partition ordering, consumers that are services rather than sockets): Kafka, with consumer-side filtering and a deliberate plan for consumer groups.
- **Per-consumer queues, competing consumers, priorities, delayed delivery, dead-lettering:** RabbitMQ — and notice that list is Module 10's job description, not this one's.
- **You'd rather not run any of it:** a managed real-time provider, or a cloud WebSocket gateway (API Gateway's `@connections`, Azure Web PubSub). These remove the connection plane and the fan-out plane together.

Below roughly 50,000 messages a second and ten pods, every option on this list works. The choice is about failure behaviour, not capacity.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **One node.** 9.1c, level 0. An in-process map is a fine fan-out plane for a single process.
- **Small deployments with Postgres already running.** `LISTEN`/`NOTIFY` is a real backplane: your pods subscribe, your writes notify, and you add no infrastructure. The limits are firm — an 8,000-byte payload, no retention, nothing delivered to a pod that wasn't connected — so it pairs with a cursor query rather than replacing one. For a two-or-three-pod product it's genuinely the right answer and it's rarely considered.
- **Adding Kafka for the fan-out plane alone.** The operational commitment is large, the topic model is a poor fit, and the durability you're paying for is not what this plane needs.
- **Queue-per-user on a broker that keeps queue state.** Broker state proportional to connection count is a capacity problem disguised as an elegant design.
- **A second substrate before the first one has failed you.** Two brokers is a legitimate end state, not a starting point.

## Trade-offs

**It's in the hot path of every live interaction, and it fails quietly.** When the broker is unreachable, HTTP still works, health checks stay green, and the product simply stops updating. This needs an end-to-end latency alert of its own; broker uptime will not catch it.

**Guarantees are only as strong as the client configuration.** Producer acks, retries, idempotence, consumer ack modes — a durable broker misconfigured gives you weak guarantees plus false confidence, which is worse than a bus whose limits you know.

**Durability converts a memory problem into a capacity problem.** Retention windows, trimming jobs, disk alarms, and a product question ("how far back can someone resume?") that nobody wants to own.

**Every guarantee costs latency.** Replication, acks, and fsync are the price of not losing things, and for a price tick that price buys nothing.

**Messages are a contract across processes.** During a rolling deploy two versions coexist, so payloads need forward and backward compatibility, and "we'll add a field" is a compatibility decision. The mature answer is a schema registry, which is Module 37's furniture.

**Channel naming becomes a security boundary.** A pod that subscribes to `lot:*` can see every lot; a bug in topic construction is a cross-tenant leak. Clients must never connect to the broker directly — the substrate is internal, and the socket layer is where authorization happens.

**Managed pricing has surprising shapes.** Per-message billing plus presence heartbeats plus typing indicators is a real invoice, and the cost model rewards exactly the shaping 9.3b recommends.

**Two substrates means two sets of operational knowledge**, two failover drills, and a rule about which one carries what that has to be enforced in review, because the fourth opening incident is one careless publish away.

## Alternatives

- **Postgres `LISTEN`/`NOTIFY`** plus a cursor query. No new infrastructure, honest limits, and appropriate for small clusters.
- **Tail a shared table or stream.** Each pod reads the rows it cares about by cursor. No node-to-node messaging at all, and the durable record and the fan-out source are the same thing.
- **Change data capture** (Debezium and friends, Module 38). Derive the fan-out from the database's own write log so notification can't diverge from what committed.
- **Direct node-to-node mesh.** Every pod connects to every other pod; fine at small N and no broker to run. This is what Elixir clustering does, and `Phoenix.PubSub` over distributed Erlang is the well-trodden version.
- **Managed real-time providers** — Ably, Pusher, PubNub, Supabase Realtime, Centrifugo. Centrifugo in particular is worth reading about even if you don't deploy it: its "engines" are this exact decision, documented as a product choice.
- **Cloud WebSocket gateways** — AWS API Gateway WebSocket APIs with the `@connections` management API, Azure Web PubSub, Cloudflare Durable Objects. The whole plane leaves your deployment and becomes someone else's failover problem.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — no substrate.** One pod, or Postgres `LISTEN`/`NOTIFY` plus a cursor endpoint.
*Next when:* you have two pods and a partial-delivery bug.

**Level 1 — a bus.** Redis Pub/Sub or NATS core, one channel, filtered locally. Only correct if clients can detect a gap (9.1b) — that's the precondition, not a nice-to-have.
*Next when:* broker traffic grows with pod count.

**Level 2 — per-topic subscription.** Same broker, refcounted subscribe/unsubscribe. 9.1c, level 2.
*Next when:* reconnecting clients need history, or per-topic ordering starts to matter.

**Level 3 — a log instead of a bus.** Redis Streams or JetStream; entry ids become both your sequence numbers and your resume cursors. The client protocol doesn't change, which is the payoff for having had a cursor since 9.1a.
*Next when:* durable business events start riding on the fan-out plane.

**Level 4 — split the planes.** Fan-out on the fast substrate, durable events on the backbone (Module 37), with a written rule about which carries what and `persist → notify` as the order.
*Next when:* the operational burden isn't earning its keep.

**Level 5 — offload.** A managed provider or a cloud gateway, and get the failover drills off your plate.

## Implementation

Go, and the implementation is an interface rather than an integration — because the decision this article is about is best made once, at a seam, where the requirement is visible in the type signature.

```go
type Cursor string

var ErrNoReplay = errors.New("substrate does not retain messages")

type Bus interface {
	Publish(ctx context.Context, topic string, m Message) (Cursor, error)

	// Subscribe delivers messages for topic. If from is non-empty, the
	// implementation MUST deliver retained messages after that cursor before
	// live ones, or return ErrNoReplay.
	Subscribe(ctx context.Context, topic string, from Cursor,
		fn func(Cursor, Message)) (cancel func(), err error)
}
```

The bus implementation, which cannot honour half of it:

```go
func (r *PubSub) Subscribe(ctx context.Context, topic string, from Cursor,
	fn func(Cursor, Message)) (func(), error) {

	if from != "" {
		return nil, ErrNoReplay                       // ← aside 1
	}
	sub := r.client.Subscribe(ctx, topic)
	go func() {
		for msg := range sub.Channel() {
			fn("", decode(msg.Payload))               // ← aside 2
		}
	}()
	return func() { sub.Close() }, nil
}
```

The log implementation, which can:

```go
func (s *Streams) Publish(ctx context.Context, topic string, m Message) (Cursor, error) {
	id, err := s.client.XAdd(ctx, &redis.XAddArgs{
		Stream: topic,
		MaxLen: 10_000, Approx: true,                 // ← aside 3
		Values: map[string]any{"m": encode(m)},
	}).Result()
	return Cursor(id), err
}

func (s *Streams) Subscribe(ctx context.Context, topic string, from Cursor,
	fn func(Cursor, Message)) (func(), error) {

	start := string(from)
	if start == "" {
		start = "$"                                   // live only
	}
	go func() {
		for ctx.Err() == nil {
			res, err := s.client.XRead(ctx, &redis.XReadArgs{
				Streams: []string{topic, start},
				Block:   0,                            // ← aside 4
			}).Result()
			if err != nil {
				continue
			}
			for _, msg := range res[0].Messages {
				start = msg.ID
				fn(Cursor(msg.ID), decode(msg.Values["m"]))
			}
		}
	}()
	return func() { /* cancel via ctx */ }, nil
}
```

**Aside 1 — the `from` parameter is the entire decision, made visible.** A substrate either retains messages or it doesn't, and the failure mode of pretending otherwise is that "resume" silently becomes "start from now" — correct in testing, wrong only for users who were disconnected, which is the population you built resume for. Returning `ErrNoReplay` forces the caller to have a snapshot path, which 9.3b argued they need anyway. Making a limitation explicit in a type is how you avoid meeting it during an incident.

**Aside 2 — the empty cursor is the honest answer.** A pub/sub message has no position, so there's nothing to hand back. That's the point at which you know your sequence numbers have to come from somewhere else (9.1c's `INCR`, or the application) — and the moment you wire that up, you've started reimplementing the log implementation below, at which point you should use it instead.

**Aside 3 — `MaxLen ~ 10_000` is your retention policy and your resume window in one parameter.** It answers "how long can a client be away and still resume," it's the memory bill, and the `~` matters: approximate trimming lets Redis cut at node boundaries and is dramatically cheaper than exact. Per topic, times topics, is a number worth computing before this ships rather than after.

**Aside 4 — blocking reads don't multiplex the way subscriptions do.** One pub/sub connection can carry thousands of channels; a blocking `XREAD` occupies a connection for the topics named in that call. With many topics you batch them into a single multi-stream read and re-issue as the set changes, which is real bookkeeping that the bus version doesn't need. It's the concrete cost of the log's advantages, and it's the reason the swap isn't purely free.

**What the interface deliberately doesn't hide.** It has no `PublishTransactional`. Notifications are published *after* the durable write commits, by the caller, in that order — and if the process dies in between, the message is lost and the client resyncs. Wrapping this bus in your database transaction would make it look safe and produce notifications for bids that rolled back. If you need better than that, it isn't a bus feature; it's an outbox, and it belongs in Module 37.

## Further Reading

- **Redis documentation: Pub/Sub, Streams, and the `client-output-buffer-limit` directive** — the third is where the at-most-once behaviour becomes concrete, and the Streams intro is the cheapest path from a bus to a log.
- **Redis 7 sharded Pub/Sub** (`SSUBSCRIBE`/`SPUBLISH`) — and the cluster-wide propagation behaviour it replaces, which is the trap in the *Topic cardinality* section.
- **NATS documentation: subject-based messaging, wildcards, slow consumers, and JetStream** — the clearest published model of topics-as-hierarchy, and worth reading even if you deploy Redis, because it shapes how you name channels.
- **RabbitMQ documentation: queue types, flow control, and memory alarms** — specifically the description of publisher blocking, which is the fourth opening incident explained by its designers.
- **Apache Kafka documentation on consumer groups**, plus KIP-429 (incremental cooperative rebalancing) and KIP-345 (static membership) — the machinery that determines how badly a deploy disrupts delivery.
- **_Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, Martin Kleppmann, ch. 11** — the log-versus-message-broker distinction, and the intellectual backbone of this article's central recommendation.
- **PostgreSQL `LISTEN`/`NOTIFY` documentation** — payload limits and delivery semantics, for the no-new-infrastructure option.
- **Centrifugo's "Engines and scalability" documentation** — this exact decision, made and documented as a product.

A Java sibling would be worth writing specifically about Kafka as a fan-out substrate, because the JVM client is where the relevant knobs live and are documented: consumer-group-per-pod and the garbage it leaves, static membership, cooperative rebalancing, and what a rebalance actually does to delivery latency during a rolling deploy. That's a genuinely different article from this one, and it would settle an argument that recurs in every organization already running Kafka. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: before choosing any piece of infrastructure that sits between two processes, write down two answers — what happens to a message when the consumer is slow, and what happens when the consumer is absent. Those two sentences predict more production incidents than throughput benchmarks ever will, and for most of the systems in this article they're one paragraph in the documentation that nobody read.
