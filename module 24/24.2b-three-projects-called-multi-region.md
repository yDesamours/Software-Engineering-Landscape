# Three Different Projects Called "Multi-Region": Failover, Latency, and Residency

_Module 24.2b — Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity_

## The Problem

Suppose you run an e-signature service. Thirty thousand companies send contracts through it; a signed document has legal weight, and the audit trail around it is the product. Everything runs in one cloud region, `us-east-1`, with the multi-AZ setup from 24.2a: a primary, a standby in another zone, quorum failover, tested switchovers.

Two pressures arrive in the same quarter. A large enterprise prospect's security questionnaire asks, as a yes/no, whether the service survives a regional failure. And a German customer's legal team says their contracts may not leave the EU.

So the team builds a second region: `eu-west-1`, with a scaled-down copy of the stack, a cross-region read replica, and a DNS failover record. It takes a quarter. Both boxes get ticked.

Eight months later, `us-east-1` has a real event. Not a clean outage — the API for launching instances is failing, one storage service is returning errors for about 30% of requests, and the provider's status page says "increased error rates." Your service is up but broken: a third of signature requests fail.

The failover doesn't go well.

- **Nothing triggers.** Health checks pass, because the service is up. The failover is a human decision, and nobody is sure whether to make it: the provider has not given an ETA, and the impact is partial.
- **The decision takes 35 minutes**, because failing over is a one-way door. Replication is asynchronous and about 8 seconds behind; promoting `eu-west-1` means telling an unknown number of customers that a document they signed was not, in fact, signed.
- **You can't scale up the standby.** The standby runs at 20% capacity, and scaling it needs new instances — which need a container image, which is in a registry hosted in `us-east-1`. The secrets manager is there too. So is the Terraform state, and so is the CI system that would deploy anything.
- **Traffic moves slowly.** DNS failover works, mostly. Forty minutes later, roughly 15% of traffic is still arriving at the degraded region, mainly from corporate resolvers and a mobile app that caches a resolved address for the life of the process.
- **You are now in the EU with US customers' contracts.** The residency arrangement is one-directional by design — EU data stays in the EU — and the failover just replicated everything the other way. Legal finds out on Thursday.
- **Failback takes three weeks.** The regional event ends after 70 minutes. The two databases have diverged, so going home requires a resync, a maintenance window, and a plan nobody wrote. Meanwhile you're running in a region sized for a fraction of the load, at full cost, with no DR.

Total: 95 minutes of user-visible impact and 20 minutes of lost signatures, in response to an event that lasted 70 minutes and degraded — not destroyed — the primary region. Doing nothing would have been cheaper.

The mistake wasn't the execution. It was earlier: the team built one thing to solve two unrelated problems, and never wrote down what "multi-region" was supposed to mean.

## What It Is

"Multi-region" names at least three separate projects. They have different architectures, different costs, and different success criteria, and building one when you needed another is the most expensive error in this area.

**Surviving a regional failure.** A disaster-recovery problem. The answer is an ordered set of copies in another region and a rehearsed procedure. Traffic normally goes to one place. The hard parts are the failover *decision*, control-plane independence, and failback.

**Serving distant users faster.** A latency problem. The answer is reads close to the user — CDN, edge caching, read replicas — while writes still travel home. It involves a second region but has nothing to do with failover, and it doesn't make you more available.

**Keeping data inside a jurisdiction.** A residency problem. The answer is to *partition*: EU customers' data lives in an EU region and does not leave it. This is the opposite of replicating everywhere, and it constrains your DR options rather than expanding them.

The failure above came from using one architecture — a replicated standby — for the first and third at once, which put US data in the EU during a failover and EU obligations in the middle of an incident.

The DR problem itself has a standard ladder, worth knowing because it turns "do we go multi-region?" into "which rung?":

```
strategy              RPO         RTO          idle cost
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
backup & restore      hours       hours        ~1%
pilot light           seconds     ~1 hour      10–20%
warm standby          seconds     10–30 min    30–50%
active-active         ~0          near zero    100%+
```

Most teams who say they want the bottom row need the second, and almost everyone should have the first regardless.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A questionnaire, contract or executive asked for "multi-region" and nobody wrote down which of the three problems it solves.
- Your stated RTO for a regional failure is "we'd figure it out."
- Your container registry, secrets, IaC state, CI runners or observability live in exactly one region — usually the one you'd be failing away from.
- You've never tried to launch your full production capacity in the secondary region.
- You have a secondary region and no failback plan.
- Users on another continent see slow page loads, and someone proposed failover infrastructure as the fix.
- You have a residency commitment and your backups are in another jurisdiction.
- Cross-region data transfer is a growing line on the bill that nobody can attribute.
- You accept writes in two regions and have no written conflict-resolution rule — which means you have one, and it's last-write-wins.
- Your last three outages were caused by your own deploys, and your resilience roadmap is entirely about the cloud provider.

## How It Works

### The latency wall

Light in fibre travels about 200,000 km/s, and real network paths are 1.5–2× longer than the straight line. That gives a floor nothing can move:

```
same host            0.05 ms  ┐
same availability zone  0.3 ms  ├ synchronous replication is comfortable here
cross-AZ, same region   ~1 ms  ┘
──────────────────────────────── the wall ────────────────────────────────
us-east ↔ us-west      ~60 ms  ┐
us-east ↔ eu-west      ~70 ms  ├ synchronous replication is a product decision
us-east ↔ ap-southeast ~200 ms ┘
```

A synchronous commit within a region costs a millisecond. A synchronous commit across the Atlantic costs seventy, on every write, forever. A request that makes five sequential cross-region calls costs a third of a second before your code does anything.

Every remaining design decision in this article follows from that one fact. **You cannot have cross-region synchronous replication and fast writes.** You choose which to give up, per class of data, and everything else is consequence.

### Active-active means one of three things

Since you can't synchronously replicate across the wall, "both regions accept writes" has to resolve into one of these:

**Partition writes by home region.** Every record — usually every tenant — has a home. Writes are routed there; the other region holds a replica for reads and for DR. No conflicts exist, because no row is ever written in two places. This is the version that actually works at scale, and it's cells (23.6) with geography attached. It also happens to be what residency requires, which is why these two projects can share an architecture when the DR one can't.

**One writer, many readers.** All writes go to one region; reads are served locally everywhere. Solves latency for read-heavy products, doesn't touch write latency, and leaves you with a single region to lose. Simple, common, often enough.

**Multi-writer with conflict resolution.** Two regions accept writes to the same data, and something reconciles. The options are all uncomfortable:

- *Last-write-wins* silently discards one of two real edits, and clock skew decides which. It's the default in several systems and it's a data-loss policy wearing a configuration setting's clothes.
- *CRDTs* merge correctly by construction, for the data types they cover — counters, sets, collaborative text. They can't express "this invoice number is unique" or "this seat is reserved once."
- *Globally consistent databases* (Spanner, CockroachDB, YugabyteDB) do it properly, with consensus, and hand you the latency bill at commit time for any transaction that spans regions. They also let you pin data to a region per row, which is the first option again, implemented for you.

The general rule: **invariants are what break.** Anything enforced by a uniqueness constraint, a sequence, a balance check, or an ordering — signature order, invoice numbering, seat inventory — cannot survive two independent writers without consensus. Data that's append-only, user-scoped, or naturally commutative survives fine.

### Regional failover is a bet, not a procedure

Within a region, failover is mechanical: a replica is seconds behind, promotion takes a minute, and you can automate it (24.2a). Across regions, three things change.

**The evidence is worse.** Regional events are usually partial. "One service degraded in one region" is far more common than "the region is gone," and it's much harder to decide about.

**The action is expensive and asymmetric.** Failing over costs minutes of async replication lag as real lost writes, plus a failback that is harder than the failover — the regions have diverged, and returning means a resync and a maintenance window.

**You're betting on the remaining duration of an outage you can't see into.** Fail over, and if the provider recovers in ten minutes you've paid the whole cost for nothing. Stay, and if it lasts six hours you've spent six hours down with a standby sitting idle.

This is why regional failover should almost never be automatic, and why the useful artifact is not a script but a **decision rule** written in advance, when nobody is panicking:

> If impact is total, or above 50% of requests, *and* the provider has not published an ETA within 20 minutes, the incident commander evacuates. Below that threshold we stay and degrade.

Any threshold beats deciding from scratch at 03:00. The thresholds also tell you what to build: an evacuation you're willing to trigger in 20 minutes must not depend on anything in the failing region.

### What has to already exist in the other region

The scenario's most avoidable failure was needing the dying region in order to leave it. Before an evacuation is possible, the target region needs, independently:

- machine images and a replicated container registry
- secrets, with their own regional replica or vault cluster
- infrastructure definitions and their state, stored somewhere neutral
- a CI/deploy path that doesn't route through the failed region
- observability and on-call tooling — you cannot fly on instruments hosted in the region you're evacuating
- **capacity**, in the form of running instances or reservations, because a regional event sends every other customer to the same neighbouring region, and "insufficient capacity" is a real error at exactly the wrong moment

This is 24.2a's static stability at continental scale: the plan must not require creating anything new while the world is on fire. A warm standby that needs to scale up during the incident is really a pilot light with optimistic accounting.

### Moving the traffic

Three mechanisms, with different tails:

**DNS with health checks** is universal and slow to converge. TTLs are honoured inconsistently by resolvers, and some clients cache for the life of the process. Expect a long tail of 10–20% for tens of minutes — plan for the degraded region to keep receiving traffic, and make sure what it does with that traffic is safe (returning errors is safer than accepting writes that will be lost).

**A global load balancer or anycast front door** moves traffic in seconds. Its cost is conceptual: it's a *global* component, so it's a shared dependency across all your regions. Several of the industry's largest multi-region outages have been failures of the global layer that was supposed to route around failures.

**Client-side failover** — an app that knows both endpoints and switches itself — has the best convergence and the worst change latency: fixing a bug in it requires a release. Good for first-party mobile and desktop clients, unavailable for browsers.

Whatever you use, the cut breaks anything sticky: sessions, WebSocket connections, in-progress uploads. And the target's caches are cold, so the first minutes after arrival are the slowest (24.2a).

### Residency partitions; it doesn't replicate

If EU customer data must stay in the EU, the architecture is one deployment per jurisdiction, each with its own database, its own object storage, its own backups, and its own search index. What crosses the boundary is: as little as possible, and deliberately.

Three consequences people meet late:

- **A shared global layer is a residency problem.** The routing table that maps a customer to their region, the identity service, the billing system, the analytics warehouse, the error tracker that captures request payloads — each holds personal data and each needs an answer.
- **Backups inherit the boundary.** Cross-region backup copies are exactly the thing residency forbids, so DR for an EU region means another EU region, with a smaller set of options and its own cost.
- **Internal tools have to become region-aware.** Support consoles, admin dashboards, the data team's queries. This is usually the largest hidden cost, and it's a permanent tax on every internal tool you build afterwards.

The mechanism is Module 5's tenancy work with a jurisdiction attached, and the legal framing is Module 34's. What's structural is the direction: residency pushes you toward *more independent* regions, while DR failover pushes you toward *more coupled* ones. Recognising that they pull in opposite directions is most of the value of separating the two projects.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Cross-region backup copies: everyone.** This is the bottom rung, it's cheap, and it's the difference between "a very bad week" and "no company." Do this before anything else here.
- **Pilot light or warm standby when a multi-hour regional outage would be existential** — when your customers' operations stop with yours. A payments processor or a hospital system is a different calculation from a project-management tool whose users will read email for four hours.
- **Residency partitioning when a contract or a regulator requires it.** Not optional, and worth doing as a partition rather than bolting a jurisdiction onto a replication topology.
- **Read replicas and edge caching when a real share of users are 100+ ms away** and the product is interactive. This is the latency project; it pays for itself in conversion metrics and has nothing to do with the other two.
- **Active-active only when writes partition naturally** by tenant or geography — which, for B2B SaaS, they very often do, since a company's users are mostly in one place.

An expected-value sanity check, done honestly, settles most of these arguments. If a four-hour regional outage costs €400,000 and severe events in your region happen once every three years, that's about €130,000 a year of expected loss against the annual cost of the standby and the engineering time to keep it trustworthy. Run the number before the quarter, not after.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Before multi-AZ is genuinely solid.** Most "region outages" people fear are zone events, and zone redundancy is an order of magnitude cheaper. Finish 24.2a first.
- **When your own deploys cause more downtime than your provider does.** Look at last year's incidents and attribute them. For most teams the top causes are deploys, migrations and config changes, and a second region addresses none of them — while adding a second place for each to go wrong.
- **Active-active for data with real invariants.** If two regions can both allocate an invoice number, you don't have high availability, you have a reconciliation backlog.
- **Multi-region for latency when a CDN would do.** Cache the static and the cacheable first; it's a week of work instead of a quarter.
- **Multi-cloud for resilience.** Every abstraction you build to stay portable is one you maintain forever, and the correlated failure you're insuring against — an entire provider globally — is rarer than the outages your added complexity will cause. Multi-cloud for procurement leverage or a specific service is a different, legitimate argument.

## Trade-offs

- **Everything happens twice.** Deploys, migrations, secret rotations, certificate renewals, dependency upgrades, audits, capacity planning, incident response. This is the cost that grows with time rather than with traffic, and it's the one that's routinely underestimated.
- **Schema migrations get much harder.** A migration must be compatible with the other region's version for as long as replication takes to catch up, and with whatever the standby is running. 24.1b's cross-store consistency problem, with 70 ms and a jurisdiction between the stores.
- **A standby that takes no traffic rots.** Configuration drift, expired credentials, an unapplied upgrade, a full disk. The only real fix is to send it real traffic regularly, which means scheduled evacuation exercises — expensive, and frightening the first few times, which is exactly why they're worth doing while everything is fine.
- **Cost.** Duplicated infrastructure, plus cross-region transfer at roughly two cents per gigabyte between US regions and more across continents, plus replicated object storage for large files, plus the engineering time that isn't building product.
- **Any global component becomes a correlated failure across every region.** The router, the identity service, the front door, the feature-flag service. Multi-region systems most often go globally down through the layer that was meant to make them regional.
- **Having a standby creates pressure to use it**, sometimes when staying put and degrading would have been better.
- **Failback is harder than failover** and is almost always the part nobody wrote down.
- **Residency and DR constrain each other**, and their intersection is often a small, expensive set of options.

## Alternatives

- **Cross-region backups plus a rehearsed rebuild.** RTO measured in hours, cost near zero, and it covers the genuinely catastrophic case. For a large share of products this is the right answer, and saying so plainly in a security questionnaire — with an RTO number and evidence of a drill — is more credible than a standby nobody has tested.
- **Read-only mode served from somewhere else.** A snapshot of customers' critical data, published to object storage behind a CDN, so that during a regional outage people can still *see* their contracts, invoices or schedules even if they can't change them. Days of work, not a quarter, and it converts a total outage into a serious inconvenience for many products.
- **Cells inside one region** (23.6). Reduces the blast radius of the failures you actually have — bad deploys, poison data, noisy tenants — which is where most of your downtime comes from.
- **A globally distributed database.** Spanner, CockroachDB, DynamoDB global tables and their peers absorb replication, failover and per-row placement. You pay in price, lock-in and commit latency for cross-region transactions, and you get back the hardest part of this article.
- **Queue and forward at the client.** If the client can hold a write and submit it later, a regional outage becomes delayed processing. Where the product allows it, this is the cheapest availability you will ever buy.
- **Accept the outage, and communicate well.** A status page hosted outside your infrastructure, an honest ETA, and proactive customer contact. Not an architecture, but frequently what determines whether an outage costs you customers (24.3).

## How to Achieve It

**1. Write down which of the three problems you're solving, as a number or a jurisdiction.** "RTO 1 hour, RPO 5 minutes for a regional failure" or "German customer data remains in the EU." Never "multi-region."
*Move on when* the requirement could be checked by someone who wasn't in the meeting.

**2. Attribute last year's downtime.** Provider, your deploys, your migrations, your dependencies.
*Move on when* you know whether a second region addresses your top cause. If it doesn't, fix that first and come back.

**3. Get backups into a second region and restore from them there, once, for real** (24.1a, 24.1b).
*Move on when* you have a measured RTO for a full rebuild.

**4. Make the second region buildable without the first.** Images, registry, secrets, IaC state, CI, observability. Prove it by building the environment from an empty account.
*Move on when* nothing in the build path resolves to the primary region.

**5. Add continuous replication, and measure lag and cost at peak.**
*Move on when* you can state what an evacuation would lose, in transactions.

**6. Write the decision rule and the failback plan, together.** Who calls it, on what evidence, at what threshold, and how you come home.
*Move on when* someone who wasn't there could execute both.

**7. Run an evacuation with real traffic, scheduled, during business hours.** Then run it again next quarter.
*Move on when* it's boring — which is the same bar as 24.2a's switchover, for the same reason.

**8. Only now consider active-active, and only if writes partition by tenant or geography.** If they don't, stop; the answer is a globally consistent database or a different design.

## Implementation

The code is Go, because the interesting piece is the routing layer that sits in front of everything and decides where a request is allowed to be served — the one component that both the residency project and the active-active project need, and the one that has to keep working when the other region doesn't.

### Home-region routing

```go
// Every tenant has a home region. Writes happen there, or they don't happen.
// This is the property that makes active-active safe and residency enforceable,
// and it holds only if there is exactly one place that decides it.
func (r *Router) ServeHTTP(w http.ResponseWriter, req *http.Request) {
    tenant := tenantFrom(req)

    home, err := r.placement.Home(req.Context(), tenant) // local cache, see below
    if err != nil {
        http.Error(w, "placement unavailable", http.StatusServiceUnavailable)
        return
    }

    if home == r.thisRegion {
        r.next.ServeHTTP(w, req)
        return
    }

    if isRead(req) && r.hasLocalReplica(tenant) && freshEnough(req) {
        r.next.ServeHTTP(w, req) // local replica read; staleness rules from 23.2a
        return
    }

    // Everything else goes home. A 307 preserves method and body and lets the
    // client talk to the home region directly, rather than paying 70 ms twice
    // by proxying through here.
    http.Redirect(w, req, r.endpointFor(home)+req.URL.Path, http.StatusTemporaryRedirect)
}
```

> **The tempting shortcut that ruins everything.** When the home region is unreachable, the obvious move is to accept the write locally "just this once" and reconcile later. That single branch converts a partitioned design into a multi-writer one, with no conflict resolution and — if the boundary was jurisdictional — a compliance incident. The correct behaviour is to fail the write, or to serve read-only, until an operator changes the tenant's home deliberately. Evacuation is an explicit act, not a fallback.

### Mode, cached locally

```go
// The region's operating mode is global state, so it must survive the global
// service that holds it. Read it centrally, cache it on disk, and never let a
// config-service outage change how this region behaves.
type Mode string

const (
    ModeActive   Mode = "active"    // serving reads and writes
    ModeReadOnly Mode = "read_only" // home region degraded; writes refused, data visible
    ModeDrained  Mode = "drained"   // evacuated; reject and let the client go elsewhere
)

func (m *ModeWatcher) Current() Mode {
    if v, ok := m.fromControlPlane(); ok {
        m.persist(v)          // write-through, so a restart mid-incident is safe
        return v
    }
    if v, ok := m.fromDisk(); ok {
        return v              // last known good: keep doing what we were doing
    }
    return ModeActive         // never fail closed on a config read
}
```

> **Why the last line is `ModeActive` and not `ModeDrained`.** A config service that becomes unreachable is a far more common event than a regional evacuation. If an unreadable config drained the region, the config service would become a single point of failure for every region simultaneously — the exact correlated-global-component failure described above. Defaulting to "keep serving" means a failure of the control plane leaves the data plane exactly as it was.

## Further Reading

- **AWS, "Disaster Recovery of Workloads on AWS: Recovery in the Cloud"** (whitepaper). The backup-and-restore / pilot-light / warm-standby / active-active ladder, with the cost and RTO characteristics of each. Google Cloud and Azure publish equivalent DR planning guides; read whichever matches your provider.
- **Martin Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, chapter 5**, on multi-leader replication, write conflicts, and the honest limits of last-write-wins and CRDTs.
- **"Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database"** (OSDI 2012), and the **CockroachDB multi-region documentation**, particularly `REGIONAL BY ROW` tables. The paper explains the cost of global consistency; the docs show the partition-by-home-region pattern as a product feature.
- **Roblox's 2021 outage postmortem** (73 hours, one region, an internal service). The best available argument that your resilience budget probably belongs inside your own systems rather than across cloud regions.
- **Your provider's traffic-steering documentation** — Route 53 health checks and failover records, Cloudflare Load Balancing, Google Cloud's global load balancer — read specifically for convergence behaviour and what happens when the steering layer itself is impaired.
- **EDPB Recommendations 01/2020 on supplementary measures for international data transfers**, for why "we'll just fail over to another region" is a legal event and not only a technical one. Module 34 covers the framework.
- **In this collection:** 24.1a and 24.1b (the backups and the state inventory this all rests on), 24.2a (intra-region failover, static stability, idempotent writes), 23.6 (cells, and the argument for blast radius over geography), 23.2a (replica reads and staleness rules), 13.1 (CDN and edge caching for the latency project), Module 5 (tenancy, which decides whether writes partition), 24.3 (the evacuation decision and who owns it), 24.4 (residency, retention and deletion across jurisdictions), Module 34 (the legal frame).

A **TypeScript** sibling would be worth writing on doing this routing at the edge instead of at the origin — an edge function that resolves a tenant's home region before the request reaches any region at all. It would show what the Go version hides by running inside a healthy region: no warm local cache to fall back on, a few milliseconds of CPU budget per request, a placement lookup that must survive cold starts, and cookie-based pinning so a signing session doesn't bounce between regions mid-flow. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: **name the failure you're buying insurance against, then check it's the one that keeps happening to you.** Most teams' outages are self-inflicted and regional; the map should be drawn from last year's incidents, not from the shape of the cloud provider's brochure.
