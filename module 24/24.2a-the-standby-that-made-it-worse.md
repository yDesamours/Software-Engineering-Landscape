# The Standby That Made It Worse: Redundancy, Failover, and Split Brain

_Module 24.2a — Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity_

## The Problem

Suppose you run the backend for card payments in 4,000 coffee shops. Terminals call your API to authorize a card, you write the authorization to Postgres, and you tell the terminal yes or no. At peak you handle about 300 writes a second, and every one of them is a real transaction with real money behind it.

The setup looks responsible. One primary in availability zone A, one hot standby in zone B, streaming replication, and a failover manager that promotes the standby automatically if the primary stops answering. A DNS record, `db.internal`, points at whichever one is primary, with a 60-second TTL.

At 08:14 on a Saturday — peak — the primary's host loses network connectivity for 25 seconds. Nothing is wrong with the machine or the data. The failover manager notices after 12 seconds, decides the primary is dead, and promotes the standby. Here is what the 25-second blip actually cost:

**412 authorizations vanish.** Replication was asynchronous and running about 3.2 seconds behind. Those transactions were committed on the old primary and confirmed to the terminals, which printed receipts and released customers. The new primary has never heard of them. The card networks have. You now owe 412 reconciliations, and some of those customers will be charged twice when the shops re-run them.

**Both databases accept writes for 40 seconds.** The old primary's network comes back at 08:14:25. It has not been told anything. Application servers that still hold TCP connections to it keep writing, happily, to a database that is no longer the primary. Two divergent timelines exist at once, and merging them later is manual work.

**Most application servers never move.** The failover changed a DNS record, but the JVM caches DNS lookups indefinitely by default, and the connection pools hold open sockets that don't care about DNS at all. Roughly two-thirds of the fleet keeps talking to the old primary until someone restarts it.

**Then the new primary falls over.** Once traffic does arrive, 900 application instances reconnect within the same two seconds and exhaust `max_connections`. The standby's page cache is empty, so queries that took 4 ms take 60 ms, the pool backs up, and requests time out. The system is "failed over" at 08:14:22 and actually serving traffic normally at 08:25.

Total downtime: 11 minutes, plus a week of reconciliation. The event that triggered it was a 25-second network blip that would have resolved itself.

```
        blip starts            blip ends
08:14:00 ──────────── 08:14:25 ─────────────────────── 08:25
   │         │             │        │                     │
   │      promote       old primary │                     └─ normal
   │      standby       still writing                     
   └─ 3.2 s of committed writes lost forever
```

This is not an argument against redundancy. It's the observation that a spare copy is not availability. What turns a spare into availability is a takeover procedure — and that procedure has failure modes of its own, several of which are worse than the failure it was built for.

## What It Is

**Redundancy** is having more than one of something so that losing one doesn't lose the capability. **Failover** is the act of moving a role — usually the write role — from one instance to another, and it's the part that's hard.

Three distinctions do most of the work:

**Stateless failover is a solved problem; stateful failover is this article.** If an application instance dies, the load balancer stops sending it traffic and nobody notices (23.1b). There's no role to transfer, because every instance was interchangeable. A database primary is not interchangeable with its replicas: exactly one node may accept writes, and deciding which one is a genuinely hard distributed-systems problem.

**Failover is unplanned; switchover is planned.** Same mechanism, wildly different risk. Switchover lets you stop writes, wait for the replica to catch up, and promote with zero data loss, at a time you chose. Most of the value of a failover setup comes from doing switchovers regularly — that's what proves the mechanism works and keeps it from rotting.

**Availability layers don't substitute for each other.** A standby protects against a machine failing. It does nothing about someone deleting the wrong rows, which is 24.1a's territory. The two get budgeted together and confused constantly.

## Recognizing the Problem

- You have a standby, and nobody can say when it was last promoted — or it's never been promoted.
- You can't state what happens to a transaction that was in flight when the primary died.
- Your replication is asynchronous and you've never measured the lag at peak, so you don't know how much a failover would lose.
- Failover is wired to DNS, and you haven't checked what your runtime and connection pools do with DNS.
- Nothing prevents a recovered old primary from accepting writes.
- The standby is in the same availability zone, or was built from the same image on the same day, or shares a certificate with the primary.
- Your health check tests that a port is open, not that the database can serve a query.
- After a failover, performance is much worse for a while and nobody has quantified "a while."
- Retrying a failed write could charge a customer twice.
- Your failover machinery (etcd, a consensus layer, a controller) can itself lose quorum and take down a database that is perfectly healthy.

## How It Works

### Redundancy only spans the failure domain it spans

Failures nest, and each layer of redundancy protects against exactly one boundary:

```
process  →  instance  →  rack  →  availability zone  →  region  →  provider/account
   │            │          │             │                 │              │
 restart     spare      spare        zone-spanning      24.2b        rarely, and
             instance   rack         replication                     mostly at the
                                                                     backup layer
```

Two instances in one zone survive a host failure and not a zone failure. Two zones survive a zone failure and not a regional one. This is mechanical and easy to reason about. The failures that surprise people are the **correlated** ones, where the redundancy exists but shares a cause:

- Both nodes run the same image with the same bug.
- Both nodes' TLS certificates expire at the same minute.
- A bad configuration push reaches both within seconds.
- Both are in the same account, and the credential that got compromised controls both.
- The standby is a replica, so it faithfully applies the poison query that killed the primary.

"How many copies?" is the easy question. "What do these copies share?" is the one that predicts your next incident.

### Detection is the half that goes wrong

The system must decide that a node is dead. It cannot. It can only observe that it hasn't heard from the node, and "dead" and "unreachable" look identical from the outside. Every failover system is therefore tuned between two bad outcomes:

- **Aggressive detection** (a few seconds) fails over during transient blips, GC pauses and brief network events — exactly the 08:14 story.
- **Conservative detection** (30–60 seconds) means every genuine failure costs that long in downtime before anything starts.

Two things improve the trade materially. First, **multiple independent observers**: a single monitor's view of the network is worthless, whereas "four of seven observers in different zones can't reach it" is real evidence. Second, **health checks that test the actual capability** — can this node execute a query and write? — rather than whether a TCP port accepts connections. A port that's open on a database whose disk is full is the worst of both worlds: it fails your users and passes your check.

Deep health checks bring their own hazard, worth knowing before you enable one. A check that fails when a *dependency* is slow will mark every node unhealthy at once, because they all share the dependency, and you'll fail over a fleet that had nothing wrong with it. Liveness checks should test the node; dependency problems belong in alerting, not in the mechanism that kills things.

### Split brain, and why fencing is the only real answer

Two nodes accepting writes at once is the failure that makes everything else recoverable-by-comparison. Neither timeline is wrong; both contain real customer transactions; merging them is a manual, judgment-heavy job.

Preventing it needs something stronger than "the new primary believes it's the primary." Three mechanisms, in increasing order of what they actually guarantee:

**Consensus on the role.** A majority of nodes in a coordination layer (etcd, ZooKeeper, Consul — or Raft embedded in the failover tool) agrees on who holds the primary lease. A node that can't reach the majority knows it might not be primary and must stop. This is why Patroni, and most managed HA, has a quorum component, and also why the coordination layer losing quorum can demote a perfectly healthy database.

**Leases with expiry.** The primary holds a lease it must renew. If it can't renew, it demotes itself before the lease expires, so a partitioned old primary stops on its own. This relies on clocks and on the demotion path actually running — a node frozen in a GC pause or paging out doesn't demote itself, and wakes up still believing it's primary.

**Fencing at the resource.** The only guarantee that doesn't depend on the failed node cooperating: make it *impossible* for it to be written to. Power it off (STONITH), revoke its network or storage access, or attach a monotonically increasing epoch number to every write so the storage layer rejects anything carrying an old one. Fencing tokens are the software version of the same idea, and they work precisely because the check happens where the data is, not where the confusion is.

The practical lesson: a failover system without fencing is one that hopes the old primary is well-behaved. Sometimes it is.

### Replication mode sets your RPO at failover

This is the one dial where the trade-off is explicit and quantifiable, so it deserves a deliberate answer rather than a default.

**Asynchronous.** The primary commits and tells the client yes without waiting for anyone. Fastest possible writes, and at failover you lose everything that hadn't shipped — 3.2 seconds of lag at 300 writes/sec is roughly a thousand transactions. Note what that means: you *confirmed* those writes to the client. This isn't lost buffered data, it's broken promises.

**Synchronous.** The primary waits for a replica to acknowledge before confirming the commit. Zero loss at failover. Two costs, and the second one catches people out:

- Every write pays a network round trip. Same-zone, that's well under a millisecond; cross-zone, a couple; cross-region, 10–80 ms *per commit*.
- With exactly one synchronous replica, losing that replica makes the primary stop accepting writes, because it can't get its acknowledgement. Naively configured, adding a synchronous standby *reduces* availability. The fix is quorum-style configuration — require an ack from any one of two or three replicas — so no single replica is load-bearing.

**In between.** Postgres exposes the gradient directly through `synchronous_commit`: `off`, `local`, `remote_write` (the replica received it), `on` (the replica flushed it to disk), `remote_apply` (the replica has applied it and will serve it to readers). MySQL's semi-synchronous replication occupies similar ground. The useful realization is that this is a *per-transaction* setting in Postgres, not a global one: a payment authorization can be synchronous while the telemetry ping in the same service is asynchronous. Most systems don't need one answer for all their writes.

Synchronous replication doesn't remove risk. It converts a data-loss risk into a latency-and-availability cost, which is usually the better currency for money-handling systems and a bad deal for high-volume telemetry.

### The failover isn't over when the database says it is

The database finished at 08:14:22. The system was healthy at 08:25. That gap is where most of the real downtime lives, and it's all on the client side.

**In-flight transactions end ambiguously.** A `COMMIT` that times out has an unknown outcome — it may have been applied and lost, applied and kept, or never applied. Retrying is the only option and it's only safe if the write is idempotent. Idempotency keys (17.1, 10.2) aren't a nicety here; they're what makes a failover a latency spike instead of a double charge.

**Connection pools don't notice.** A pool holding 20 open sockets to the old primary will keep using them. What moves a fleet is: a proxy or virtual IP that fails over with the role, or a client that knows all the hosts and asks which one is the primary. DNS is the weakest of the three — TTLs are honoured inconsistently, and the JVM's default caching of successful lookups is *forever* unless `networkaddress.cache.ttl` is set.

**Everyone reconnects at once.** 900 instances re-establishing pools simultaneously is a self-inflicted connection storm against a database that's still warming up. Jittered backoff on reconnect, and a connection limit that accounts for the whole fleet (23.2a), are what stop the recovery from becoming the second outage.

**The new primary is cold.** An empty page cache after promotion means 5–20× slower queries until it fills. For a database of any size, this is minutes, and it's often the largest single contributor to user-visible impact. A standby that serves some read traffic stays warm — at the price of coupling your read capacity to your failover capacity, since after promotion one node is doing both jobs.

A realistic budget looks like this, and the shape of it is the point:

```
detect   elect   promote   clients notice   cache warm
  12s  │   3s  │    8s   │      45s       │   ~9 min
  └──────── 23s ─────────┘└──────── the part you forgot ────────┘
```

### Static stability

A related habit worth naming, from AWS's operational writing: the system should survive a failure **without needing to do anything**. Capacity is pre-provisioned so no scaling is required mid-incident; the data plane keeps serving even if the control plane that manages it is broken; recovery doesn't depend on a resource in the failed zone (an image registry, a config service, a secret store).

The test question: *if the thing that performs failovers is itself down, what happens?* If the answer is "everything stops," the failover machinery has become a single point of failure protecting you from single points of failure.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A standby in a second zone: nearly every production database.** The cost is one instance plus cross-zone traffic; the alternative is that a single host's failure is a full outage of unknown length.
- **Automatic failover when downtime is expensive per minute, detection is quorum-based, fencing exists, and writes are idempotent.** For 4,000 shops mid-Saturday, minutes matter and automation earns its risk — *given* those four conditions. Missing any of them, automation is how you turn a blip into an incident.
- **Manual failover when failures are rare, downtime is tolerable for ten minutes, or the data-loss decision needs a human.** Paging a human who runs a tested script is a perfectly respectable HA strategy, and it's strictly safer against false positives.
- **Synchronous replication for writes whose loss you'd have to explain to a customer or a regulator** — payments, ledgers, orders, anything confirmed to a person. Use the per-transaction setting rather than blanketing the database.
- **N+2 rather than N+1 when a node can be down for a planned reason** (a deploy, a version upgrade) while another fails. With N+1, every maintenance window is a period with no redundancy.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Automatic failover on async replication for a system where ten minutes of downtime is survivable.** You're trading a rare, cheap outage for a rare, expensive data-loss event, in the wrong direction.
- **Building failover machinery for a stateless tier.** That's load balancing plus health checks; it's already done (23.1b). The role-transfer problem only exists where there's a role.
- **Cross-region synchronous replication for latency-sensitive writes.** 40 ms added to every commit is a product decision, not an infrastructure one, and it's usually the wrong one (24.2b).
- **Writing your own failover controller.** Patroni, repmgr, and every managed database's built-in HA have absorbed years of edge cases in exactly the areas — fencing, quorum, clock skew — where a homegrown version will be subtly wrong. This is close to the strongest "don't build it" in the module.
- **Redundancy that shares fate.** A second node from the same image, in the same zone, on the same certificate is a bill, not a safety measure.
- **Chasing more nines than your dependencies have.** If your payment processor is down 20 minutes a month, a five-nines database changes nothing your customers can perceive.

## Trade-offs

- **You pay for a machine that mostly does nothing.** Serving reads from it recovers some of that cost and couples read capacity to failover capacity: after promotion, one node does both jobs, at the exact moment it's coldest.
- **The failover system is itself a distributed system that can fail.** Quorum loss in the coordination layer can demote or freeze a healthy database. You've added a dependency in exchange for removing one.
- **False failovers.** Every reduction in detection time increases them. There's no setting that avoids both, only a choice about which error you'd rather have.
- **Synchronous replication costs latency on every single write**, forever, to protect against an event that happens twice a year.
- **Split-brain risk never reaches zero.** Fencing has its own failure modes — a STONITH path that can't reach the node it's meant to kill, an epoch check that some code path skips.
- **The cold-cache cliff** is real, unavoidable without a warmed standby, and routinely the biggest part of the user-visible impact.
- **Standbys drift.** Less-exercised nodes accumulate differences: a disk filling at a different rate, an unapplied minor-version upgrade, a config change made on the primary by hand. A standby you never promote is a standby you can't be sure about.
- **Applications have to be written for it.** Retries, idempotency keys, connection recycling, tolerance for a read-only window. That's product engineering work, and no infrastructure choice removes it.

## Alternatives

- **Accept downtime and restore.** An RTO of 30 minutes via a tested restore (24.1a) is a legitimate availability strategy for plenty of systems, and it's dramatically simpler. The honest comparison is "30 minutes twice a year" against "a standby, a quorum layer, fencing, and their own failure modes."
- **Degrade to read-only instead of failing over.** If the primary is gone but a replica is fine, serving reads with writes disabled turns a total outage into a partial one. For most products this is a much better minute-by-minute experience, and it buys time to make the promotion decision deliberately rather than automatically. Underused, and cheap to build if the application has a global "writes disabled" mode.
- **Buffer writes at the edge.** Terminals that can queue an authorization and submit it later turn a backend outage into delayed settlement. This is how physical payment systems have always worked, and it moves the redundancy to where the failure is cheapest to absorb.
- **Client-side failover.** A multi-host connection string with "give me whichever host is the primary" removes DNS and virtual IPs from the path entirely. Simple, widely supported, and it fixes the most common client-side failure in this article.
- **Leaderless or multi-primary replication.** Dynamo-style quorum systems have no failover event because they have no leader; the cost is conflict resolution in your application and a different consistency model, which is a large commitment for a payments ledger.
- **Shrink the blast radius instead of eliminating failure.** Cells (23.6) accept that failures happen and make each one affect 1/N of customers. For large systems this is often a better investment than making any single failover more reliable.
- **Use the managed option.** Multi-AZ on a managed database gives you quorum, fencing and a stable endpoint, maintained by people who do nothing else. For most teams this is the correct answer and the rest of this article is for understanding what it's doing on your behalf.

## How to Achieve It

**1. Write down what each spare protects against, and what it shares with the thing it replaces.** Zone, image, certificate, account, deploy pipeline.
*Move on when* every redundancy has a named failure and no unlisted shared cause.

**2. Make writes retry-safe before touching any failover machinery.** Idempotency keys on anything that moves money or sends messages. This is the prerequisite: with it, a failover is a latency spike; without it, every failover is a data-integrity incident.
*Move on when* replaying a request cannot produce a second effect.

**3. Fix the client side, and test it by killing the primary in staging.** Connection validation and a bounded max lifetime in the pool, a DNS TTL your runtime actually respects, or a multi-host connection string. Add jittered reconnect backoff.
*Move on when* a killed primary in staging costs seconds and the fleet moves by itself.

**4. Measure replication lag at peak, and decide replication mode per class of write.**
*Move on when* you can state, in transactions, what an unplanned failover would lose.

**5. Do a planned switchover in production, during business hours, with everyone watching.** This is the step people skip, and it's the one that finds the drift, the stale config, and the surprise in the reconnect path.
*Move on when* a switchover is boring.

**6. Only then automate it, and only with quorum-based detection and a fencing mechanism you've tested by partitioning a node on purpose.**
*Move on when* an isolated old primary provably cannot accept a write.

**7. Build the read-only degraded mode** so that a promotion decision can be made calmly instead of automatically.
*Move on when* you can disable writes fleet-wide with one change.

**8. Make switchovers recurring** — monthly, or as part of every minor-version upgrade — and alert on "days since last failover" the way you'd alert on a stale backup (24.1a).
*Move on when* nobody has to remember to do it.

## Implementation

The code is Java, because the client side is where the surprises are, and JDBC exposes every one of them explicitly: DNS caching, pool lifetimes, host lists, per-transaction durability, and retry classification are all visible settings rather than framework defaults you can't see.

### Making the fleet move without DNS

```java
// The pgJDBC driver knows every host and asks which one accepts writes.
// No DNS record to update, no virtual IP, no TTL to be wrong about.
jdbc:postgresql://db-a.internal:5432,db-b.internal:5432/pos
    ?targetServerType=primary      // reject a host that's in recovery
    &loadBalanceHosts=false        // try in order; only one can be primary anyway
    &connectTimeout=3&socketTimeout=10
```

```java
HikariConfig cfg = new HikariConfig();
cfg.setJdbcUrl(URL);
cfg.setMaximumPoolSize(15);                 // × instances, budgeted against max_connections
cfg.setMaxLifetime(Duration.ofMinutes(10).toMillis());  // no connection outlives a failover for long
cfg.setKeepaliveTime(Duration.ofSeconds(30).toMillis()); // probe idle connections
cfg.setValidationTimeout(2_000);
cfg.setInitializationFailTimeout(-1);       // start up even if the DB is mid-failover
```

> **The one-line trap.** None of the above helps if the JVM never re-resolves a hostname. `java.security.Security.setProperty("networkaddress.cache.ttl", "30")` — or the equivalent in your container's security policy — is the difference between a fleet that moves in 45 seconds and one that needs a rolling restart. Check it; the default in many configurations is to cache successful lookups for the life of the process.

### A write that survives a promotion

```java
// Idempotency first: the retry below is only safe because this insert can't
// take effect twice. The key comes from the terminal, not from us.
private static final String SQL = """
    INSERT INTO authorizations (idempotency_key, shop_id, amount_cents, network_ref)
    VALUES (?, ?, ?, ?)
    ON CONFLICT (idempotency_key) DO NOTHING
    RETURNING id""";

Optional<Long> authorize(Auth auth) throws SQLException {
    return retryOnFailover(3, () -> {
        try (Connection c = pool.getConnection()) {
            c.setAutoCommit(false);
            // Money is worth a round trip; the telemetry writer in this same
            // service leaves this at the asynchronous default.
            try (Statement s = c.createStatement()) {
                s.execute("SET LOCAL synchronous_commit = 'remote_apply'");
            }
            try (PreparedStatement ps = c.prepareStatement(SQL)) {
                ps.setString(1, auth.idempotencyKey());
                /* … */
                ResultSet rs = ps.executeQuery();
                Long id = rs.next() ? rs.getLong(1) : lookupExisting(c, auth);
                c.commit();
                return Optional.of(id);
            }
        }
    });
}

// Retry only what a failover produces; never retry a constraint violation or a syntax error.
private static final Set<String> TRANSIENT = Set.of(
    "08000", "08003", "08006", "08001", "08004",  // connection exceptions
    "57P01", "57P02", "57P03",                    // admin shutdown, crash shutdown, cannot connect now
    "25006");                                     // read-only transaction: we hit a demoted node
```

> **The ambiguous commit.** If the connection dies *during* `commit()`, the transaction's fate is genuinely unknown — it may have been applied on the old primary and lost, or applied and replicated. The retry resolves this only because `ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING` plus the lookup makes both outcomes converge on the same answer. Without an idempotency key there is no safe behaviour here, only two ways to be wrong, which is why step 2 of the adoption path comes before any failover work.

> **`25006` is the interesting one.** It means you connected to a host that's now a replica — the signal that your view of who's primary is stale. Treat it as a reason to discard the connection and re-resolve, not as an application error.

## Further Reading

- **Martin Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_,** chapter 5 on leaders, followers and failover (it enumerates the same failure modes: lost async writes, split brain, and the impossibility of tuning the timeout correctly), and chapters 8–9 on fencing tokens and consensus.
- **GitHub's post-incident analysis of the October 21, 2018 outage.** A 43-second network partition triggered an automated cross-country database promotion; writes landed on both coasts, and restoring consistency degraded the service for over 24 hours. Every theme in this article appears in one incident.
- **Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout, "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm" (Raft).** Worth reading once for what your failover tool's coordination layer is actually doing, and why a majority is required.
- **David Yanacek, "Implementing health checks," AWS Builders' Library.** On deep versus shallow checks and how dependency-sensitive checks cause fleet-wide failures. Read it before enabling an aggressive one.
- **Becky Weiss and Mike Furr, "Static stability using Availability Zones," AWS Builders' Library.** Pre-provisioned capacity and keeping the data plane independent of the control plane.
- **PostgreSQL documentation:** `synchronous_commit`, `synchronous_standby_names` (including the quorum syntax), and the pgJDBC documentation on multi-host connection strings and `targetServerType`.
- **Patroni documentation**, particularly the replication-modes and fencing sections. Even if you use a managed service, it's the clearest description of what an HA controller must handle.
- **In this collection:** 23.1b (health checks and draining for the stateless tier), 23.2a (replication lag and connection budgets), 24.1a (why a standby is not a backup), 24.2b (when the failure domain is a whole region), 24.3 (who is allowed to decide, and the runbook), 17.1 and 10.2 (idempotency and retry semantics), Module 18 (limiting the reconnect storm), 36.3 (circuit breakers, so a failing database doesn't take the callers with it).

A **Go** sibling would be worth writing on the other side of this — the controller rather than the client. Leader election over etcd or Kubernetes leases is idiomatic Go, and the article could show what the Java view can't: why a lease must be renewed well before it expires (clock skew and pause budgets), why the controller must fence before it promotes rather than after, and what happens to a controller that loses quorum while its database is perfectly healthy. Not yet written.

The habit to take from this: **a spare is not a plan.** For every redundant thing, know what it shares with what it replaces, how the takeover is decided, what the decision costs in lost writes, and when you last did it on purpose.
