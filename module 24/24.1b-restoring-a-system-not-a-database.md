# Restoring a System, Not a Database: Files, Indexes, Queues and the Stores That Don't Share a Clock

_Module 24.1b — Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity_

## The Problem

Suppose you run an applicant-tracking system. Nine hundred companies use it to hire. The state lives in five places:

```
Postgres        420 GB   candidates, applications, interview notes
Object storage   14 TB   28M resumes, portfolios, signed offer letters
Elasticsearch    61M docs  candidate search
Redis                    sessions, rate limits, a few counters
Job queue                resume parsing, email sending, webhook delivery
```

On Tuesday you ship "merge duplicate candidates." A backfill starts at 11:00. The duplicate-detection rule has a bug — it matches on email *domain* rather than address — so instead of merging a few thousand true duplicates, it folds 43,000 distinct people into about 12,000 records. Losing rows are deleted. Their attachments are deleted too, because the merge was written to clean up after itself.

At 14:10 a customer calls: three of their candidates now share one profile and a stranger's resume. You stop the job and do the thing 24.1a made possible — point-in-time recovery of Postgres to 10:58, two minutes before the backfill started. It takes 50 minutes and it works exactly as designed. The database is correct.

Then the rest of the system arrives.

- **Resumes 404.** Postgres is back at 10:58 and knows about 28 million attachments. Object storage is still at 15:00, and 61,000 of those objects were deleted by the merge job. Every candidate page that touches one throws a 500, because nothing in the code ever considered that a row could point at a file that isn't there.
- **Search shows people who no longer exist.** Elasticsearch never rolled back. It holds the merged records. Clicking a search result 404s. It also *doesn't* hold the 43,000 restored candidates in their original form. Reindexing 61 million documents from the restored database takes about nine hours.
- **The queue is full of jobs about a world that ended.** 180,000 jobs enqueued between 11:00 and 15:00 reference application IDs that no longer exist. They fail, retry with backoff, fail again, and the dead-letter queue fills while the retry traffic slows down the jobs you do want.
- **Customers were already told.** Between 11:00 and 14:10 you delivered about 9,000 `candidate.merged` webhooks to customers' own systems, and sent 400 "your application has been updated" emails. Those events are now, in your database, things that never happened. You can't un-deliver them.
- **New candidates get old IDs.** Postgres sequences rolled back with everything else. The next candidate created is assigned ID 8,412,309 — an ID that was already used this morning, already sent to a customer's HRIS over a webhook, and already sitting in Elasticsearch. Two different people now share an identifier across three systems, and nothing errors.

The database restore was correct and fast, and it left the product broken in five ways, two of which are silent. Four hours of cleanup later, the incident's real lesson is a scoping mistake: the team had a backup strategy for *a database*, and the thing they needed to restore was *a system*.

## What It Is

The unit of recovery is the set of stores that together produce what a user sees. Restoring one of them to a past moment while the others stay in the present is not a partial success — it creates a new, inconsistent state that neither existed before nor was intended.

The reason is simple and unavoidable: **your stores don't share a clock, and you can't stop time across all of them.** A backup tool can give you a consistent snapshot of one system. Coordinating a genuinely consistent moment across a database, an object store, a search cluster and a third-party payment provider would require freezing writes to all of them simultaneously, which nobody does for a system that's serving traffic.

```
            10:58 .................................. 15:00 (now)
Postgres    ●─────── restored to 10:58
Objects     ●──────────────────────────────────────● still 15:00
Search      ●──────────────────────────────────────● still 15:00
Queue       ●──────────────────────────────────────● jobs about rows that don't exist
Stripe      ●──────────────────────────────────────● not yours to restore at all
```

So the goal isn't a perfectly consistent restore. It's knowing, in advance, which stores will be out of step, which direction the mismatch runs, and what you'll do about each one. That knowledge is the **state inventory**, and it's the actual deliverable of this article.

The backup world has a name for the weaker version of this problem within a single machine: a **crash-consistent** copy looks like the power was cut, while an **application-consistent** copy was taken with the application quiesced so nothing was half-written. Across separate services, application consistency isn't available at any price. You plan for reconciliation instead.

## Recognizing the Problem

- You can name the database's backup schedule but not what protects uploaded files.
- Your object storage has no versioning, and a lifecycle rule deletes things.
- Rebuilding your search index would take longer than your RTO, and nobody has timed it.
- Nothing in the codebase handles "the row exists but the file doesn't" — that path is a stack trace.
- No one has asked what happens to in-flight and queued jobs after a restore.
- "Restore" means the whole cluster. There's no way to give one customer their data back.
- The secrets you'd need during a recovery are stored in the system being recovered.
- You could not stand up the environment in an empty cloud account, because the running one is the only description of how it's configured.
- You can't say which stores are sources of truth and which are copies — or someone can, but it isn't written down.
- You've sent events to third parties or customers that your database no longer agrees happened.

## How It Works

### Classify every store before you plan anything

Four categories, and the test for each is one question: *if this were empty right now, where would I get it back from?*

**Sources of truth.** Nothing else can produce them: database rows, uploaded files, encryption keys, the audit log. These need real backups, and the set is usually larger than "the database." An uploaded resume is not derived from anything.

**Derived state.** Recomputable from a source of truth: search indexes, thumbnails and transcodes, materialized views, denormalized counters, caches. These need a *recipe* and a measured rebuild time, not necessarily a backup.

**External state.** Lives in someone else's system: charges in Stripe, messages at your email provider, rows you've pushed into customers' systems by webhook or API. You cannot restore it. You can only compare and compensate.

**Ephemeral state.** Sessions, rate-limit counters, in-flight jobs, WebSocket connections. Losing it is a nuisance, not a loss — provided the application treats it as losable, which is the assumption 23.1a was about.

Most bad recoveries come from a miscategorization: treating an uploaded file as if it were derived, or a search index as if it were a source of truth, or external state as if it were yours.

### Derived state is only cheap if the rebuild fits in RTO

"We can just rebuild it" is true and often irrelevant. Sixty-one million documents at a sustained 3,000 docs/sec is about five and a half hours, and the real constraint is usually reading from the database you've only just restored, while it's also serving the application.

Once measured, there are three honest options, and the middle one is the one people forget:

- **Rebuild inside the RTO.** Fine if it fits.
- **Come up degraded and rebuild behind it.** Search returns a banner saying results may be incomplete; everything else works. For a great many products this is dramatically better than being down, and it's a product decision worth making in advance rather than at 03:00.
- **Back up the derived store too.** When the rebuild takes 40 hours, the index has effectively become a source of truth regardless of how it was produced, and a snapshot is cheaper than the rebuild.

This turns into a restore order, which belongs in the runbook (24.3): what has to be up at T+0, what can lag, and what the product looks like in between.

### The four shapes of cross-store mismatch

Every inconsistency after a partial restore is one of these, and each has a different fix:

| Shape | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Dangling reference** | Row points at a deleted object | Tolerate in the app, then recover or mark unavailable |
| **Orphan** | Object with no row pointing at it | Quarantine; never delete immediately |
| **Stale copy** | Index holds post-incident state | Wipe and rebuild from truth |
| **Phantom effect** | Webhook sent for an event that no longer happened | Compare and compensate; can't be undone |

The first two are mirror images and they arrive together, because a point-in-time restore moves the database backwards past both creations and deletions. Rolling back past a *creation* orphans the object; rolling back past a *deletion* dangles the reference.

Two rules make both survivable, and both are cheap to adopt before you need them:

**Never hard-delete an object immediately.** Turn on bucket versioning and let deletes write a delete marker, or move objects to a `quarantine/` prefix with a lifecycle rule that removes them after 30 days. Then a rollback past a deletion is recoverable rather than fatal. This one setting would have saved 61,000 resumes in the scenario above.

**Make the application tolerate a missing blob.** A candidate page that renders "resume unavailable" and increments a counter is a support ticket. The same page throwing a 500 is an outage. This is five lines of code that only matter on one day, which is exactly the kind of code that never gets written unless someone decides to.

### Sequences: the silent one

This is the failure in the scenario that nobody notices for weeks. Point-in-time recovery rolls back sequence counters along with everything else, so the database will re-issue identifiers it already issued this morning. Those identifiers have leaked: into the search index, into webhook payloads sitting in customers' databases, into emails, into your own logs and analytics.

Reused IDs don't produce errors. They produce two people who are the same person according to a system you don't control.

After any restore that moves a database backwards in time, bump every sequence past the highest value the outside world could have seen:

```sql
SELECT setval('candidates_id_seq', (SELECT max(id) FROM candidates) + 100000);
```

The gap is deliberate — you don't know exactly how far the sequence had advanced before the rollback, and gaps are free. Better still, don't have the problem: UUIDv7 or ULID keys are generated from time and randomness, not from a counter that can rewind. The cost is real (bigger indexes, worse locality than a bigint), and it is usually worth paying for anything whose identifier is visible outside your system.

### The restore you'll actually be asked for

The scenario above is the rare one. The common request is: *"Customer 412's admin deleted their entire pipeline. Get it back. The other 899 customers must not notice anything."*

A full rollback can't serve that, because it would discard four hours of work for 899 other companies to fix one. The shape that works is a **side restore**:

```
  production ──────────────────────────────────────▶  (keeps running)
                  ╲
 backup + WAL ──── ● restore to 09:40 into a scratch instance
                          │
                          └─ extract tenant 412 ──▶ merge back into production
```

The extraction is where multi-tenancy design shows up. With a discriminator column, it's a set of queries filtered by `tenant_id`, in foreign-key order. With schema-per-tenant or database-per-tenant, it's a copy of one schema — dramatically simpler, which is one of the underrated arguments in Module 5's trade-off.

Merging back is the hard part, and it needs a decision made by a human: rows the customer has edited *since* the deletion must not be overwritten by their older copies, and rows created since may collide on unique constraints — the workspace slug someone else has taken, an email address reused. Write the merge as insert-if-absent with an explicit conflict policy, run it in a transaction, and count what it changed.

### Rebuilding the environment, not only the data

There's a last category that isn't data at all: the environment. Infrastructure definitions, DNS records, TLS certificates, secrets, third-party configuration (webhook endpoints, OAuth callback URLs, provider API keys), CI credentials, queue and bucket definitions.

Two failure modes recur:

- **The bootstrap paradox.** The credentials you need to start the recovery are in the vault that's down; the runbook is in the wiki hosted on the cluster you're rebuilding. A small break-glass kit — root credentials, the runbook, the key material — stored outside all of it fixes this, and it must have its own access controls and its own review, because it's also a standing backdoor.
- **Drift.** Infrastructure as code describes the environment as of the last time someone used it. Anything changed by hand in the console since then exists only in the console. A restore from code reproduces the environment you documented, not the one you had.

The test is a **cold start**: could you bring this up in an empty account, from code plus backups plus the break-glass kit? You don't need it to be fast. You need to know the answer, and the first attempt always finds three things nobody knew were manual.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Know where every store's data lives, and turn on object versioning: every system.** Versioning is a checkbox and a modest storage bill, and it converts the most common catastrophic case — a delete you can't undo — into an inconvenience.
- **Write tolerant reads when the database and a blob store together make one user-visible object.** Which is to say, nearly every product with file uploads.
- **Measure the rebuild time of derived stores once they exceed a few million records.** Before that, the answer is obviously "rebuild it."
- **Build the side-restore path when you're B2B and multi-tenant**, or when customers can destroy their own data with one click. The first time, do it by hand. Script it the third time, when you know which parts you actually repeat.
- **Rehearse a cold start when a compromised account or a lost region is in your threat model**, or when a customer's security review asks — which for enterprise deals it will.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Single-store systems.** If everything is in Postgres and there are no uploads, 24.1a is the whole story. Don't build an inventory of one.
- **Snapshotting a derived store that rebuilds in six minutes.** Keep the recipe, delete the backup job.
- **Tenant-restore tooling before anyone has asked**, at twelve customers. A manual side restore twice a year is cheaper than a self-service feature you maintain forever.
- **Automated reconciliation with external systems.** For a one-off incident, an export, a spreadsheet and a person is often the correct tool. Automate it when it happens the third time, or when the volume makes human review impossible.
- **Quiescing writes to take a coordinated multi-store snapshot.** You pay write downtime on every backup to avoid a reconciliation you'll have to do anyway. Not worth it outside a few regulated batch-oriented systems.

## Trade-offs

- **Versioning and quarantine prefixes cost storage, and they fight with deletion obligations.** "Deleted" objects that still exist for 30 days are still in scope for an erasure request and for a breach (24.4). Pick the window deliberately.
- **Tolerant reads hide corruption.** A page that quietly renders "unavailable" for a missing file will quietly render it for ten thousand missing files. The placeholder needs a metric and an alert behind it, or degradation becomes the new normal.
- **Reconciliation scripts rot.** They run twice a year, against a schema that changes weekly. Either give them a test in CI or accept that you're writing them fresh during the incident — and if it's the latter, at least write down what they need to do.
- **A side restore needs somewhere to put a full copy of production.** That's cost, and a second copy of your most sensitive data, with its own access control problem. Whoever can run a side restore can read everything.
- **UUID keys cost index size and write locality.** Real, measurable, and usually the right trade for external identifiers — but not free, and not worth retrofitting to an internal join table.
- **More stores, more inventory.** Every store you add — a second search cluster, a vector database, an analytics warehouse — extends the restore plan. That cost belongs in the decision to add it, not in the recovery project two years later.
- **Break-glass credentials are a permanent standing risk** in exchange for being able to recover at all.

## Alternatives

- **Fix forward instead of rolling back.** Often the better answer, and specifically *because* of everything above. A targeted repair script — recompute the 43,000 merged records from an audit log, restore their attachments from versioned objects — keeps the other four hours of everyone's work and leaves the other stores alone. Rewinding is for the case where the correct data genuinely doesn't exist anywhere in the current system. Ask which situation you're in before reaching for PITR; the answer is "fix forward" more often than the incident's panic suggests.
- **Have fewer sources of truth.** Postgres full-text search instead of a separate cluster is a real architectural option below a certain scale (Module 12), and it removes an entire row from the inventory. Every store you don't run is a store you don't have to restore.
- **Route all cross-store effects through a transactional outbox.** If writing a row and telling the outside world are one atomic act, then "what did we tell people that didn't happen?" becomes a query against a table rather than an archaeology project across five log sources (17.1, Module 37).
- **A database per tenant.** Makes per-tenant restore trivial by construction, and multiplies operational work by the number of tenants. Module 5 covers when that trade is right.
- **Soft delete in the application**, again. It's the single highest-leverage item in this whole module for the incidents you'll actually have.

## How to Achieve It

**1. Write the inventory.** One table: every store, its category, what protects it, its RPO, and its rebuild time if derived. It's a two-hour exercise and it usually reveals at least one store nobody was protecting.
*Move on when* every row has a real answer, including the ones that say "nothing."

**2. Turn on versioning and make deletes reversible.** Bucket versioning, a quarantine prefix, a lifecycle rule. Do this before anything else on the list; it's the cheapest item with the largest payoff.
*Move on when* deleting an object in production can be undone tomorrow.

**3. Make reads tolerant of missing derived and blob data**, with a metric behind the placeholder.
*Move on when* deleting a random object from staging degrades one page instead of breaking a request.

**4. Time the rebuild of each derived store**, and write down what the product looks like while it runs.
*Move on when* the restore order and the degraded mode are in the runbook (24.3).

**5. Do one side restore by hand.** Pick a tenant, restore beside production, extract, merge into staging. Time it, and keep every query you wrote.
*Move on when* you've done it once and the queries are in a repository.

**6. Write the post-restore checklist** — bump sequences, purge or quarantine queues, rebuild indexes, reconcile external state, sweep for dangling references and orphans. This is a checklist, not a program, and its value is that nobody has to remember item three at 04:00.
*Move on when* it exists and someone who wasn't there could follow it.

**7. Attempt a cold start in an empty account or namespace**, timeboxed to a day, with the goal of producing a list of things that turned out to be manual.
*Move on when* the list is short enough to be a plan rather than a surprise.

## Implementation

The code is TypeScript, because the two pieces worth showing live in the application and its scripts rather than in infrastructure: a read path that survives a missing object, and the sweep that finds every place the stores disagree. Both are small. Their value is that they exist before the incident.

### A tolerant read

```ts
type Attachment =
  | { state: "ok"; url: string }
  | { state: "missing"; id: string };

async function attachmentFor(row: AttachmentRow): Promise<Attachment> {
  try {
    await s3.send(new HeadObjectCommand({ Bucket: BUCKET, Key: row.key }));
    return { state: "ok", url: await signedUrl(row.key) };
  } catch (err) {
    if (!isNotFound(err)) throw err;         // a real S3 outage must not look like a gap
    metrics.increment("attachment.missing", { bucket: BUCKET });
    return { state: "missing", id: row.id };
  }
}
```

> Distinguishing "this object is gone" from "object storage is unreachable" is the whole point of the `isNotFound` check. Collapsing both into a placeholder means a regional storage outage renders as every candidate having lost their resume, and your dashboards say the data is gone.

### The sweep, in both directions

```ts
// Dry run by default. Nothing in a recovery script should delete on its first run.
async function sweep({ apply = false }) {
  const dangling: string[] = [];
  const orphans: string[] = [];

  // Direction 1: rows whose object is gone (rolled back past a deletion).
  for await (const batch of rowsInBatches(5_000)) {
    const results = await mapWithConcurrency(batch, 50, async (row) => {
      const exists = await objectExists(row.key);
      return exists ? null : row.id;
    });
    dangling.push(...results.filter(Boolean));
  }

  // Direction 2: objects no row references (rolled back past a creation).
  // Only consider objects older than the restore point plus a margin — anything
  // newer may belong to a write that is legitimately still in flight.
  const cutoff = new Date(RESTORE_POINT.getTime() - 15 * 60_000);
  for await (const obj of listObjects(PREFIX)) {
    if (obj.LastModified > cutoff) continue;
    if (!(await rowExistsForKey(obj.Key))) orphans.push(obj.Key);
  }

  report({ dangling: dangling.length, orphans: orphans.length });
  if (!apply) return;

  await markUnavailable(dangling);            // visible in the product, not a 500
  await copyToPrefix(orphans, "quarantine/"); // never delete during an incident
}
```

> **The margin matters more than it looks.** Without it, the sweep races normal traffic: an upload that has written the object but not yet committed its row looks exactly like an orphan, and a sweep that deletes orphans would be destroying live data while you're trying to recover. This is the same interleaving problem as a dual write, and it's why the second direction uses a cutoff and a quarantine rather than a delete.

> **What this deliberately doesn't do.** It doesn't touch external state. Reconciling the 9,000 webhooks you sent for events that no longer happened isn't a sweep — it's a decision about whether to send corrections, which customers can even process them, and whether a support email is the better instrument. Write the *query* that lists them; leave the action to a person.

## Further Reading

- **Martin Kleppmann, _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_, Part III**, especially the distinction between systems of record and derived data, and the chapters on deriving current state from an event log. This is the theory under the four categories above.
- **AWS S3 documentation on versioning, delete markers and lifecycle configuration**, and the equivalents for GCS object versioning and Azure blob soft delete. Short, and the delete-marker semantics are worth reading before relying on them.
- **AWS Well-Architected Framework, Reliability Pillar, REL13**, for the standard ladder of DR strategies — backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, multi-site — which is the framing 24.2b builds on.
- **Elasticsearch documentation on snapshot and restore, and on reindex from remote.** The reindex throughput numbers you measure here decide whether your index is derived or a source of truth in practice.
- **Chris Richardson, "Transactional outbox" (microservices.io)**, for the pattern that makes "what did we tell the outside world" a table rather than an investigation.
- **PostgreSQL documentation on sequence functions** (`setval`, `nextval`) and the **UUIDv7 specification, RFC 9562**, for the two answers to the identifier problem.
- **Your provider's break-glass guidance** — AWS's material on root account and emergency access, or the equivalent — before you invent your own.
- **In this collection:** 24.1a (the database half), Module 11 (object storage, versioning and pre-signed URLs), Module 12 (search indexes and reindexing), Module 10 (queues, retries and dead letters), 17.1 (webhook delivery and idempotency), Module 5 (tenancy, which decides whether per-tenant restore is easy or impossible), Module 13 (caches as ephemeral state), 24.3 (restore order, runbooks and who decides), 24.4 (why versioned deletes complicate erasure), 37.1 (event logs as the reconciliation spine).

A **Go** sibling would be worth writing on the sweep at real scale. At 28 million objects, the script above stops being a script: listing takes hours, the object store rate-limits you, and a crash halfway through means starting over. A Go version could show bounded concurrency with `errgroup`, a resumable checkpoint so the sweep survives being killed, and adaptive throttling when the store starts returning 503s — all of which the Node SDK's paginator hides behind an `await`. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: **for every piece of state, know what else already knows about it.** A restore doesn't move your data back in time — it moves one store back in time, and leaves the rest to disagree with it.
