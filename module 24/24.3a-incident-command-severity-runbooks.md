# Eighty-Two Minutes, Twelve of Them Technical: Incident Command, Severity and Runbooks

_Module 24.3a — Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity_

## The Problem

Suppose you run an expense-management product used by 1,200 companies. At 09:12 on a Monday a routine deploy ships a query that misses an index. Database CPU saturates within three minutes, and the API starts timing out. The technical fix — roll back the deploy — takes four minutes and would work at any point after 09:15.

The outage lasts 82 minutes. Here is where they go.

**09:14** — an alert fires into a channel that receives forty alerts a day. Two people see it. Neither is sure it's real, because that channel is usually noise.

**09:21** — a customer posts publicly. A support agent asks in the engineering channel whether something is up. Three engineers begin investigating, two of them in direct messages with each other. Nobody knows the other two are looking.

**09:29** — everyone is debugging and nobody is deciding. Two mitigations happen within thirty seconds of each other: one engineer rolls back the deploy; another, not knowing that, restarts the database. The restart drops the buffer cache the rollback was about to benefit from, and the next fifteen minutes look exactly like the previous fifteen, so everyone concludes the rollback didn't help.

**09:35** — the CEO joins the channel and asks for an ETA. The engineer who understands the system best spends the next twenty minutes explaining the situation to executives, in prose, four times.

**09:40** — support has nothing to send. Two hundred tickets accumulate. The status page isn't updated, because only one person has the credentials and she's on a flight.

**09:58** — someone finds the runbook. It links to a dashboard deleted in March and a command requiring a VPN profile that no longer exists.

**10:34** — resolved, by rolling back again, properly this time.

Afterwards, nobody can reconstruct what happened. The decisions were made in DMs and on a call nobody recorded. The timeline in the review document is a guess.

Twelve minutes of this was technical work. The other seventy were coordination — or the absence of it. And notice that none of the previous articles in this module would have helped: the backups were fine, the standby was fine, the failover mechanism was irrelevant. The thing that was missing was a way for six people to work on one problem without getting in each other's way.

## What It Is

Incident response is a structure agreed in advance for coordinating people under time pressure and incomplete information. It exists because the default — everyone competent does whatever seems most useful — produces exactly the morning above.

The structure comes, by a fairly direct lineage, from the **Incident Command System** developed for wildfire response in the 1970s. Its central insight transfers without modification: under pressure, **separate deciding, doing, and communicating**, because one person cannot hold all three and the person who tries is always the one who understands the system best — precisely the person you most need thinking.

A few terms, each of which is doing real work:

- An **incident** is any situation you've decided to coordinate around. It doesn't require an outage. A stuck queue, a data-integrity problem, a leaked credential and a partial degradation are all incidents.
- An incident is **declared**, not discovered. Declaring is an act: it creates a channel, a commander, and a clock.
- **Severity** describes the response you're committing to, not the damage.
- **Mitigation** makes the symptoms stop. **Resolution** is when you're confident they'll stay stopped. **Understanding** is a separate activity that happens later (24.3b).

That last distinction is the one most worth internalising early. During an incident, your job is to make it stop. Curiosity is a liability — if a deploy correlates in time, roll it back before you understand why, because understanding takes an hour and rolling back takes four minutes.

One neighbouring idea, since they're routinely conflated: alerting and on-call (Module 19) tell you something is wrong and get a human involved. Incident response is everything that happens after that human wakes up.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Nobody said the word "incident." It was just a bad morning that everyone reacted to.
- You can't reconstruct last month's timeline, because the decisions were in DMs and a call.
- The person who understood the system best was also the one briefing executives.
- Two people made changes without knowing about each other.
- Support found out from customers.
- The status page wasn't updated, or only one person can update it.
- Your runbook references a dashboard, command or tool that no longer exists.
- Severity was determined by who complained loudest.
- People hesitate to declare, because declaring feels like escalating, or like an accusation.
- "Who's running this?" either has no answer or the answer is "everyone."
- The incident ended when the graphs recovered, and nothing was written down.

## How It Works

### Declaring, and why the bar should be low

The cost asymmetry is stark. A declared incident that turns out to be nothing costs a handful of people ten minutes. An undeclared real one costs an hour of duplicated, uncoordinated work — and the hour is only visible afterwards.

So set the bar deliberately low and say so out loud: *anyone may declare, for any reason, and nobody is ever criticised for declaring something that turned out to be minor.* The first time someone gets a raised eyebrow for a false alarm, you've taught the whole team to hesitate, and hesitation is the expensive failure.

Declaring should be one action that produces four things: a dedicated channel, a named commander, a severity, and a timestamp.

### Severity is a decision about response

Write severity levels in terms of what you commit to doing, not how bad it feels. Three levels are enough for almost everyone:

| | Trigger | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Sev1** | Core function unusable, or data at risk | Page the on-call and the owning team; IC required; status page immediately; updates every 30 min |
| **Sev2** | Significant degradation, or a subset of customers affected | Page the owning team; IC required; status page if it exceeds 30 min; updates hourly |
| **Sev3** | Contained; workaround exists | Handled in business hours; no paging; channel and ticket only |

Two rules make the ladder work. **Anyone can raise severity; only the commander lowers it.** And **when in doubt, start higher** — downgrading twenty minutes in costs nothing, while upgrading means you spent twenty minutes under-resourced.

One branch deserves separate handling. **Security and data-integrity incidents follow different rules**: preserve evidence rather than restarting things, restrict the channel, involve legal early, and be careful what you write down in a place that may later be discoverable. If you have a regulatory notification clock — GDPR's 72 hours, say — it starts before you've finished investigating (Module 34). The instinct to "fix it fast" is wrong here, and it's worth knowing that in advance rather than discovering it mid-incident.

### The roles, and what each one is forbidden from doing

```
                    ┌────────────────────┐
   executives ◀─────│   Communications   │─────▶ customers, support
                    └─────────▲──────────┘
                              │   one voice, one cadence
                    ┌─────────┴──────────┐
                    │ Incident Commander │   decides; does not type
                    └─────────▲──────────┘
                              │
             ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐
         Ops / SME        Ops / SME          Scribe
      (hands on system) (hands on system)  (timestamps)
```

**Incident Commander.** Owns the incident and fixes nothing. This is the counterintuitive part and the whole point: the moment the IC starts debugging, nobody is tracking state. The IC's loop is four questions, repeated out loud: *what do we know, what are we doing, who is doing it, when do we check back.* Every thirty minutes at most, they force a checkpoint — even when momentum feels good, especially then.

The IC does not have to be the most senior or the most knowledgeable person. It is often better if they aren't, because your best expert is most valuable thinking, not coordinating. On a small team the IC is frequently whoever is least directly involved.

**Operations / subject-matter experts.** The only people who touch the system. They announce what they're about to do *before* doing it, in the channel, and report the result.

**Communications.** Talks to support, customers and executives so the IC doesn't. The most valuable thing this role does is absorb the CEO. When an executive asks for an ETA, the correct destination for that question is the comms lead, and the correct answer is usually "next update at 10:30."

**Scribe.** Timestamps decisions and observations. This is nearly free if the coordination already happens in one channel, and nearly impossible to reconstruct if it doesn't.

At four people, one person can hold two roles. At two people, one is IC-and-comms and the other has hands on the system. The separation that matters most, and the one to preserve longest, is **IC from ops**.

### One change at a time, announced

The restart-during-rollback in the scenario is the canonical coordination failure, and the rule that prevents it is mechanical:

> All mitigations go through the IC. One change in flight at a time. Announce before executing, report after.

The second reason for this rule is subtler than avoiding collisions: with two simultaneous changes, you can't attribute the effect. The rollback did work; the restart hid it; the team concluded the rollback was useless and spent fifteen minutes looking elsewhere. Serialising changes is what keeps the feedback loop legible.

Prefer mitigations that are reversible and fast — roll back, turn off the feature flag, shed load, fail over (24.2a) — over investigative ones. "Let me check something in the database" is not a mitigation.

### Communication is a deliverable with a cadence

The single most useful rule: **update on a schedule, whether or not you have news.** "No new information; investigating; next update at 10:30" is a complete and valuable update. It stops the stream of individual questions that otherwise consumes the person answering them, and it tells customers you haven't forgotten.

Practical points that repeatedly matter:

- **Tell support before you tell customers.** They're absorbing the tickets, and a prepared response macro is worth more to them than an engineer's explanation.
- **Host the status page outside your own infrastructure.** A status page that's down during your outage is a recurring, entirely avoidable embarrassment (24.2b).
- **Have templates.** Writing prose at minute nine of a Sev1 produces either nothing or something you regret. Three templates — investigating, identified, resolved — cover nearly everything.
- **One voice.** Engineers speculating publicly, in parallel with official updates, is how "we believe this is contained" and "we're not sure of the scope" go out within a minute of each other.

### Runbooks, and why they rot

A runbook is a procedure for a situation you've seen before. The failure mode isn't that runbooks are a bad idea; it's that they're write-once artifacts describing a system that changes weekly, read only by someone tired and stressed.

What keeps them alive:

- **Link them from the alert that fires.** A runbook nobody can find at 03:00 doesn't exist. The alert should carry its own URL.
- **Write them at the moment of confusion**, by the person who was confused — during or immediately after an incident, not in a planned documentation sprint. That person knows exactly which step was missing.
- **State the expected output of each step.** "Run this; you should see roughly 200 connections; if you see zero, the pooler is down and you're in the wrong runbook." Without expected output, a reader can't tell when the runbook is wrong, and at 03:00 they will assume they are.
- **Give each one an owner and a last-verified date**, and treat "used but not edited" as suspicious — someone followed it and either it was perfect or they worked around a gap silently.
- **Turn the deterministic parts into scripts.** A step that's the same every time isn't documentation, it's un-automated code.

Worth distinguishing from its neighbour: a **playbook** covers a class of situation where judgment is required — "the primary region is degraded," where the content is decision criteria and options (24.2b). A **runbook** covers a specific known procedure — "the export worker queue is backed up" — where the content is steps. Runbooks can be automated away; playbooks can't, because their value is the thinking they structure.

### Handoffs and endings

Incidents that outlast a shift need an explicit handoff: the outgoing IC states current status, what's been tried, what's in flight, and who's doing what, and the incoming IC repeats it back. Anything less and the new commander spends twenty minutes rediscovering the last two hours.

And an incident should be **declared resolved**, not allowed to fade out. Say it, name who is watching for recurrence, and schedule the review before people disperse — because the review will not get scheduled tomorrow (24.3b).

## When It's Appropriate

- **A lightweight version — declare, one commander, one channel, scheduled updates — from about five engineers and the first paying customers.** The whole thing fits on one page.
- **Full role separation once incidents routinely involve more than three people**, or once executive attention during incidents is a predictable factor.
- **A written severity ladder once you have an SLA, support staff, or a status page** — i.e. once "how bad is it" determines obligations to someone outside the team.
- **Practice when incidents are rare.** A team that has a serious incident twice a year is rusty every time. The drills from 24.1a and 24.2a double as incident-response practice, which is the cheapest way to get both.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A four-person team with twenty users.** "Say something in the channel, one person leads" is the entire process, and buying incident-management software at that size is a way to feel prepared rather than be prepared.
- **Five severity levels with twenty qualifying criteria.** Nobody can apply that at 03:00; they'll guess, and the guess will be inconsistent, which is worse than three coarse levels applied reliably.
- **Appointing a commander for a five-minute problem one person is already fixing.** The process should be proportional; a Sev3 doesn't need a ceremony.
- **A formal review for every blip.** It devalues the practice and fills calendars, which is how reviews stop happening for the incidents that needed them.
- **Runbooks for one-off situations**, or for anything you could delete the need for. Writing a runbook is sometimes an elaborate way of avoiding a fix.

## Trade-offs

- **Process has overhead, and it can feel absurd** when a single engineer could have fixed it in three minutes. Some of that friction is the price of the cases where it isn't three minutes, but the friction is real and worth keeping proportional.
- **Role separation means your best expert isn't in charge**, which needs genuine cultural buy-in. If "IC" is treated as a seniority marker rather than a job, the separation collapses back into the expert doing everything.
- **Announce-before-you-change costs seconds** in a moment when seconds feel expensive. It still pays, but it's not free and it takes discipline to hold.
- **A low declaration bar means more incidents on the chart.** If anyone counts incidents as a quality metric, you've created pressure to under-declare — which quietly destroys the practice. Count something else.
- **A wrong runbook is worse than no runbook**, because it's trusted at exactly the moment nobody has capacity to be skeptical.
- **A communication cadence commits you** to producing updates while you're busy. That's the point, and it's also a real tax on the comms lead.
- **On-call is a human cost.** Sleep, stress and attrition are the actual currency here. Rotations smaller than about six people are corrosive, follow-up work from incidents has to displace planned work rather than stack on top of it, and compensation for being on-call should exist.

## Alternatives

- **No formal process — whoever's around handles it.** Genuinely adequate below a certain size. Its failure mode is that it degrades invisibly: it stops working at some headcount and nobody notices until an incident like the one above.
- **Escalation-only.** The owning team handles everything, and a manager gets pulled in if it drags. Works in small organisations with clear ownership; breaks when incidents span teams, which is when the interesting ones happen.
- **Automated remediation.** The best incident is one nobody attends: auto-rollback on an error-rate regression, circuit breakers (36.3), load shedding, automatic restarts. Every runbook executed more than about three times is a candidate. The caveats are real — automated remediation can mask a worsening problem until it's much larger, and it can misfire in exactly the way 24.2a describes — so it wants a rate limit, an audit trail, and a kill switch.
- **Buy the tooling.** PagerDuty, incident.io, FireHydrant and their peers encode most of this article. Worth it once incident volume justifies it; before then a Slack workflow and a template document do the same job. Either way, don't let the tool become the process: the process is people agreeing who decides.
- **Outsourced NOC or managed on-call.** Buys you coverage at the cost of context. Usually works for infrastructure-level alerts and poorly for anything product-specific.

## How to Achieve It

**1. Write one page**: what counts as an incident, how to declare, who to page, and the three severity levels.
*Move on when* someone who joined last week could declare correctly.

**2. Name the commander role and use it in a low-stakes incident.** Rotate it. The first few times will be awkward; that's the training.
*Move on when* someone other than the most senior engineer has run one.

**3. Move coordination into one visible channel and ban decision-making in DMs.**
*Move on when* the timeline can be reconstructed from the channel alone.

**4. Add the comms layer**: three templates, a status page hosted elsewhere, an explicit update cadence per severity.
*Move on when* an update went out on schedule with nothing new to say.

**5. Attach a runbook URL to every alert that pages a human.** Alerts that page and have no runbook are either missing one or shouldn't page.
*Move on when* that's true for every paging alert.

**6. Write runbooks only in the moment of confusion**, immediately after an incident, by whoever was lost.
*Move on when* two runbooks have been written that way.

**7. Automate the third repetition.** When you notice you've run the same runbook three times, convert it.
*Move on when* at least one runbook has been deleted because the situation can no longer occur or resolves itself.

**8. Run a game day each quarter**, reusing the restore and failover drills from 24.1a and 24.2a. The technical exercise is the excuse; the coordination practice is the return.
*Move on when* a drill surfaces a process gap rather than a technical one — that's the sign the technical side is in reasonable shape.

## Implementation

There isn't much code in incident response, and most attempts to write some produce a tool that stands in for the agreement it was supposed to support. Two pieces are worth building, both small, and the code is TypeScript because this lives in whatever chat platform your team already uses.

### Incident state as one pinned object

The trick that makes everything else cheap: keep the incident's state in one place that's always visible, and let the tool enforce the cadence nobody remembers under pressure.

```ts
type Incident = {
  id: string;                 // INC-2026-041
  severity: 1 | 2 | 3;
  status: "investigating" | "identified" | "mitigating" | "monitoring" | "resolved";
  commander: string;          // never empty; declaring assigns one
  comms?: string;
  summary: string;            // one line, customer-facing language
  startedAt: Date;
  nextUpdateDue: Date;        // the cadence, made mechanical
  timeline: { at: Date; who: string; text: string }[];
};

// The pinned message is rendered from this object. Nobody edits prose; they run
// commands, and the state and the timeline stay in sync by construction.
async function declare(by: string, severity: 1 | 2 | 3, summary: string) {
  const inc: Incident = {
    id: nextId(), severity, status: "investigating", commander: by,
    summary, startedAt: new Date(),
    nextUpdateDue: addMinutes(new Date(), cadenceFor(severity)),
    timeline: [{ at: new Date(), who: by, text: `declared Sev${severity}: ${summary}` }],
  };
  const channel = await chat.createChannel(`inc-${inc.id.toLowerCase()}`);
  await chat.pin(channel, render(inc));
  if (severity <= 2) await pager.page(onCallFor(summary), inc);
  return save(channel, inc);
}
```

> **The scribe's job is a by-product.** Because every state change goes through a command in the channel, the timeline builds itself. The reason the scenario's team couldn't reconstruct their morning is that the decisions happened in DMs — a tooling problem only incidentally, and a norms problem mostly.

```ts
// Runs every minute. The one piece of nagging that consistently earns its keep.
async function checkUpdateCadence() {
  for (const inc of await openIncidents()) {
    if (inc.status !== "resolved" && inc.nextUpdateDue < new Date()) {
      await chat.post(inc.channel,
        `@${inc.comms ?? inc.commander} update due. "No new information" is a valid update.`);
      inc.nextUpdateDue = addMinutes(new Date(), cadenceFor(inc.severity));
      await save(inc.channel, inc);
    }
  }
}
```

### An executable runbook step

The part of a runbook that never rots is the part that runs.

```ts
// Each step states what it expects. A step that can't say what "good" looks like
// is a step the reader can't evaluate at 3am, and usually shouldn't be a step.
const steps: Step[] = [
  {
    name: "Is the export queue actually backed up?",
    run: () => redis.llen("queue:exports"),
    expect: (depth: number) => depth < 5_000,
    ifUnexpected: "Depth above 5k confirms the backlog. Continue. " +
                  "Depth near zero means the symptom is elsewhere — stop and re-triage.",
  },
  {
    name: "Are workers alive?",
    run: () => countHealthyWorkers(),
    expect: (n: number) => n >= 4,
    ifUnexpected: "Fewer than 4 workers: scale the deployment (step 3). " +
                  "Exactly 0: check the image pull — see INC-2026-011.",
  },
  {
    name: "Scale workers to 12",
    destructive: true,          // requires an explicit confirmation, and is logged
    run: () => k8s.scale("export-worker", 12),
    expect: () => true,
    ifUnexpected: "",
  },
];
```

> **Why `destructive` is a flag and not a comment.** The runner prints a diff, asks for confirmation, and writes the action into the incident timeline automatically. That's the announce-before-you-change rule from earlier, enforced by the tool rather than by someone remembering it at minute forty.

> **What this deliberately isn't.** It stops short of running the steps by itself. An executable runbook that self-drives is automated remediation, which is a good thing to build *once the procedure has proven stable* — and a bad thing to arrive at by accident, because it needs a kill switch, a rate limit and an audit trail that a diagnostic script doesn't.

## Further Reading

- **Google, _Site Reliability Engineering_, chapters 12–14** — troubleshooting, emergency response, and "Managing Incidents," which is explicitly modelled on the Incident Command System and is the densest few pages on this subject anywhere. Chapter 11 on being on-call is the human-cost companion.
- **PagerDuty's Incident Response documentation** (response.pagerduty.com). Free, opinionated, and directly usable: role definitions, severity matrices, and scripts for what an IC actually says.
- **FEMA's ICS-100 course material.** Forty minutes, free, and it's where all of this comes from. Reading the original makes the software version make more sense.
- **Atlassian's Incident Management Handbook**, for a smaller-organisation take with concrete templates.
- **Atul Gawande, _The Checklist Manifesto_.** The argument for why checklists work on experts under pressure, which is the theory behind runbooks having expected outputs and explicit steps.
- **Casey Rosenthal and Nora Jones, _Chaos Engineering_**, for designing game days that produce learning rather than theatre.
- **In this collection:** Module 19 (alerting, and the alert quality that decides whether anyone notices), 24.1a and 24.2a (the drills that double as practice), 24.2b (regional evacuation as a playbook decision rather than a runbook), 24.3b (the review afterwards), 30.2 (rollbacks and feature flags — the mitigations you want to reach for first), 36.3 (circuit breakers, which turn some incidents into non-events), Module 34 (regulatory notification clocks that start during, not after).

A **Go** sibling would be worth writing on the step after this one: turning a stable runbook into an automated remediation controller. The interesting content is all in the guardrails rather than the actions — a rate limiter so a flapping condition can't trigger fifty scale-ups, a kill switch reachable when the thing it controls is broken, an audit trail that lands in the incident timeline, and a hard rule that the controller escalates to a human rather than escalating its own actions. Go's controller idioms make that shape natural, and the TypeScript chat tooling above can't show any of it. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: **the technical part of an outage is usually the short part.** When you read your next incident's timeline, mark which minutes were spent fixing and which were spent finding out who was doing what — and treat the second number as a design problem, not a personality one.
