# The Log You Must Keep and the Data You Must Forget: Retention, Erasure, and Audit Logs

_Module 24.4 — Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a freelance marketplace. Clients post projects, freelancers bid, they message each other, you process payments and issue invoices, and — because money moves through you — you verify freelancers' identities by collecting a passport scan.

A freelancer named Maria closes her account and asks, under GDPR, for her data to be erased. The "delete account" button does what it has done since launch:

```sql
DELETE FROM users WHERE id = 8812;   -- ON DELETE CASCADE everywhere
```

Here is what that one statement did and didn't do.

**It deleted too much.** Her invoices cascaded away — records that tax law obliges you to keep for years. So did every message thread she was part of, which means eleven clients just lost their own conversation history about work they paid for. A year later, an auditor finds gaps in your invoice numbering.

**It deleted too little.** Her profile photo is still in object storage, unreferenced, and the CDN will serve it for a year because of the cache headers. The search index lists her profile for three more days, until the nightly reindex. The analytics warehouse has four years of her events, keyed by her email address. Thirty days of request logs contain her email and IP, and the error tracker has captured several of her request bodies — including one with her bank details. Her data was also sent by webhook to your CRM, your email provider and your payment processor, and none of them has been told anything.

**It can be undone by accident.** Your backups are immutable for 35 days (24.1a). Six weeks later, the team does a side restore to recover another customer's deleted project (24.1b) — and, briefly, Maria exists again. The CRM sync picks her up. She receives a marketing email. She complains to the data-protection authority, and she has a point.

**And one record got it right by accident, then wrong on purpose.** Your audit log recorded every time a support agent viewed her passport scan. But each entry is free text — *"agent@company viewed passport for Maria Keller (maria.k@example.com)"* — so the log now contains exactly the personal data you just promised to erase. Either you edit the audit log, and it stops being evidence of anything, or you keep it and explain why.

This is where the whole module comes together uncomfortably. Everything in 24.1 was about making copies — more of them, in more places, harder to destroy. Everything here is about being able to make data disappear on request, and about keeping one kind of record that must never disappear or change at all. The three pull against each other, and none of them is optional.

## What It Is

Three ideas, which only make sense together.

**A retention policy** says, for each category of data, how long you keep it and why. It has a *floor* — legal obligations like tax and anti-money-laundering rules that require you to keep something — and a *ceiling*, because GDPR's storage-limitation principle requires you to stop keeping personal data once its purpose is served. Data lives between the two. "Forever" is not a retention policy; it's an absence of one, and it means every future breach includes everything you've ever held.

**An erasure workflow** finds a person's data in every place it lives — including systems you don't own — and deletes or anonymises it, *except* where the floor says you must keep it. An erasure request doesn't delete everything about someone. It deletes everything you no longer have a basis to hold, and restricts the rest.

**An audit log** is an append-only record of who did what, to which data, when. It must be complete (no action without an entry), trustworthy (the people being audited can't change it), and governed by its own retention rules — because a record of who viewed what is itself personal data.

A cluster of neighbouring terms, since the differences matter legally:

- **Soft delete** — the undo mechanism recommended in 24.1a — is the opposite of erasure. A row with `deleted_at` set still holds all the data. It's the right answer for accidents and the wrong answer for an erasure request.
- **Pseudonymised** data, where names are replaced by an ID but a mapping exists somewhere, is still personal data under GDPR. **Anonymised** data, where re-identification is genuinely not reasonably possible, is not. Genuine anonymisation is much harder than removing the name column, and most "anonymised" datasets are pseudonymised.
- An **application log** is for debugging, with a short life. An **audit log** is for accountability, with a long life and strict integrity. An **event log** in an event-sourced system (Module 37) is the source of truth itself. All three are append-only, and all three collide with erasure in the same way — which is why one technique below serves all three.

The retention *periods* themselves are legal decisions that belong to your lawyers and Module 34. This article is the engineering that makes whatever they decide actually true.

## Recognizing the Problem

- "Delete account" is one `DELETE` with cascades.
- Nobody can list every store that holds a given user's data.
- Asked "how long do we keep X, and why?", the answer is a shrug or "forever."
- Emails, IPs, names or request bodies appear in application logs, the error tracker, or analytics events.
- Your backups are immutable for longer than the erasure deadline you promise, and nobody has reconciled the two.
- A restore would bring deleted users back to life.
- A deleted user's photo is still on the CDN, or their profile still turns up in search.
- Audit entries contain personal data in free text.
- Anyone with database write access can edit or delete audit records — including the people whose actions they record.
- Data sent to third parties by webhook or sync has no deletion path at all.
- Deleting a user breaks other users' records — invoices, threads, shared documents.

## How It Works

### Retention is a matrix, not a number

For each category, write a floor, a ceiling, and what happens at expiry. For the marketplace:

| Category | Floor (must keep) | Ceiling (must stop) | At expiry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Invoices | tax law — years | same | delete or cold-archive |
| Identity documents | AML rules — typically 5 years after the relationship ends | same | delete |
| Messages | none | account life + 30 days | delete |
| Request logs | none | 30 days | delete |
| Product analytics | none | 25 months | aggregate, drop the identifiers |
| Audit log | security/compliance basis — e.g. 2 years | same | drop the partition |
| Backups | none | 35 days | expire |

Now Maria's request has a precise meaning. Messages: erase. Photo, profile, analytics identity: erase. Invoices: keep, because a legal obligation overrides erasure, but keep only what the obligation actually requires. Identity documents: keep until the AML period ends, restricted from use, then delete. Audit entries about her: keep for their own stated period, but — as we'll see — without her name in them.

The matrix does a second job: short ceilings are the cheapest erasure mechanism that exists. If request logs live 14 days and backups 30, then most of the "long tail" of Maria's data disappears on its own before the one-month response deadline, and your job is to document that rather than chase it.

### Design so that deletion is possible

The scenario's cascade failure is a schema problem, not a deletion problem. Four habits fix most of it:

**Tombstone the person, don't delete the row.** Keep the user row as `{id: 8812, status: 'erased', erased_at: …}` with every personal field nulled. Foreign keys stay intact; the clients' message threads render "Deleted user"; nothing cascades.

**Split retained records from personal profiles.** An invoice shouldn't point at the live `users` row for its billing details; it should carry a snapshot of the billing party at issue time. Then erasure can remove the profile while the invoice, which the law requires, keeps exactly the fields the law requires and nothing more.

**Key every secondary store on an internal ID, never on an email.** If analytics, logs and search know Maria as `8812`, then erasing her identity in the primary store makes most of her secondary footprint pseudonymous in one move — and the rest can be found by that ID.

**Don't collect what you'll have to delete.** Don't log request bodies. Truncate IP addresses. Configure the error tracker to scrub fields before sending. Minimisation is the only deletion technique with no failure modes.

### Erasure is a saga across stores

Deleting a person across eight systems, two of which are slow, one of which is down, and three of which belong to other companies, is a long-running distributed job with partial failures. It's the saga pattern (Module 10, 37.4) wearing a legal deadline.

```
erasure request ──▶ ledger: subject 8812, received 2026-09-21, due 2026-10-21
                     ├─ postgres        ✓ tombstoned; invoices retained (basis: tax)
                     ├─ object storage  ✓ 14 objects deleted; CDN paths purged
                     ├─ search index    ✓ document removed
                     ├─ analytics       ✓ identity mapping deleted; events now anonymous
                     ├─ app logs        ⧗ expire by 2026-10-21 (30-day ceiling)
                     ├─ backups         ⧗ expire by 2026-10-26; suppressed on restore
                     ├─ email provider  ✓ contact deleted via API
                     └─ CRM             ✗ retrying (3 of 10)
```

The properties that matter:

- **A ledger, per request, per store**, recording what was done and when. It's your proof of compliance and your retry queue at once. It must hold only the internal ID, never the person's details — a list of everyone who asked to be forgotten is precisely the list you shouldn't be keeping in readable form.
- **Idempotent steps**, because retries are guaranteed.
- **"Expires naturally" is a legitimate status**, provided it's true and documented.
- **Third parties need explicit deletion calls**, and your contracts with them (data processing agreements, Module 34) should require them to honour those calls.

### Backups against erasure: the three answers

Immutable backups (24.1a) and a one-month erasure deadline can't both be satisfied by rewriting backups — that's the point of immutability. There are three ways out, and one of them is a trap.

**Short expiry, plus suppression on restore.** Keep backups for a documented period no longer than your erasure commitment can tolerate. Keep the erasure ledger *outside* the backups. Any restore — full, point-in-time, or a side restore of one tenant — replays every erasure recorded since the restore point before the data goes anywhere live. The UK ICO's guidance describes the target state as putting backup data "beyond use": not deletable yet, but never restored into processing. This is the pragmatic answer for most systems, and it turns into a single assertion in the restore drill.

**Crypto-shredding.** Encrypt each person's personal fields with a key unique to that person, held in a separate key store. Erasure means destroying the key. Every copy of that data, in every backup, every replica and every event log, becomes permanently unreadable at once. It's elegant and it's the standard answer for event-sourced systems and data lakes that can't be rewritten. Its costs are real: the key store becomes a critical dependency (lose it and you lose everyone's data), you can't index or search encrypted fields, and the key store's *own* backups must expire quickly — otherwise you've backed up the keys you're trying to destroy.

**Rewriting backups on each request.** Mentioned so it can be dismissed: expensive, incompatible with object-lock, and an operational risk to the backups you're supposed to be protecting.

### Audit logs you can actually trust

An audit log answers "who accessed or changed this, and when" — for security investigations, customer disputes, compliance audits, and the insider threat that everyone would rather not discuss. It earns trust through four properties.

**Complete.** Every audited action produces an entry. The classic failure is a dual write: perform the action, then call a separate audit service. A crash between the two produces an action with no record — which, in an audit log, is the one outcome that matters. Write the entry in the same database transaction as the action, or through a transactional outbox (17.1) that ships entries onward.

**Unalterable by the audited.** The support agent whose passport views are recorded must not be able to delete those records, and neither should the application's normal database role. Insert-only permissions for the writer, a separate store or schema, and eventual delivery to write-once storage — the same object-lock mechanism as 24.1a's backups, used for a different reason.

**Tamper-evident.** Chain the entries: each includes a cryptographic hash of the previous one, so altering any entry breaks every hash after it. A chain *detects* tampering; it doesn't prevent it — someone with full write access can recompute the entire chain. What stops that is **anchoring**: periodically publishing the latest hash somewhere the attacker doesn't control — another account, another system, an external timestamping service. Certificate Transparency logs apply the same idea at internet scale with Merkle trees.

```
 entry 1041        entry 1042        entry 1043        anchor (hourly, elsewhere)
┌──────────┐     ┌──────────┐     ┌──────────┐
│ data     │     │ data     │     │ data     │
│ prev: 7c…│◀────│ prev: a9…│◀────│ prev: 3e…│ ─────▶  "1043 = 51f…"
│ hash: a9…│     │ hash: 3e…│     │ hash: 51f│
└──────────┘     └──────────┘     └──────────┘
 edit 1042 → its hash changes → 1043's prev no longer matches → the anchor disagrees
```

**Useful.** Each entry records the actor, *including who they were acting as* when an admin impersonates a user (Module 26 — impersonation without this is an audit hole); the action; the subject and target, by ID; the time; the source; the outcome — **including denials**, which are often the most informative entries; and, for privileged access, a reason or ticket reference.

And the move that resolves the scenario's last problem: **refer to people by internal ID, never by name or email in free text.** Then erasing Maria tombstones her identity in the users table, and the audit log remains intact and meaningful — "subject 8812, erased 2026-09-22, was viewed by agent 311 on …" — without containing her personal data. The log keeps its own retention, justified by its own basis, and time partitioning makes expiry a partition drop rather than a delete.

What to audit is a judgment call. Privileged and admin actions, permission changes, authentication events, exports, deletions themselves, and **reads** of genuinely sensitive data — identity documents, health records, payroll. Not every read of every row: that produces a vast store of behavioural personal data with no one ever reading it.

## When It's Appropriate

- **The retention matrix: any system holding personal data**, which is nearly all of them. Storage limitation is a principle of the regulation, not an optional feature.
- **Tombstones and split records** as soon as deleting one user can damage another user's data — which, in anything with collaboration, messaging or billing, is from day one.
- **An automated erasure workflow once requests exceed a handful a month.** Below that, a written checklist and the ledger, executed by hand, is fine and honest.
- **Crypto-shredding when immutable stores outlive your erasure window** — event-sourced systems, long-retention data lakes, or backups you're obliged to keep longer than a month.
- **Tamper-evident audit logs in regulated domains**, for admin tooling with impersonation, when enterprise customers ask in security reviews (they will), and wherever an insider could have motive.
- **Read auditing for high-sensitivity data**, specifically and narrowly.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Crypto-shredding when backups already expire inside the erasure window.** Suppression on restore plus natural expiry does the job with far less machinery.
- **A ledger database or blockchain for audit logs.** A hash chain, insert-only permissions, write-once storage and an external anchor give you the property that matters at a small fraction of the complexity.
- **Auditing every read of non-sensitive data.** High volume, no reader, and a new personal-data store to govern.
- **Building an erasure orchestrator for three requests a year.** Do them by hand from a checklist, record them in the ledger, and automate once the volume is real.
- **Anonymisation projects when deletion would serve.** Anonymising properly is a research problem. If you don't need the data, delete it.

## Trade-offs

- **Tombstones and split records add schema complexity**, and every query now has to consider an `erased` status.
- **Erasure is irreversible**, which cuts against every instinct 24.1b cultivated. Soft delete handles accidents; verified erasure is final. If you offer a grace period between request and execution, say so to the user — and don't let the grace period become a way of never erasing.
- **Crypto-shredding moves risk into key management.** The key store is critical-path, per-row decryption costs CPU, encrypted fields can't be indexed, and the key store's own backup policy is a small, sharp edge.
- **Suppression lists are a list of people who asked to be forgotten.** Keep only internal IDs, restrict access, and treat the list itself as sensitive.
- **Audit logs are large, and they're personal data.** A record of behaviour needs its own access controls, its own retention and its own place in the erasure map.
- **Transactional audit writes add latency and couple every audited write path to the audit schema.** An outbox decouples them at the cost of delay before the entry reaches write-once storage.
- **Hash chains serialise writes.** Every entry needs the previous hash, which becomes a contention point; chaining per tenant or per time partition, with each chain anchored, restores throughput and makes partition-based expiry possible without breaking the chain.
- **Short log retention hurts debugging and incident review**, as 24.3b noted about metrics expiring. Security logs can justify a longer period under a separate basis; application debug logs usually can't, and that's a real cost to accept rather than argue around.
- **You depend on third parties' deletion** — their APIs, their honesty, and your contracts with them.

## Alternatives

- **Minimise first.** Every field you don't collect and every body you don't log is a deletion you never have to perform. It's the only item in this article that reduces cost rather than moving it.
- **Short retention as default policy.** Arguably the most effective single decision here: 14-day logs and 30-day backups solve most of the erasure long tail without any tooling.
- **Anonymise instead of delete, for analytics.** Keeps statistical value. Only safe when done properly — aggregation, suppression of small groups, formal techniques — because re-identification from "anonymous" behavioural data is routinely possible.
- **Per-tenant or per-user storage.** Makes erasure a drop, as it made restore a copy in 24.1b, at the operational cost Module 5 describes.
- **Buy the orchestration.** Privacy platforms that handle data-subject requests across common SaaS integrations are worth it once request volume and third-party count are both high.
- **Provider audit trails** — the cloud provider's own logs of infrastructure actions — cover who changed a bucket policy or a database parameter. They don't cover who viewed Maria's passport in your admin panel. Application-level audit is always yours to build.

## How to Achieve It

**1. Build the retention matrix with your lawyers**, writing the reason for each row.
*Move on when* every data category has a floor, a ceiling and an expiry action.

**2. Trace one synthetic person end to end**, starting from 24.1b's store inventory.
*Move on when* you can list every place a test user's data lands, including third parties.

**3. Stop the leaks at the source.** Redact logs, scrub the error tracker, key analytics on internal IDs, truncate IPs.
*Move on when* searching every log and tracker for a test user's email returns nothing.

**4. Replace cascading hard deletes with tombstones**, and give retained records their own snapshots.
*Move on when* erasing a test user leaves every other user's records intact.

**5. Build the erasure ledger and the per-store steps**, idempotent, with third parties handled manually at first.
*Move on when* a request can be executed and proven complete from the ledger alone.

**6. Reconcile backups.** Set expiry within your commitment, add restore-time suppression, and put an assertion in the 24.1a drill that erased subjects stay erased.
*Move on when* a restore drill fails if it resurrects anyone.

**7. Move the audit log to its own store**, written transactionally or through an outbox, with insert-only permissions and subjects referenced by ID.
*Move on when* nobody who is audited can alter an entry, and no entry contains a name or email.

**8. Add tamper evidence**: a hash chain per partition, an external anchor, and delivery to write-once storage.
*Move on when* a deliberately edited entry is detected by a scheduled verifier.

**9. Automate expiry everywhere** — lifecycle rules on buckets, partition drops for logs and audit.
*Move on when* nothing in the matrix depends on someone remembering to delete it.

**10. Consider crypto-shredding** only if some immutable store still outlives your erasure window after all of the above.

## Implementation

The code is Go, and it covers the two places where the design is easiest to get subtly wrong: writing an audit entry that can't be skipped or quietly altered, and making sure a restore can't bring back someone who asked to be forgotten.

### An audit entry in the same transaction as the action

```go
// Reading an identity document: the audit entry and the read commit together,
// or neither happens. For sensitive reads, no audit means no access.
func (s *KYC) ViewDocument(ctx context.Context, agent Actor, docID string, reason string) (*Document, error) {
	var doc *Document
	err := pgx.BeginFunc(ctx, s.db, func(tx pgx.Tx) error {
		d, err := s.docs.Get(ctx, tx, docID)
		if err != nil {
			return err
		}
		doc = d
		return s.audit.Append(ctx, tx, Entry{
			Actor:     agent.ID,
			ActingAs:  agent.ImpersonatingID, // empty unless impersonating; never omitted
			Action:    "kyc_document.view",
			SubjectID: d.OwnerID,             // an ID — never a name or an email
			Target:    d.ID,
			Outcome:   "allowed",
			Reason:    reason,                // ticket reference, required for this action
		})
	})
	return doc, err
}
```

> **The dual write this avoids.** The tempting version reads the document, returns it, and then posts an event to an audit service. If the process dies after the read and before the post, an agent viewed a passport and no record exists. In an audit log, that isn't a small gap — it's the exact case the log exists to catch. Writing both in one transaction removes the gap; an outbox (17.1) removes it too, while letting a separate shipper deliver entries to write-once storage.

```go
func (a *Audit) Append(ctx context.Context, tx pgx.Tx, e Entry) error {
	// A hash chain has exactly one "previous", so appends to a chain must be
	// serialised. Chain per tenant per day, not one global chain, or this lock
	// becomes the throughput ceiling for every audited action in the system.
	chain := chainKey(e.Tenant, time.Now().UTC())
	if _, err := tx.Exec(ctx, `SELECT pg_advisory_xact_lock(hashtext($1))`, chain); err != nil {
		return err
	}

	prev, err := a.lastHash(ctx, tx, chain) // genesis value for a new chain
	if err != nil {
		return err
	}
	e.Chain, e.At, e.Prev = chain, time.Now().UTC(), prev
	e.Hash = sha256.Sum256(canonical(e)) // every field plus prev, in a fixed encoding

	// This role has INSERT on audit_log and nothing else. The application's
	// normal role cannot UPDATE or DELETE here, and neither can its operators.
	_, err = tx.Exec(ctx, insertAuditSQL, e.Values()...)
	return err
}
```

> **`canonical(e)` is where hand-rolled chains usually break.** Hashing a JSON encoding whose field order or number formatting can change between library versions produces a chain that fails verification after an upgrade, with no tampering involved. Use a fixed, versioned encoding and include the version in the entry.

### A restore that can't resurrect anyone

```go
// Runs as part of every restore — full, point-in-time, or side restore — before
// the restored data reaches anything live. The ledger lives outside the backups
// and holds only internal IDs and dates.
func ReapplyErasures(ctx context.Context, restored pgx.Tx, ledger Ledger, restorePoint time.Time) error {
	ids, err := ledger.ErasedSince(ctx, restorePoint)
	if err != nil {
		return fmt.Errorf("erasure ledger unavailable, refusing to release restore: %w", err)
	}
	for _, id := range ids {
		if err := eraseSubject(ctx, restored, id); err != nil { // same code as the live path
			return fmt.Errorf("re-erase subject %d: %w", id, err)
		}
	}
	return nil
}
```

> **Why the error message says "refusing."** If the ledger can't be read, the restore must stop rather than proceed without it — otherwise a ledger outage becomes a quiet re-publication of everyone who asked to be forgotten. This is the rare place where failing closed is correct, and it's the opposite of the routing-mode default in 24.2b for exactly that reason: here, the unsafe outcome is serving data, not refusing it.

And the one line to add to the restore drill from 24.1a, so that this stays true as the code changes:

```go
// After restore and re-erasure: nobody on the ledger may be anything but erased.
var resurrected int
db.QueryRow(ctx, `SELECT count(*) FROM users WHERE id = ANY($1) AND status <> 'erased'`,
	ledgerIDs).Scan(&resurrected)
if resurrected > 0 {
	return fmt.Errorf("restore resurrected %d erased subjects", resurrected)
}
```

## Further Reading

- **The GDPR text itself**, specifically Article 5(1)(e) on storage limitation, Article 17 on erasure and its exceptions in 17(3), and Article 30 on records of processing. Short, readable, and more precise than most summaries of it.
- **UK ICO guidance on the right to erasure**, particularly its treatment of backups and the "beyond use" standard. One of the few regulator documents that addresses the engineering problem directly.
- **Mathias Verraes, "Eventsourcing Patterns: Forgettable Payloads" and "Crypto-Shredding."** Short, practical, and the clearest treatment of making immutable logs compatible with erasure.
- **Scott Crosby and Dan Wallach, "Efficient Data Structures for Tamper-Evident Logging"** (USENIX Security 2009), for the theory behind hash-chained and tree-based logs and what they can and can't prove.
- **RFC 6962, Certificate Transparency.** The largest real deployment of tamper-evident, externally verifiable logging, and a good mental model for anchoring.
- **OWASP Logging Cheat Sheet**, for what to record in security logs and — equally useful — what never to put in any log.
- **NIST SP 800-92, Guide to Computer Security Log Management**, for retention, protection and review practices around audit data.
- **In this collection:** 24.1a (immutable backups and the drill this extends), 24.1b (the store inventory, and why quarantine instincts must yield here), 19.1 (keeping personal data out of logs), 17.1 (the outbox that makes audit writes reliable), Module 10 and 37.4 (sagas for multi-store erasure), 26.3 (admin audit and impersonation), 4.3 (authorisation, which decides who can read what the audit log records), 5.3 (tenant deletion and retention at the tenant level), Module 34 (the legal framework behind every number in the matrix), Module 37 (event logs, where crypto-shredding is the standard answer).

A **Java** sibling would be worth writing on the audit write specifically, because Spring makes it both easier and easier to get wrong. `@TransactionalEventListener` lets you hook audit entries into the business transaction cleanly — and its default phase is `AFTER_COMMIT`, which quietly reintroduces the exact dual write this article's Go version avoids by construction. Showing `BEFORE_COMMIT` versus the default, and what each means when the process dies at the wrong moment, is a framework-level trap that no Go code can illustrate. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this, and from the module: **every copy you make to recover is a copy you must also be able to forget.** When you add a replica, a backup, a cache, a log or a third-party sync, ask the resilience question from 24.1 and the erasure question from here in the same breath — where does this data now live, and how would I remove one person from it?
