# There Is No Root Cause: Incident Reviews, Contributing Factors, and Why MTTR Lies

_Module 24.3b — Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a customer-support ticketing platform. In January, a schema migration takes an exclusive lock on the tickets table at 14:00 on a Tuesday, and every customer's inbox is unavailable for 26 minutes.

The incident itself goes reasonably well — someone declares it, a commander runs it, the migration is cancelled. Then comes the part that decides whether it happens again.

**The review is scheduled for the 29th**, eighteen days later, because the calendar was full. By then the Slack channel has scrolled, the high-resolution metrics have aged out of retention, and three participants are reconstructing from memory.

**The template has a field called "Root Cause."** Someone types: *Engineer ran a migration during business hours without reviewing its locking behaviour.* The meeting becomes, in effect, a hearing. The engineer explains, a little defensively. Everyone else gets quieter. Nobody mentions that the migration tool printed no warning, that staging has 400 rows where production has 40 million, that the deploy checklist has 22 items and no one reads item 14, or that this engineer was covering for a colleague on leave and had never run a production migration before. Once "human error" is in the root cause box, the investigation is finished.

**Nine action items come out**, all assigned to "the platform team," none with dates. One is *add more monitoring*. Another is *be more careful with migrations*. Six months later, two are done.

**The metric reported upward is MTTR**, and it improves that quarter — because a flood of trivial Sev3s pulled the average down while the serious incidents got longer.

**In March, it happens again.** Different engineer, different table, same 26 minutes.

The outage cost 26 minutes. The review cost the organisation the chance to not have a second one, which is the more expensive loss and the invisible one.

## What It Is

An incident review — postmortem, learning review, correction-of-errors document, whichever name your organisation uses — is a structured investigation whose product is a change to the system. Not a record that something happened. Not an explanation for management. A change.

Three ideas carry nearly all of the value, and each of them contradicts an intuition.

**Blameless.** Not a kindness. A technique. The assumption is that everyone acted reasonably given the information available to them at the time, and you hold that assumption because people who fear consequences withhold exactly the information you need. If the conclusion is "he should have been more careful," you have learned nothing that changes anything, and the same conditions are still in place for the next person.

**There is no root cause.** Complex systems fail through combinations of conditions, none of which is sufficient alone. "Root cause" is a single-cause story imposed after the fact, and it almost always stops at the most human-shaped link in the chain, because that's the one that looks like a decision. The useful output is a set of **contributing factors**, several of which you could have broken independently.

**Hindsight is not evidence.** After the fact, the signal is obvious: the graph was right there, the warning was in the log. At the time it was one of forty graphs and one of nine thousand log lines. Any sentence in a review that begins "if only they had" describes a world that didn't happen and teaches nothing. The productive question is the reverse one: *what made this look like the right action at the time?*

A short disambiguation, since four neighbouring practices get mixed up:

- A **sprint retrospective** (28.2) reviews a period of work and its process. An incident review investigates one event in a system.
- **Root cause analysis** as practised in ITIL and many compliance regimes seeks a single cause and a single corrective action. It's what a regulator may ask for, and you can produce it as an export of a real review — but not as a substitute for one.
- **Five whys** is RCA with a friendly face. It produces a single chain, and which chain you get depends entirely on which "why" you asked first. Two facilitators running five whys on the same incident reliably reach different conclusions, which tells you what kind of instrument it is.
- **Near-miss review** studies something that almost happened. Cheaper than a real incident and often the same lessons.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Your template has one field called "root cause."
- Reviews are scheduled more than a few days out, or get bumped when something urgent appears.
- The same class of incident has occurred more than once.
- Action items have no named owner, no date, or read as *add monitoring*, *update the docs*, *be careful*.
- The room gets quieter when the person who ran the command starts talking.
- Only the team involved attends, and nobody outside reads the document.
- You can't find last quarter's reviews, or they live in a folder no one opens.
- Your incident metrics are improving and your incidents aren't.
- No review has ever said what went *right*, or examined something that almost went wrong.
- The most recent review's outcome was a new item on a checklist.

## How It Works

### Timing, and which incidents get one

Within three to five working days. Not because of urgency, but because of evidence: memories degrade fast, chat channels scroll, and high-resolution metrics often expire in 15 days. A review held three weeks later is investigating people's reconstructions rather than the event.

Reviewing everything means reviewing nothing, so set a trigger: every Sev1; Sev2 when the failure mode is new; anything that genuinely surprised someone, whatever its impact; and near misses that somebody noticed. A recurrence of a known failure with a known fix needs a paragraph, not a facilitation.

### The timeline is most of the work

Build it from artifacts, not memory — the incident channel, deploy history, alert records, dashboards. If 24.3a's coordination happened in one visible place, this is nearly free.

What turns a timeline from a machine log into an investigation is a second column: **what people believed at each moment.**

```
14:02  migration starts                          (deploy log)
14:04  p99 latency crosses 2s                    (metrics)
14:07  first customer ticket                     (support)
14:09  on-call engaged                           believed: a traffic spike —
                                                 the only dashboard they had
                                                 open shows request volume
14:18  scaled the API tier +6 instances          believed: this would relieve it
14:24  noticed the migration in the deploy log   believed: now the DB, not traffic
14:26  cancelled the migration
14:28  recovery
```

Ten minutes there were spent scaling a tier that was never the constraint. That's not carelessness; it's what the available information pointed at. The belief column is what makes the fix obvious — the on-call's default view didn't show database locks, and the deploy log wasn't correlated with anything. Neither of those would appear in a timeline of system events alone.

### Blamelessness, operationally

The principle is easy to endorse and easy to violate accidentally. Concretely:

- **Write actions with their information context.** Not "scaled the API tier unnecessarily," but "scaled the API tier, based on the request-volume dashboard, which was the view surfaced by the alert."
- **Ban counterfactuals from the document.** "Should have," "could have," "failed to" — each of these smuggles in a judgment about a world that didn't exist.
- **The facilitator is someone who wasn't involved**, and is not anyone's manager. This does more than any wording guideline.
- **Names or roles is a real debate.** Names help clarity and, in a genuinely blameless culture, cost nothing; roles ("the on-call engineer") reduce the chance of the document being read badly by an audience that isn't in that culture. Pick deliberately and be consistent.

And the honest boundary: blameless is about error, not about everything. Deliberate negligence or malice is a different conversation, handled elsewhere, by different people. That case is rare enough that treating every incident as though it might be one is how you get a team that tells you nothing.

### Contributing factors, in five buckets

Rather than one cause, sort what you find:

1. **Trigger** — what started it. Often the least interesting part.
2. **Conditions** — what made the trigger dangerous rather than harmless.
3. **Detection factors** — what made it take that long to know.
4. **Response factors** — what made it take that long to stop.
5. **What limited the damage** — the defences that worked.

For the January migration:

```
trigger:     migration with an exclusive lock, run at peak

conditions:  ├ no statement/lock timeout configured by default
             ├ staging has 400 rows; production has 40 million
             ├ migration tool prints no warning about lock class
             └ first-time operator, no second pair of eyes required

detection:   ├ latency alert threshold set above the failure's latency
             └ alert routed to a channel with ~40 messages/day

response:    ├ default dashboard shows traffic, not locks
             └ no runbook for "cancel an in-flight migration"

what worked: read replicas kept search and reporting alive, so the
             blast radius was the inbox rather than the whole product
```

Now look at the shape of it. There are at least six independent places the chain could have been broken, and the cheapest of them — a default `lock_timeout`, one line of configuration — has nothing to do with the trigger and would have capped the outage at seconds. The most expensive one, "make staging data-realistic," is a quarter of work. A single-cause narrative would have picked the person at the start of the chain and missed both.

The fifth bucket isn't decoration. Naming the defences that worked tells you which ones to protect the next time someone proposes removing them for cost.

### Action items that survive contact with next week

- **One named person, not a team.** A team is nobody.
- **A date**, and it goes into the normal backlog at a normal priority, tracked where all other work is tracked. A separate postmortem-action tracker is a place items go to be forgotten politely.
- **Three to five, maximum.** Nine items is a declaration that none of them is important. Rank the contributing factors by cost-to-fix against impact-if-fixed and take the top few.
- **Classify each as prevent, detect, or mitigate.** Most teams write only *prevent* items, which are the most expensive and the least reliable — you cannot prevent the next unknown failure, but you can reliably shrink how long it takes to notice and how much it costs while it lasts. A review whose items are all preventive is usually a review that stopped at the trigger.
- **Write down what you decided *not* to do, and why.** It stops the same debate recurring, and it's honest about the trade you made.
- **Start the next review with the previous review's items.** If they aren't done, that's the first topic — and if it happens repeatedly, the problem isn't the review process, it's capacity allocation, and it needs a manager rather than a better template.

Banned phrasings, because they're unfinishable: *add more monitoring* (alert on what, at what threshold, paging whom?), *be more careful*, *improve documentation*, *train the team*.

### The payoff is in aggregate

A single review changes one system. Twenty reviews, read together, tell you something no individual review can: that eleven of them involved a configuration change with no second reviewer, or that seven had detection times over twenty minutes for the same reason.

That requires two boring things — a consistent tag vocabulary for contributing factors, and someone who actually reads them quarterly. It's the highest-return activity in this article and the one most likely to go unowned.

Distribution matters for the same reason. A review read only by the team that lived it teaches only them. Put a plain-language paragraph at the top that a support agent or an account manager can follow, and share it widely; the practice of reading other teams' incidents is one of the cheaper ways engineers learn how the system actually behaves.

### Metrics, and why MTTR is the wrong one

Mean time to recovery is the metric everyone reaches for, and it misleads in at least four ways:

- **The distribution is badly skewed.** Incident durations are roughly lognormal: many short ones and a few very long ones. The mean is dominated by the tail and is not a description of a typical incident. The VOID reports, which collected thousands of public incident records, make this argument with the data — the variance is so high that an MTTR figure over a normal quarter's incident count carries almost no signal.
- **It rewards the wrong behaviours.** You improve MTTR by declaring more trivial incidents, by closing early, or by not declaring the hard ones at all. Each of those directly attacks 24.3a's low declaration bar.
- **It mixes three different problems.** Time to detect, time to engage, and time to mitigate have different causes and different fixes. The aggregate hides which one is bad.
- **It measures duration, not harm.** Twenty-six minutes of unavailable inboxes at 14:00 on a Tuesday is not the same event as twenty-six minutes at 03:00 on a Sunday, and one number treats them identically.

Better instruments, in rough order of usefulness:

- **Customer-visible impact against an SLO** — error budget burned, which is denominated in the thing you actually care about (Module 19).
- **Time to detect**, tracked separately and on its own. It's the one number that is nearly always improvable and nearly always worse than people think.
- **Action-item completion rate.** A direct measurement of whether reviews are doing anything.
- **Recurrence of tagged contributing factors.** The only metric that tells you whether you're learning.
- **Near-miss reports**, which should *increase* as the culture improves. A number going up as a good sign is a useful thing to have on a dashboard, because it inoculates everyone against reading the others naively.

One rule on all of them: **incident metrics are for spotting trends in your process, never for evaluating individuals or teams.** The day MTTR appears on a performance review, people stop declaring incidents, and every technique in these two articles stops working.

### Study the near misses too

Most days, the same system, the same people and the same pressures produce success. Failure isn't a separate mode; it's the same activity with a different outcome. That's why a near miss — the migration that was caught in review, the deploy rolled back before customers noticed — carries almost the same information at a fraction of the cost, and why "what went right" belongs in every review.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A full facilitated review for every Sev1**, for novel Sev2s, for anything that crossed team boundaries, and for repeats — repeats especially, since a recurrence means the previous review was wrong about something.
- **A written-only review** for a known failure with a known fix: a few paragraphs, one action item, no meeting.
- **A near-miss review** when someone noticed a close call and says so. Reward the saying.
- **A formal RCA document** when a contract or regulator requires one — produced from the real review, with the caveats about discoverability below.
- **An aggregate read** once you have ten or so reviews, then quarterly.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A review for every Sev3.** You'll burn the practice out and stop doing the ones that matter.
- **Four pages for a twelve-minute incident with an obvious, single fix.** A paragraph and one item is a complete review.
- **A meeting when the document suffices** — and the reverse: for anything contentious or cross-team, the document alone is not enough, because the disagreements are exactly what the conversation surfaces.
- **A parallel tracker for postmortem actions.** Use the backlog everyone already looks at.
- **Reviewing before the incident is actually over**, which produces a timeline you'll have to rewrite.

## Trade-offs

- **A good review costs real time** — several hours of a facilitator's work plus a meeting with six people. Done badly it's worse than nothing, because the organisation now believes it has learned something.
- **A blameless culture is fragile and slow to rebuild.** One incident where someone is visibly punished undoes a year of it, and people remember far longer than managers expect.
- **Blamelessness can drift into nobody-is-accountable** for the fixes. The counterweight isn't blame, it's named owners, dates, and the standing agenda item about last time's actions.
- **Written records are discoverable.** In regulated or litigious contexts, an incident document can end up somewhere you didn't intend. The answer is to write factually and avoid speculation about legal significance — not to stop writing. For security and data-breach incidents, bring legal in early (Module 34).
- **Action items compete with roadmap work.** If they're always outranked, the review is theatre, and the honest fix is a standing capacity allocation rather than a stricter template.
- **Preventive fixes add complexity.** Every new check, approval gate and alert has a cost on every deploy from now on, and each is a candidate to become someone's future incident. "Add a required review step" is not free.
- **Aggregate analysis needs consistent tagging**, which is tedious work with a delayed payoff and no natural owner.

## Alternatives

- **An informal "what did we learn" in the next standup.** Adequate for small teams and low severities. It leaves no record and no aggregate, so it stops scaling exactly when you'd want it.
- **Formal RCA with a single corrective action.** Satisfies auditors, and its single-cause framing loses most of the information. If you need one, write the real review and derive the RCA from it.
- **Fix the obvious thing and move on.** Sometimes correct — a known cause, a cheap fix, no cross-team involvement. The risk is that "obvious" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and repeats are the signal that it was wrong.
- **Chaos engineering and game days.** Find the contributing factors before they combine, rather than after. A complement, not a substitute: they test the failures you thought of.
- **SLO- and error-budget-driven prioritisation.** Instead of arguing about per-incident action items, let budget consumption decide how much of the next cycle is reliability work. A genuinely different model, and a strong one, because it converts a recurring political argument into an agreed rule. It needs meaningful SLOs first (Module 19).

## How to Achieve It

**1. Delete the root cause field from your template** and replace it with the five buckets.
*Move on when* the next review produces more than one contributing factor.

**2. Write down the trigger**: which severities get a review, and within how many days.
*Move on when* a review happens without anyone having to decide whether it should.

**3. Appoint a facilitator who wasn't involved**, and who isn't anyone's manager.
*Move on when* that's the default rather than an exception.

**4. Build the timeline from artifacts, with a "what we believed" column.**
*Move on when* a reader who wasn't there can explain why each action made sense at the time.

**5. Cap action items at five**, each with a person, a date, and a prevent/detect/mitigate label, in the normal backlog.
*Move on when* one review's items are all genuinely closed.

**6. Put last review's items first on the next agenda.**
*Move on when* that conversation becomes uneventful.

**7. Publish a one-paragraph plain summary and circulate it beyond the team.**
*Move on when* someone from another team references your incident in theirs.

**8. Tag contributing factors, and read them all once a quarter.**
*Move on when* an aggregate read produces a fix that no single review suggested.

**9. Replace MTTR in any reporting** with error-budget burn, time-to-detect and action-item completion.
*Move on when* nobody is optimising a number by declaring fewer incidents.

## Implementation

The documents are the deliverable here, so the only code worth writing is the code that removes friction from producing them. It's TypeScript, continuing from the incident state in 24.3a, and there are two pieces: drafting the timeline automatically, and making aggregate analysis possible at all.

### Draft the timeline, leave the thinking blank

```ts
// A review that starts from a blank page gets written three weeks late.
// This assembles everything mechanical so the facilitator starts from evidence.
async function draftReview(incidentId: string): Promise<string> {
  const inc = await loadIncident(incidentId);
  const window = { from: addMinutes(inc.startedAt, -60), to: inc.resolvedAt! };

  const events = [
    ...inc.timeline.map(e => ({ at: e.at, source: "incident", text: `${e.who}: ${e.text}` })),
    ...(await deploys.between(window)).map(d => ({ at: d.at, source: "deploy", text: d.description })),
    ...(await alerts.between(window)).map(a => ({ at: a.at, source: "alert", text: `${a.name} → ${a.channel}` })),
    ...(await configChanges.between(window)).map(c => ({ at: c.at, source: "config", text: c.summary })),
  ].sort((a, b) => +a.at - +b.at);

  return renderTemplate({
    incident: inc,
    // Three columns: time, what happened, and one left empty on purpose.
    rows: events.map(e => ({ ...e, believed: "" })),
    factors: { trigger: [], conditions: [], detection: [], response: [], worked: [] },
  });
}
```

> **The empty column is the point.** "What we believed at the time" cannot be recovered from logs — it comes from asking the people involved, in a conversation where they're not afraid to answer. Every attempt to infer it from telemetry produces a plausible reconstruction that flatters hindsight. Automate the evidence; do not automate the investigation.

> Pulling deploys, config changes and alert routing into the same timeline is what made the January review's real factors visible: the migration and the alert-to-noisy-channel appear side by side, which is exactly the correlation a human staring at one dashboard won't make.

### Tags, so twenty reviews can say something

```ts
// A closed vocabulary, deliberately small. A free-text tag field produces
// fourteen spellings of "config change" and no aggregate at all.
type Factor =
  | "unreviewed-config-change" | "schema-migration" | "capacity-exhaustion"
  | "dependency-failure"       | "expired-credential" | "alert-threshold"
  | "alert-routing"            | "staging-prod-divergence" | "missing-runbook"
  | "insufficient-isolation"   | "deploy-regression" | "first-time-operator";

type ReviewRecord = {
  incidentId: string;
  severity: 1 | 2 | 3;
  detectedAfterMinutes: number;      // tracked alone, never averaged into MTTR
  mitigatedAfterMinutes: number;
  factors: { factor: Factor; bucket: "condition" | "detection" | "response" }[];
  actions: { owner: string; due: Date; class: "prevent" | "detect" | "mitigate"; done: boolean }[];
};

// The quarterly read, which is where the money is.
function recurring(records: ReviewRecord[]) {
  return Object.entries(
    records.flatMap(r => r.factors.map(f => f.factor))
           .reduce<Record<string, number>>((acc, f) => ({ ...acc, [f]: (acc[f] ?? 0) + 1 }), {}))
    .sort(([, a], [, b]) => b - a);
}
```

> **Why `detectedAfterMinutes` is stored separately and never folded into an average.** Keeping detection, mitigation and severity as distinct fields is what lets you ask "which of our incidents took over twenty minutes to detect, and what did they have in common" — a question with an actionable answer. A single MTTR column can only be watched going up and down.

## Further Reading

- **Richard Cook, "How Complex Systems Fail."** Eighteen numbered observations across four pages, including the flat statement that there is no root cause. If you read one thing on this subject, read this one.
- **John Allspaw, "Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture"** (Etsy, 2012). The essay that moved this practice into mainstream software engineering, and still the clearest argument for *why* blamelessness is instrumental rather than sentimental.
- **Sidney Dekker, _The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error'_.** The theory underneath: hindsight bias, the "second story," and why "human error" is the beginning of an investigation rather than its conclusion.
- **Google, _Site Reliability Engineering_, chapter 15 ("Postmortem Culture"), and the _SRE Workbook_'s postmortem chapter**, which includes full example documents — useful for calibrating how much detail is enough.
- **The Etsy Debriefing Facilitation Guide** (Allspaw, Evans, Schauenberg). Free, short, and specifically about running the meeting, which most material skips.
- **The VOID reports (Verica Open Incident Database), Courtney Nash.** The empirical case against MTTR as a metric, drawn from thousands of public incident reports, plus a large corpus of real postmortems to read.
- **"Howie: The Post-Incident Guide"** (Jeli). A practical, step-by-step treatment of interviewing participants and assembling a timeline.
- **In this collection:** 24.3a (the response whose artifacts this depends on), Module 19 (SLOs, error budgets and alert quality — the alternative prioritisation model and several of the better metrics), 28.2 (sprint retrospectives, a different practice with a similar name), 30.2 (rollbacks and feature flags, which turn many contributing factors into non-events), 31.3 (technical debt, where unglamorous action items go to compete), 32.4 (disagreement and escalation, since reviews surface both), Module 34 (regulatory and legal constraints on what you write down).

---

The habit to take from this: **when you find yourself satisfied with an explanation, ask what else had to be true.** The trigger is rarely the cheapest place to intervene, and the fix that would have capped the January outage at four seconds was a one-line default nobody was looking for, six links away from the person everyone was looking at.
