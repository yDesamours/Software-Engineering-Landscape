# Backups You Haven't Restored: RPO, RTO, and Point-in-Time Recovery

_Module 24.1a — Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a scheduling product for dental clinics. Six hundred clinics use it, the database is one Postgres instance of about 90 GB, and receptionists type in roughly 40,000 appointments a day from phone calls. None of that data exists anywhere else — not in an email, not in a partner's system, not in a file the customer uploaded. If a row is gone, the information is gone.

At 15:40 on a Tuesday, an engineer clearing out old cancelled appointments runs a `DELETE` in a production console. The statement was meant to end with `WHERE clinic_id = 412 AND status = 'cancelled' AND created_at < '2024-01-01'`. A bad paste ends it after the first condition. 1.2 million rows disappear, including every future booking for every clinic. The phones start ringing in four minutes.

There is a backup. `pg_dump` runs at 02:00 nightly and uploads to a bucket in another region. Three things go wrong from there.

**The backup is 13 hours and 40 minutes old.** Even in the best case, restoring it throws away every appointment booked, moved or cancelled since 02:00 — about 22,000 of them, which now exist only in the deleted rows and in people's memories.

**Nobody knows how long a restore takes.** The dump is 11 GB compressed. Loading it takes 5 hours 40 minutes, and most of that is rebuilding indexes on one table. The dump is plain SQL piped through `gzip`, a format that restores as a single stream on a single core; nothing about the format allows parallelism, and nobody had ever needed to find that out.

**The most recent dump is 11 days old.** The cron line is `0 2 * * * pg_dump ... | gzip > /backups/db.sql.gz && aws s3 cp ...`. The volume filled up on the 7th. `pg_dump` has exited non-zero every night since, cron has mailed the error to a mailbox nobody reads, and the `&&` has quietly skipped the upload. The monitoring dashboard shows "Backups: OK" because it checks that the bucket exists and has objects in it.

So the real recovery is: restore an 11-day-old database, six hours later, and tell 600 clinics that a week and a half of their work is gone.

```
02:00  nightly dump — failing since the 7th
   ✗  ✗  ✗  ✗  ✗  ✗  ✗  ✗  ✗  ✗  ✗ ─────────────▶ 15:40 bad DELETE
   │                                                    │
   └──────────── last good copy: 11 days ───────────────┘
                                        restore finishes ▼ 21:20
```

Notice what none of these failures is about. The job existed. The file format was valid. The bucket was in a different region. Every failure is on the other side of the operation — how much you lose, how long it takes, and whether the copy is real. Backing up is the easy half, and it's the half that gets the attention because it's the half that runs every night.

## What It Is

Two numbers describe what you actually need, and nearly every decision in this module trades one against the other or against money.

**RPO — Recovery Point Objective:** how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time. A nightly dump and nothing else means an RPO of up to 24 hours. Continuous archiving of the database's log can bring it under a minute.

**RTO — Recovery Time Objective:** how long you can afford to be down before you're serving correct data again. In the story above, the target was probably "an hour or two" in everyone's head, and the real figure was six hours plus.

Both come in two versions, and confusing them is where teams get hurt: the **declared** number, which lives in a document, and the **measured** number, which is what happened the last time someone actually did it. Only one of them is true.

One more distinction, because it's the most common and most expensive mix-up in this area. **A replica is not a backup.** A replica exists to keep serving when a machine dies; it does that by applying everything the primary does, as fast as it can, including your `DELETE`. It's a faithful copy of your mistake within a second. Snapshots, dumps and archived logs are backups because they hold a *past* state. Replication holds the *current* one.

The same line separates two whole disciplines. **High availability** is about surviving infrastructure failure with nothing to decide — a machine dies, another takes over, you find out from a graph (24.2). **Disaster recovery** starts when the data itself is wrong and someone has to choose which past to go back to. A multi-AZ database with automatic failover, which many teams reasonably describe as "we're covered," offers exactly nothing against that Tuesday `DELETE`.

## Recognizing the Problem

- You can't state your RPO and RTO as numbers, or you can, but nobody has ever measured them.
- Nobody has restored a backup into a working system in the last quarter — or ever.
- Your backup alerting fires when the job fails, but not when the job stops running at all.
- Backups live under the same cloud account and the same credentials as the thing they protect.
- The only "backup" is a read replica, a RAID array, or a managed service's built-in redundancy.
- You know how to restore the database, but not the uploaded files, the secrets, or the search index.
- The restore procedure exists as one person's knowledge, and that person is on holiday.
- Restores are all-or-nothing: there's no way to get one customer, or one table, back without rolling everything back.
- Retention is "keep everything" or "seven days," and nobody can say why that number.
- You have never deliberately destroyed something to see whether you could get it back.

## How It Works

### Three different things people call a backup

**A logical dump** (`pg_dump`, `mysqldump`) writes out the contents — schema plus rows, as statements or a structured archive. It's portable across versions and platforms, it's the only form that easily lets you restore one table, and it's slow to load because the target database has to re-insert every row and rebuild every index from scratch. The load, not the dump, is the expensive half.

**A physical backup** (`pg_basebackup`, a cloud block-storage snapshot) copies the data files byte for byte. It's fast in both directions, it's exact, and it's tied to the same database version and often the same platform. A snapshot of a running database is *crash-consistent*: it looks exactly like the machine lost power at that instant, which a database with a write-ahead log recovers from routinely. That's fine — until the data files and the log live on two different volumes, because two snapshots taken "at the same time" are not taken at the same instant, and the pair can be unrecoverable.

**Continuous archiving** is a base backup plus every change since. This is the one that changes what's possible, so it's worth understanding rather than just enabling.

### The write-ahead log, and why it collapses RPO

Before a database changes a page of data, it writes a description of the change to a sequential log and makes sure that write has hit the disk. This is the write-ahead log — WAL in Postgres, the binary log in MySQL, the redo log in InnoDB and Oracle. It exists so the database can survive a crash: on restart it replays the log and finishes whatever was in flight.

That log is also a complete record of everything that happened, in order. If you keep copying finished log segments off to durable storage as they're produced, then a base backup plus the log stream is a recording you can replay to any moment you like.

```
base backup                 archived WAL segments
    ●───────────┬────┬────┬────┬────┬────┬────┬────┬────▶
 Sun 02:00      │    │    │    │    │    │    │    │
                                    ▲
                         replay stops at 15:39:58,
                         two seconds before the DELETE
```

That is **point-in-time recovery**. Two consequences matter more than the mechanism.

First, RPO stops being "since the last backup" and becomes "since the last archived log segment" — typically seconds to a couple of minutes, depending on write volume and how aggressively you flush partial segments. In the clinic scenario, that's the difference between losing 22,000 appointments and losing about twelve.

Second, you can restore to *just before* the damage rather than to last night. That's a qualitatively different capability: it's what lets a bad migration or a bad `DELETE` cost minutes instead of a day, and it's the reason PITR is usually the single highest-value thing on this list.

It isn't free of judgment, either. Replaying to 15:39:58 also un-does every *good* thing that happened between 15:39:58 and now. That decision — how far back, and what gets thrown away with it — is the hard part of a real restore, and it's why the choice usually needs someone with authority, not just someone with credentials (24.3).

### What RTO is actually made of

People plan RTO as though it were the restore command's runtime. Here's a realistic six-hour recovery, broken up:

```
detect   decide      provision  transfer  load      reindex    verify  cut over
  4m  │    25m    │     10m   │   18m  │  2h10m  │   3h30m  │   20m  │   5m
```

The restore command occupies the middle. Everything around it is where the time actually goes, and where it's reducible:

- **Decide** is human latency: who is allowed to say "restore production to 15:39," and how fast can you reach them at 03:00? Deciding that in advance is free and saves the most minutes of anything on this list.
- **Reindex** dominates logical restores and is absent from physical ones. This one fact is usually the whole argument for snapshot- or base-backup-based recovery once a database passes a few tens of gigabytes.
- **Transfer** is where cross-region storage quietly shows its price. 11 GB from a distant bucket is fine; 900 GB is a different conversation, and it's the reason a local copy plus a remote copy beats a remote copy alone.
- **Verify** is not optional padding. Coming back up on a database that's missing the last four hours *without anyone noticing* is worse than staying down.

### Proving the copy is real

There are three levels of confidence, and only the third one is evidence:

1. **The job exited zero.** This tells you a process ran. The clinic scenario's job didn't even clear this bar, and its dashboard still said OK.
2. **The artifact is readable.** Checksums verify, the archive lists its contents, the file size is in the expected range. This catches truncation and corruption, and it's cheap enough to do on every backup.
3. **A restore came up and answered questions correctly.** Row counts in the right range, the newest record close to the target time, a few known values present, the application's own health checks passing against it.

Only the third one measures RTO, and measuring RTO is half the point of doing it.

There's a monitoring detail here that's easy to get wrong and specific enough to be worth stating. Alerting on job *failure* doesn't catch the most common real-world outcome, which is the job silently not running any more — the cron entry removed, the instance replaced, the credentials expired, the disk full. The check has to be phrased as an absence:

```
time() - backup_last_success_timestamp_seconds > 36 * 3600
```

This is a dead man's switch: something must keep proving it's alive, and silence is the alarm. Apply the same shape to the drill (19.1).

### Keeping the copy out of the blast radius

A backup has to survive the thing it protects you against, and the list of things has grown. Hardware failure and human error were always on it; deliberate destruction now is too. Ransomware operators look for backups first, because a victim with working backups doesn't pay. In practice, that means asking what a single compromised production credential can reach.

The old **3-2-1 rule** — three copies, on two kinds of media, one off-site — comes from photography, predates the cloud, and still holds up if you translate "media" and "off-site" into failure domains:

- A copy in the same account, same region: fast to restore from. Covers the usual case.
- A copy in another region or another account: covers a regional outage and an account-level mistake.
- A copy the production role cannot delete or overwrite: object-lock or write-once storage, a separate credential for the retention policy. This is the one that covers malice, and the one most setups don't have.

The test to apply is concrete: *if an attacker had our deploy credentials for an hour, what would still exist tomorrow?*

### Beyond the database

The database is where this article stays, but a restore that only restores Postgres restores a system that doesn't work. Uploaded files, secrets, search indexes, queue contents, infrastructure definitions and the schema version matching the data are all part of it, and restoring one store to a past moment while its neighbours stay in the present creates its own class of bug. That's 24.1b.

## When It's Appropriate

Backups themselves aren't a judgment call; the level of investment is. Roughly, in order of cost:

**Automated, monitored, restore-tested backups: everything.** Including the internal tool with 30 users. The cost is an afternoon.

**Point-in-time recovery when the data is typed in by humans and can't be re-derived.** The clinic system writes about 200 rows a minute of information that exists nowhere else; a 24-hour RPO means 288,000 rows of unrecoverable loss, and PITR takes it to roughly 200. Compare an ad-impression pipeline replaying from a Kafka topic with seven days' retention, where the events can simply be reprocessed and PITR on the serving database buys much less.

**Cross-region copies when a regional failure is a real scenario you'd act on**, or when a compliance regime asks for it. Note this is about the *copies*, not about running in two regions — a far cheaper thing than 24.2b's multi-region architecture, and a good first step.

**Immutable copies when a compromised credential is in your threat model**, which for anything internet-facing with paying customers it is.

**Per-tenant restore when you're B2B and multi-tenant.** The realistic incident isn't "the database is gone," it's "customer 412 deleted their workspace and wants it back, and the other 599 customers must not notice anything." All-or-nothing restore can't serve that, and it's the request you'll actually get (24.1b).

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Derived data.** Search indexes, thumbnails, materialized views, caches: back up the recipe, not the output. The exception is when rebuilding takes longer than your RTO — a 40-hour reindex makes the index effectively a source of truth, and a snapshot of it becomes cheaper than the rebuild.
- **Sub-minute RPO for data the user can retype in ten seconds.** Buying five nines of durability on a form draft is a way to spend a week on nothing.
- **Hand-rolling PITR when your managed database already does it.** RDS, Cloud SQL and their equivalents ship continuous archiving as a checkbox with a retention slider. Building it yourself here is not a learning exercise, it's an on-call burden.
- **Indefinite retention "just in case."** Old copies of personal data are a liability, not an asset — every copy is in scope for a breach and for deletion obligations (24.4).
- **Fifteen-minute RTO for a system where nobody works at night.** Match the target to what the downtime costs at that hour, not to the smallest number you can imagine.

## Trade-offs

Once you have a real backup system, these are the costs you now own:

- **Money, mostly in storage and egress.** Modest for 90 GB; a serious line item when the database is measured in terabytes and you keep 35 daily copies in two regions.
- **A second attack surface holding your most sensitive data.** Backups are a complete copy of everything, often with weaker access controls than production because "it's just backups." The restore path itself is a privilege escalation route: whoever can restore production into a scratch environment can read all of it. Encrypt, scope credentials narrowly, audit restores.
- **Encryption keys become a single point of failure.** A backup you can't decrypt is noise. The key has to survive the same disaster, and it can't live only in the system being backed up.
- **The backup system can cause the outage.** This one bites people. In Postgres, if archiving fails — destination unreachable, bucket permissions changed, disk full — the server doesn't drop the segments, it keeps them. WAL accumulates on the primary's volume until the volume fills and the database stops accepting writes. A misconfigured backup taking production down is a common enough story that the archive destination deserves monitoring in its own right.
- **Retention collides with deletion obligations.** A user exercising a right to erasure is asking you to delete data that also sits in 35 nightly copies you deliberately made immutable. There are workable answers (24.4), and none of them is free.
- **Drills cost real time.** A quarterly restore drill is an engineer-day plus infrastructure, every quarter, forever, and it will always be possible to postpone it for something urgent.
- **Restores are destructive decisions made under pressure.** Replaying to 15:39:58 deletes everything after 15:39:58. Somebody has to own that call while the phones are ringing.

## Alternatives

Other ways to get data back, several of which are better first moves than improving your backups:

- **Soft delete and an undo path in the application.** The overwhelmingly common cause of data loss is a person deleting the wrong thing through the product. Marking the row deleted and offering 30 days to reverse it turns a six-hour restore into a flag flip, and it's the one intervention here that customers can perform themselves. If you do one thing from this article, this is competitive with PITR.
- **A delayed replica.** A second replica configured to apply changes an hour late (`recovery_min_apply_delay` in Postgres, `SOURCE_DELAY` in MySQL) is a warm copy of one hour ago. When someone drops a table, you have an hour to stop replay and pull the table out of a live database rather than restoring from cold storage. It costs one instance and complements PITR; it is not a substitute, because it holds exactly one moment and it dies with the region.
- **An append-only event log as the source of truth.** If state is derived by folding events, the recovery story is "replay from the log," and the log is the only thing you must protect (Module 37). Real, powerful, and a much larger commitment than adding backups to a system that already exists.
- **Managed time-travel features.** Some engines and warehouses let you query or restore a table as of a past timestamp directly. Where available, it's the fastest route to a single-table recovery.
- **Accepting the loss**, explicitly, for data that's genuinely derived or genuinely cheap to recreate. Written down, this is a legitimate answer. Unwritten, it's what you'll discover you chose.

## How to Achieve It

**1. Write down RPO and RTO per data store, as numbers, with a name attached.** One line each: "appointments: RPO 5 minutes, RTO 2 hours, agreed by the COO." The conversation is the point — the answers are usually smaller than engineers assume and larger than executives assume.
*Move on when* the numbers exist and someone outside engineering has agreed to them.

**2. Get one automated backup running with a success heartbeat.** Emit a timestamp on success; alert on that timestamp going stale. Not on job failure.
*Move on when* a backup silently stopping would page someone within a few hours.

**3. Restore it once, by hand, into a scratch environment, and time every phase.** This is the step that converts declared numbers into measured ones, and it's where the surprises are: the missing extension, the wrong Postgres minor version, the role that doesn't exist in the target, the 3-hour index rebuild.
*Move on when* you have a measured RTO written next to the target — even if it's far worse, as long as it's honest.

**4. Close the largest gap the measurement exposed.** Usually that's the restore taking too long (switch from logical dumps to base backups plus WAL, or to the managed service's snapshots), or the RPO being a full day (turn on continuous archiving).
*Move on when* measured numbers meet the targets, or the gap is documented and accepted.

**5. Put one copy out of reach.** Separate account or separate region, plus object-lock so the production role can't delete it.
*Move on when* an attacker with your deploy credentials couldn't destroy every copy.

**6. Automate the drill and make it recurring.** Restore to a randomly chosen recent timestamp — not a fixed one, which only ever exercises the base backup — verify with real queries, and record the duration as a metric you can watch drift upward as the database grows.
*Move on when* the drill has run unattended for a month and the duration graph exists.

**7. Rotate who runs the manual version.** Once a quarter, someone who didn't build it follows the runbook. Every place they get stuck is a defect in the runbook, which is the actual output of the exercise (24.3).
*Move on when* two different people have done it without help.

**8. Extend the inventory past the database** — files, secrets, indexes, infrastructure — and repeat from step 1 (24.1b).

## Implementation

The code is Go, because the interesting artifact here isn't the backup command — those are one-liners you should be using someone else's implementation of — it's the small, boring, scheduled program that proves the backup works and turns your RTO into a number on a graph. Single static binary, runs in CI or as a cron job in the recovery environment, no runtime to install on a machine you're rebuilding under pressure.

### The drill

```go
// Runs weekly. Restores to a random recent timestamp, then asks the data questions.
func drill(ctx context.Context, cfg Config) error {
	start := time.Now()

	// A random target inside the retention window exercises WAL replay.
	// A fixed target (say, "last midnight") often only exercises the base backup,
	// which is the half that rarely breaks.
	target := time.Now().Add(-time.Duration(rand.Intn(72)) * time.Hour)

	// Deliberately the same command the runbook gives a human at 03:00.
	// If the drill uses a different path, it verifies a path nobody will take.
	if err := run(ctx, "pgbackrest", "--stanza=main", "--type=time",
		"--target="+target.Format(time.RFC3339), "restore"); err != nil {
		return fmt.Errorf("restore to %s: %w", target, err)
	}
	restored := time.Now()

	if err := verify(ctx, cfg.ScratchDSN, target); err != nil {
		return err
	}

	emit("restore_duration_seconds", restored.Sub(start).Seconds())
	emit("verify_duration_seconds", time.Since(restored).Seconds())
	emit("backup_last_verified_timestamp_seconds", float64(time.Now().Unix()))
	return nil
}
```

### The verification is the part that's usually wrong

```go
func verify(ctx context.Context, dsn string, target time.Time) error {
	db, err := pgx.Connect(ctx, dsn)
	if err != nil {
		return fmt.Errorf("connect to restored db: %w", err)
	}
	defer db.Close(ctx)

	var newest time.Time
	var clinics, appts int64
	err = db.QueryRow(ctx, `
		SELECT max(created_at), count(DISTINCT clinic_id), count(*)
		FROM appointments`).Scan(&newest, &clinics, &appts)
	if err != nil {
		return fmt.Errorf("query restored db: %w", err)
	}

	// The check that catches replay stopping early: the data must reach
	// almost up to the target, not merely exist.
	if gap := target.Sub(newest); gap > 5*time.Minute {
		return fmt.Errorf("newest row is %s before target %s: replay stopped early",
			gap, target)
	}
	if clinics < 500 || appts < 1_000_000 {
		return fmt.Errorf("restored volumes implausible: %d clinics, %d appointments",
			clinics, appts)
	}
	return nil
}
```

> **Why `count(*) > 0` isn't verification.** A restore that came up on the base backup alone and replayed no log at all has plenty of rows — it's a perfectly healthy database from last Sunday. Everything that makes this check meaningful is in the comparison against `target`. The same applies to restoring the wrong stanza, or to a restore that silently used yesterday's archive because today's credentials expired: the database is fine, the data is just from the wrong moment. Freshness relative to an expected point is the only assertion that catches those.

> **Where this still falls short.** The drill restores; it doesn't cut over. Promoting a restored instance to serve production traffic involves connection strings, DNS, read-only flags and the application's own migrations, and those steps are only exercised by a human drill (step 7). Treat the automated drill as continuous proof that the data is recoverable, and the manual one as proof that *you* can recover it.

### The archiving side, briefly

Getting the log segments into durable storage is where hand-rolled setups tend to break, in ways the Postgres documentation is unusually blunt about: the command must never overwrite an existing archived segment, must return non-zero when it fails so the server retries instead of dropping data, and must ensure the write is actually durable before it reports success. `cp %p /archive/%f` satisfies none of these. And because the server retains segments it can't archive, a broken archive command eventually fills the primary's disk and stops writes.

This is the strongest argument in the whole article for using pgBackRest, Barman, WAL-G or your provider's managed equivalent rather than shell: not that the shell is hard to write, but that its failure mode is an outage plus a silently incomplete archive.

---

The habit to take from this: **a backup is a claim, and a restore is the evidence.** The only recovery numbers worth quoting are the ones you measured, and they expire — they get worse every month your data grows, quietly, until someone runs the drill again.
