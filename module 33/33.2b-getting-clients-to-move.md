# Getting Clients to Move: Migration Guides, Outreach, and Brownouts

_Module 33.2b — API Versioning & Deprecation_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a maps and geocoding API with about 9,000 active accounts: delivery apps, real-estate listings, logistics planners, and a very long tail of small websites with a store locator on them. The legacy endpoint, `GET /geocode/v1`, is backed by a data provider whose contract ends on **30 June 2027**. After that date the endpoint has no data behind it. This deadline can't slip — it isn't yours.

You've done everything in 33.2a. The replacement, `/places/search`, has been live and proven for a year. You have per-account usage with last-seen timestamps, `Deprecation` and `Sunset` headers on every response, SDK warnings, a published policy, and a technical contact for most accounts. Nine months out, 2,300 accounts still call the old endpoint. You announce.

Five months later, 1,900 remain. The burn-down has gone nearly flat. Looking at why:

**The migration guide is a reference, not a guide.** It says "use the Places API" and links to the Places reference docs. But the old endpoint returned a single best match, and the new one returns up to five ranked results. The old `accuracy` field (`rooftop`, `street`, `city`) has become a numeric `confidence` plus a `match_level` with different categories. The old `country` parameter accepted "Germany"; the new one accepts only `DE`. Every client has to discover these differences for themselves. Several who did migrate shipped worse results than before, because they took `results[0]` without checking `match_level`, and complained.

**Everyone got the same message.** Two emails in five months, identical for every account, saying that a legacy endpoint is being retired. Nothing in them told a reader whether they were affected, which of their systems used it, or what specifically they'd need to change.

**The messages went to people who couldn't act.** 640 of the remaining accounts are small shops using the same third-party WordPress store-locator plugin. The shop owners received your emails. None of them can change the plugin. One person — the plugin's author — could move all 640 accounts at once, and nobody had contacted them.

**Nothing has happened to anyone.** Everything still works. The warnings are in log files nobody reads. For most of the 1,900, the cost of staying has been zero, so they've stayed.

33.2a established who is still on the old thing. This article is about the other half: **getting them to move before the date.** Clients migrate when the cost of moving is low, when the cost of staying is felt, and when the message reaches someone who can actually do the work. The first attempt did none of the three.

## What It Is

This is the active half of the migration window — sometimes called a migration campaign. It's made of four parts:

- **A migration guide** that makes moving cheap.
- **Segmented outreach** that reaches the people who can act, with facts specific to them.
- **Escalating friction**, which makes staying progressively more noticeable — ending in **brownouts**: scheduled, announced periods during which the deprecated thing fails exactly as it will after the sunset, so that dependents find out while it's still reversible.
- **Assisted migration** for the clients where your effort goes furthest.

Two things share the name "brownout" and are worth separating. In resilience engineering, a brownout means deliberately degrading non-essential features under load to keep the core working — a technique for protecting your own service. The deprecation brownout here is a communication technique aimed at *other people's* systems. It's also different from chaos engineering, which tests your own resilience to failure; a deprecation brownout rehearses a permanent change so that clients discover their dependence on it.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The burn-down curve flattened partway through the window.
- Replies to announcements ask "what do I need to change?" or "does this affect us?"
- Many of the remaining accounts share one plugin, SDK version, or partner platform.
- Clients who migrated report that things behave differently, and the guide doesn't mention it.
- Most of the remaining accounts haven't opened a single message.
- You expect a rush in the final weeks, and support is already dreading it.
- The date can't move, and the curve says you won't make it.

## How It Works

### Why clients don't move

From a client's point of view, migrating is a cost now — engineering time, testing, a release, some risk — to avoid a cost later. Until the later cost is close and real, the rational choice is to wait, especially for anyone with a release process slower than yours. Each part of the campaign acts on one side of that trade:

```
   will they move?   ≈   felt cost of staying   −   cost of moving
                               ▲                        ▲
                    friction, brownouts,        migration guide,
                    a credible date             tooling, assistance

   ...and nothing happens unless the message reaches someone who can act.
```

### Make moving cheap: the migration guide

A reference for the new API tells people how the new thing works. A migration guide tells them how to get from what they have to what they need, which is a different document:

**Written from the old side.** Organize it by the old endpoint's parameters, fields, and behaviors — the things the reader's code already contains — and give the new equivalent for each:

| Old (`/geocode/v1`) | New (`/places/search`) | What's different |
|---|---|---|
| `q` | `query` | Name only. |
| `result` (single object) | `results[0]` | Up to five ranked results. Taking the first reproduces the old behavior; check `match_level` first. |
| `accuracy`: `rooftop` / `street` / `city` | `match_level`: `address` / `street` / `locality` / `region`, plus `confidence` 0–1 | `rooftop` ≈ `address` with `confidence ≥ 0.9`. The old endpoint never returned region-level matches; the new one does. |
| `country=Germany` or `DE` | `country=DE` only | Country names are now rejected with `400`. |
| no match → `200`, `result: null` | no match → `200`, `results: []` | |

**Honest about behavior, not just shape.** The rows that matter most are the ones in the last column. A different provider will return somewhat different coordinates for the same address; say so, and say how much, based on a comparison you've run. Silent differences are how migrations "succeed" and then produce complaints.

**Ordered by real usage.** Your usage data from 33.2a tells you which parameters and patterns people actually use. The pattern 1,400 accounts rely on gets a worked example in every SDK language; the parameter three accounts use gets one line.

**With a way to verify before switching.** Clients will move faster if they can check the result first: a sandbox, a comparison script that sends their own sample of addresses to both endpoints and reports differences, or the ability to try the new behavior per request before committing (the version override from 33.1b).

**With a way back, until the sunset.** If switching is reversible — a config flag, a per-request option — the risk of moving drops sharply, and so does the cost.

**Automated where possible.** An SDK release that makes the switch internally, or a codemod that rewrites the common call patterns in client code, turns a migration into an upgrade. Kubernetes shows how far this can go: for its API removals it publishes a migration guide mapping each removed API to its replacement, provides tooling to convert manifests, and has the API server attach deprecation warnings to responses — which `kubectl` then prints in front of the person running the command. That last part is the in-band signal from 33.2a, delivered by the client's own tool to someone who can act on it.

### Reach who can act: segment by integration

The remaining 1,900 accounts are not 1,900 independent problems. Group them by integration — the user agent, SDK and version, plugin name, key labels, referring domain — and a different picture appears:

```
 1,900 accounts still on /geocode/v1, grouped by integration

 store-locator WordPress plugin (3rd party)  ████████████████  640  → 1 author
 your own map widget, pre-2025 embed         ███████████       420  → you
 three e-commerce platform partners          ██████████        380  → 3 partner teams
 key accounts, custom code                   █                  40  → account managers
 long tail, custom code                      ███████████       420  → automation, brownouts
```

Five conversations can move 1,440 accounts. The campaign is built around these segments, each with its own approach:

- **Your own software.** The old map widget is a script you host, so you can update it to call the new endpoint, and 420 accounts migrate without doing anything. (With one catch: sites that embedded it with a Subresource Integrity hash will break if the file's content changes. Those need a new file URL and a message — and your usage data can tell you which ones they are.)
- **Third-party software used by many accounts.** Contact the plugin author directly and early. Offer early access, a review of their change, and a public note to their users once the fixed version ships. A pull request from your engineers to an open-source plugin is often the fastest route of all.
- **Platform partners.** Their integration team, through the partnership relationship, with a date that fits their release train.
- **Key accounts with custom code.** Account managers and a named engineer on your side. For the largest, offer to pair on the change.
- **The long tail.** Automated, personalized messages, the dashboard, SDK warnings, and — for those who respond to none of it — brownouts.

**Integration fingerprints are heuristics.** A custom client using a generic HTTP library shows up as `python-requests/2.31`, which tells you the language and nothing about the integration. You can make future campaigns much easier by having your SDKs send an application name and version in the user agent, and by encouraging clients to label their API keys.

### Outreach: specific, escalating, and quiet once they've moved

Each message should answer what the recipient actually needs to know: whether they're affected, which of their integrations, what happens if they do nothing, what to change, how to test it, and where to get help. The facts from 33.2a make this possible: "Your account called `/geocode/v1` from `storelocator-wp/3.2` on 212 sites in the last 30 days, most recently this morning."

**A cadence tied to the date.** For example: at announcement, then at six, three, and one month, then two weeks, one week, and the day before. Each message should contain something new — a brownout schedule, a newly released plugin fix, a changed count — or it trains people to ignore the next one.

**Stop messaging people who've moved.** Check usage before every send. Telling an account that has already migrated that it hasn't erodes trust in everything else you send.

**An escalation ladder for non-responders.** Technical contact → all account admins → the account owner or billing contact (who may not know what a geocoding endpoint is, but does know they don't want their store locator to break) → an account manager's call → for the largest accounts, a message between senior people on both sides. Each step goes up only after the previous one has gone unanswered.

**Every channel.** Email, the in-app dashboard, response headers and SDK warnings, the status page's scheduled-maintenance section, and a note on every support ticket from an affected account.

### Make staying felt: escalating friction, ending in brownouts

Advisory signals — headers, warnings, banners — move people who are paying attention. For the rest, the cost of staying has to become real before it becomes permanent. The usual ladder:

```
 headers + SDK warnings ─► dashboard banners ─► louder SDK warnings ─► brownouts ─► sunset
    (months)                  (months)             (weeks)            (weeks)     (the date)
```

A **brownout** is a scheduled window during which the deprecated endpoint returns exactly the error it will return after the sunset. Its purpose is to make things fail in clients' systems *while they can still be fixed* — to trigger their alerts, surface the batch job nobody remembered, and make the dependence visible to someone who can act.

A typical schedule for a 30 June sunset:

```
 Apr 13   ▮                      30 min, twice (07:00 and 19:00 UTC)
 May  4   ▮▮▮                    2 hours, twice
 May 25   ▮▮▮▮▮▮▮                6 hours, twice
 Jun 15   ▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮  24 hours
 Jun 30   ──────────────────────  removed
```

A few details decide whether brownouts help or just hurt:

- **Announce them.** Publish the schedule in advance, in every channel. An announced brownout still catches everyone who wasn't reading — and being announced makes it fair.
- **Cover different hours.** Batch jobs run at night; business traffic runs during the day, in several time zones. Two windows at different times of day reach more of both. GitHub's brownouts for its password-authentication removal ran two windows on each brownout day, hours apart, and said plainly that the goal was to trigger alerts in customers' own systems.
- **Start short, during your support hours.** The first brownout should be small, and at a time when your team is awake to answer the flood of questions it produces.
- **Return the real post-sunset response,** with a message saying it's a scheduled brownout, when it ends, and when removal happens. A brownout that returns something different from the sunset doesn't rehearse the sunset.
- **Treat the results as data.** Every account that hits a brownout window is on your list; every support ticket during one names someone the campaign missed. A brownout can also show you aren't ready. GitHub once paused a planned API sunset after its first 12-hour brownout revealed a larger impact than expected. If the date *can* move, that's the moment to move it — before the date, with a stated reason — rather than rolling back afterwards.
- **Exclude deliberately, not by default.** Accounts with recorded exceptions skip brownouts. Life-safety uses — say, a customer that geocodes addresses for emergency dispatch — shouldn't be browned out at all; but that means someone has to confirm with them, personally, that they've migrated.

### Assist where it pays

Some of the remaining accounts are worth your engineers' time directly: the plugin that carries 640 accounts, a key account whose integration is complex, a partner on a slow release train. Offer office hours during the final months. Pair with their engineers. Review their pull request. This is expensive per account, and it's by far the most effective thing you can do for the accounts where it's used.

### The endgame

Expect a rush in the last few weeks, however well the campaign has gone. Staff support for it, and keep the exception process (33.2a) open to requests with specific plans attached. On the day, remove as rehearsed, with the switch to reverse it ready. After the sunset, the `410` stub keeps counting who you missed.

Then hold a short review: what the burn-down curve did after each message, each release, and each brownout; which segments moved and which didn't; what the guide was missing. That review is what makes the next deprecation faster, and it belongs in your deprecation policy.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A large, varied client base** where "email everyone" doesn't reach the people who can act — thousands of accounts, many built on shared plugins, SDKs, or platforms.
- **A fixed external deadline,** like a vendor contract, a regulation, or a certificate expiry, where the date can't slip and the curve has to reach the bottom in time.
- **Critical flows,** where the cost of a client discovering the change on the day is high for them and for their users.
- **Brownouts specifically** when a meaningful group of clients is unreachable by any other channel, and a short, announced failure is acceptable in exchange for them finding out before the permanent one.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A small client base.** If forty accounts remain and you can call all of them, call them. Segmentation and brownout schedules are for numbers you can't handle by hand.
- **Known internal consumers.** A migration among internal teams is a planning problem, not a campaign.
- **When a permanent adapter is cheap and honest** (33.2a). If the old contract can be served faithfully on top of the new system, there may be nothing to campaign for.
- **Brownouts for flows where any outage is unacceptable.** Handle those accounts individually. A brownout is a deliberate outage; it's only acceptable where a short one is survivable.

## Trade-offs

- **Brownouts are real outages for real people.** Your clients' users see failed store locators, missing delivery estimates, broken forms. Make sure your terms and SLA exclude deprecated features during announced brownouts, or a brownout may breach contracts.
- **The campaign costs engineering and support time,** much of it spent on other companies' code: guides, SDK releases, pairing sessions, plugin PRs, and the ticket spike after every brownout.
- **Message fatigue.** Every message that doesn't contain new or specific information makes the next one less likely to be read — including messages about entirely different things.
- **Segmenting takes data work,** and fingerprints are imperfect. Some integrations will be misattributed, and some messages will go to the wrong people.
- **Assistance isn't evenly distributed.** Large accounts and popular plugins get your engineers; the long tail gets automated emails and brownouts. That's usually the right allocation, and it's worth being conscious that it's a choice.
- **Auto-migrating clients is a change you make to their product.** Updating the widget on 420 sites changes behavior they didn't ask for, and they'll hear about any differences from their users before they hear from you. Announce it, and say what will look different.

## Alternatives

**Extend the deadline behind the scenes.** If the date is set by something like a vendor contract, negotiating a short extension may be cheaper than a campaign that has to finish in a hurry. It only buys time; the campaign still has to happen.

**A permanent adapter — honestly labeled.** Reimplementing `/geocode/v1` on top of the new provider looks like continuity, but it returns different coordinates under an unchanged contract. That's a breaking change disguised as none (33.1a). It can be the right choice, provided it's announced as a change in results rather than presented as nothing having happened.

**Incentives.** Credits, discounts, or access to new features for accounts that migrate early. This lowers the relative cost of moving rather than raising the cost of staying, which tends to generate more goodwill than brownouts do, though it rarely reaches the unresponsive long tail.

**Paid extended support** (33.2a), for accounts that would rather pay than move on your schedule.

**Hard cutover with no campaign.** Cheapest to run. With a large client base, you pay for it in support tickets and trust on the day.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — A guide written from the old side.** Before announcing: a mapping from every old parameter, field, and behavior to its new equivalent, behavior differences stated plainly, examples for the most-used patterns, and a way to verify. *Move here when:* you've decided to deprecate something — before the announcement, not after the first confused replies.

**Level 1 — Segmented, personalized outreach.** Group remaining usage by integration, choose a channel per segment, send account-specific facts on a cadence, stop messaging accounts that have moved, and track the burn-down per segment. *Move here when:* you have more remaining accounts than you can contact individually.

**Level 2 — Work the leverage points.** Update your own clients (SDKs, widgets, plugins), work directly with third-party integrators and platform partners, and assign key accounts to account managers and named engineers. *Move here when:* grouping shows that a few integrations account for a large share of the remaining accounts.

**Level 3 — Escalating friction and brownouts.** A published brownout schedule, rehearsing the exact post-sunset response, with deliberate exclusions, staffed support, and a review after each window. *Move here when:* the curve has flattened with weeks to go and the remaining accounts aren't responding to any channel.

## Implementation

TypeScript, for the two pieces that turn this from a communications exercise into something you can run: grouping by integration, and brownouts in the request path.

### Grouping the remaining accounts by integration

```ts
type Segment = "own-software" | "third-party" | "partner" | "key-account" | "long-tail";

interface Group {
  integration: string; // e.g. "storelocator-wp/3.x"
  accounts: number;
  segment: Segment;
}

function planCampaign(rows: UsageRow[]): Group[] {
  const byIntegration = groupBy(rows, (r) => familyOf(r.integration)); // collapse patch versions
  return Object.entries(byIntegration)
    .map(([integration, rs]) => ({
      integration,
      accounts: new Set(rs.map((r) => r.accountId)).size,
      segment: segmentFor(integration, rs), // known plugin list, partner registry, account tier
    }))
    .sort((a, b) => b.accounts - a.accounts); // leverage first
}
```

The sort order is the campaign's priority order: work from the top, where one conversation moves hundreds of accounts, down to the long tail, where it moves one.

> **`familyOf` hides a judgment call.** Collapse too much (`python-requests/*`) and unrelated custom integrations look like one group you could contact; collapse too little (`storelocator-wp/3.2.1` vs `3.2.2`) and one plugin looks like dozens of small groups. The generic HTTP-library families should fall straight into the long tail rather than being treated as an integration you can reach.

### Brownouts in the request path

This extends the deprecation middleware from 33.2a:

```ts
const BROWNOUTS: Record<string, { start: Date; minutes: number }[]> = {
  "endpoint:geocode-v1": [
    { start: new Date("2027-04-13T07:00:00Z"), minutes: 30 },
    { start: new Date("2027-04-13T19:00:00Z"), minutes: 30 },
    { start: new Date("2027-05-04T07:00:00Z"), minutes: 120 },
    // ...through the 24-hour window on June 15
  ],
};

function activeBrownout(depId: string, now: Date) {
  return (BROWNOUTS[depId] ?? []).find(
    (w) => now >= w.start && now.getTime() < w.start.getTime() + w.minutes * 60_000,
  );
}

async function brownoutGate(req: ApiRequest, res: ApiResponse, dep: Deprecation) {
  const window = activeBrownout(dep.id, new Date());
  if (!window || (await exceptions.covers(req.accountId, dep.id))) return false;

  await usage.touch(req.accountId, `brownout:${dep.id}`, req.integration);
  const ends = new Date(window.start.getTime() + window.minutes * 60_000);
  res.status(410).json({
    error: {
      code: "endpoint_removed", // the same code the post-sunset stub returns
      message: `Scheduled brownout until ${ends.toISOString()}. ` +
        `This endpoint will be removed permanently on ${dep.sunsetAt.toISOString().slice(0, 10)}.`,
      guide: dep.guide,
    },
  });
  return true;
}
```

> **Why `410` and not `503`.** A `503` says "temporarily unavailable, try again" — clients retry, their dashboards blame your uptime, and some of them hammer the endpoint for the whole window. The point of the brownout is to rehearse the *permanent* failure, so the client's handling of the real post-sunset response gets exercised. Return exactly what the stub will return, with the same error code, so a fix that handles the brownout also handles the removal. The message is where it says "temporary."

> **The `brownout:` usage record is the most valuable output.** Accounts that hit a brownout window and still show up in the next one haven't acted on the failure — they may not even have noticed it. That short list is where your support team's personal outreach should go before the next, longer window.

> **Put brownouts on your status page as scheduled maintenance for the deprecated endpoint only.** Otherwise a client's on-call engineer, woken at 07:00 UTC, sees errors from your API, checks your status page, finds everything green, and opens an urgent incident against you — which is at least a way to find out who they are, but not a kind one.

## Further Reading

- **GitHub's deprecation changelog and developer blog posts on removing password and query-parameter authentication (2020–2021)** — a well-documented real campaign, with announced brownout schedules, windows at different times of day, a final longer brownout, and, separately, a sunset GitHub paused after a brownout showed more impact than expected.
- **Kubernetes documentation: "Deprecated API Migration Guide" and "Kubernetes Deprecation Policy"** — a migration guide organized from the old side, per removed API, and a policy that sets support windows by stability level. Also worth reading for the API server's deprecation warnings, which `kubectl` shows to the person running the command.
- **Codemod tooling: jscodeshift (JavaScript/TypeScript) and OpenRewrite (Java)** — for shipping migrations as automated rewrites of client code rather than as instructions.
- **Klein et al., "Brownout: Building More Robust Cloud Applications"** (ICSE 2014) — the *other* meaning of brownout, as degradation under load, worth knowing to avoid confusion.
- **Software Engineering at Google, chapter "Deprecation"** (also referenced in 33.2a) — especially its argument that the team doing a deprecation should expect to do much of the migration work itself.
- **In this collection:** 33.1a (why silent behavioral differences are breaking changes), 33.1b (version overrides that let clients migrate per request), 33.2a (usage tracking, headers, exceptions, and the post-sunset stub), 42.3 (forced-upgrade prompts for mobile clients, which play the brownout's role for apps).

A **Java sibling on shipping a migration as an OpenRewrite recipe** would be worth writing. This article treats the client's code as something you can only describe changes to; OpenRewrite lets an API provider publish a recipe that clients run against their own codebase to make the change mechanically — renaming calls, rewriting request builders, updating dependency versions — with type information that plain text-based rewrites don't have. It's the strongest version of "make moving cheap," and the Java ecosystem has the most mature tooling for it. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this, and from the module: **every change to an API has a cost that lands on someone else's calendar.** Compatibility (33.1a) avoids the cost where it can, versioning (33.1b) lets clients choose when to pay it, deprecation (33.2a) tells them it's due, and a migration campaign makes it as small as possible and makes sure the right person is holding the bill.
