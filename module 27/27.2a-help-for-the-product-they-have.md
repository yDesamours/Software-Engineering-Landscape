# Help for the Product They Actually Have: Building a Knowledge Base That Works

_Module 27.2a — Customer Support Integration_

## The Problem

Suppose you build booking and payments software for hair and beauty salons. About 4,500 salons use it in the US, the UK and Australia, on three plans. There's a web app for the front desk and a mobile app for stylists. Support is nine agents, and the help center has 310 articles, most written by whoever built the feature, around the time it launched.

Leadership asks support to "deflect more tickets with self-service." A year later, the help center has grown, reported deflection is up, and customers are angrier. Looking closely:

**Articles for a screen that no longer exists, or doesn't exist yet.** The calendar was redesigned and rolled out behind a flag over four months. During that time, half the salons saw the old calendar and half saw the new one. The articles showed the new one, because they were rewritten on launch day. Salons still on the old calendar followed instructions for buttons they didn't have. An audit finds 38% of articles mention a menu name that has since changed.

**Articles for features the customer doesn't have.** "Take a deposit when booking" explains a feature that's only on the Pro plan. Nothing says so. A Basic salon follows it, can't find the setting, and files a ticket that starts "your help center is useless." A UK salon reads the US article about card readers, which describes hardware not sold in the UK.

**Search that doesn't speak the customers' language.** Salons search for "no show fee" and "cancellation charge." The article is titled "Configuring booking protection policies." The help center's own search returns nothing for 22% of queries. Nobody has ever looked at the search log.

**Knowledge that lives everywhere except the knowledge base.** Agents answer the same 40 questions every week using saved replies (macros) that are more accurate than the articles, because agents update them the moment something changes. Those replies aren't public. The best explanation of how tips are split between stylists is a Slack thread from last spring.

**A deflection number that measures giving up.** The team reports 70% deflection: the share of help-center visits not followed by a ticket within 24 hours. But to raise it, the "Contact us" button was moved behind three pages of suggested articles. Tickets fell. Phone calls, one-star app reviews and cancellations that mention support rose. Some customers who didn't file a ticket had solved their problem; many had simply stopped trying.

The help center was treated as a pile of static pages, when it's really a view of the product that has to match what each customer sees, stay current as the product changes, and be judged by whether people's problems got solved.

## What It Is

A **knowledge base** is the collection of self-service help content (how-to articles, troubleshooting guides, reference pages, FAQs) customers use to solve problems without contacting support, together with the processes that keep it correct.

Three ideas separate a working one from a pile of pages:

1. **Applicability.** Every article, or part of one, knows which plans, regions, platforms and product versions it applies to, and readers see the version that matches their product.
2. **Ownership tied to the product.** Articles are linked to the features they describe, so changing a feature triggers a review of its articles, and the people closest to customer questions (agents) can improve content as part of their work.
3. **Measuring resolution, not avoidance.** The goal is customers solving problems. Fewer tickets is a side effect, and only a good one when it comes from success rather than friction.

The knowledge base is different from **developer documentation** (API references, SDK guides), which has a technical audience and a reference style, though many of the same practices apply. It's also different from **internal knowledge** for agents (troubleshooting steps, policies, known issues from 27.1b). Internal and public content can share one system with visibility levels, but they have different audiences and different rules about what may be said.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Tickets start with "I followed the help article but…"
- Screenshots in articles don't match what customers see, or match only some customers.
- Articles don't say which plan, region or platform they apply to.
- Nobody reviews articles when features change; you learn an article is wrong from a ticket.
- The help center's search log has never been read, or there isn't one.
- Agents' saved replies are more accurate than the public articles on the same topic.
- Self-service success is reported as "deflection," and the contact option has become harder to find.
- In-app "Help" links go to the help center's home page, not the article for that screen.

## How It Works

### 1. Applicability: one article, several products

Your product is really several products: each plan, each region, each platform, and each flag variant during a rollout (26.2b). An article that ignores that is wrong for most readers.

Attach applicability to content, at the article level or for individual blocks inside an article:

```
Article: "Take a deposit when booking"
  applies_to: plan ∈ {pro}, region ∈ {us, uk, au}, feature: deposits
  ├─ block "Turn on deposits"             (all)
  ├─ block "Old calendar: …"              (variant calendar_v2 = off)
  ├─ block "New calendar: …"              (variant calendar_v2 = on)
  └─ block "Refunding a deposit via Square" (region = us)
```

The help center renders it for the reader's context:

- **Signed-in customers** (from the product, or signed in to the help center) see their version: the calendar they have, their region's hardware, and for features they don't have, a clear note ("Deposits are on the Pro plan") instead of instructions they can't follow. That note is itself useful; it answers the real question.
- **Anonymous readers and search engines** see every variant, clearly labeled, so nobody gets a single version that's wrong for them.
- **Unreleased features** are the exception: an article for a feature in private beta shouldn't be public at all, even labeled. A flag-gated article accidentally published is an announcement you didn't mean to make.

Keeping plan, region and variant names identical to the product's own entitlement and flag keys is what makes this cheap. The help center asks the same questions the product does.

### 2. Contextual help: link from the screen, not to the home page

The most useful help is the article for the screen a customer is looking at, one click away. That means in-app help links keyed by **screen or feature identifiers**, not URLs:

```
In product:  <HelpLink topic="booking.deposits.settings" />
Help center: topic "booking.deposits.settings" → current article + section
```

The mapping from topic to article lives with the help content. Articles can then be reorganized, retitled or split without breaking links in a release that shipped last year. It also works in reverse: every topic key in the product that points at nothing is a gap you can list.

In-product explanations (26.2a) are the best version of this: "Deposits are on the Pro plan" on the locked setting removes a search, an article and a ticket.

### 3. Search in the customer's vocabulary

Customers search for symptoms and their own words ("no show fee," "double booked," "why was I charged"), not your feature names. Three sources tell you what they actually say:

- **The search log.** Queries with no results, and queries where nobody clicked a result, are a list of missing articles and missing synonyms. Review them weekly.
- **Ticket subjects and first messages.** They're written in exactly the language customers use before they know your terms.
- **Searches followed by a contact.** Someone who searched "cancellation charge," read two articles, then opened a ticket is telling you those articles didn't answer it.

The fixes are mostly unglamorous: synonyms in the search index (Module 12), titles and first sentences written with customer words ("Charge a fee for no-shows and late cancellations"), and articles for questions rather than features.

### 4. Ownership: articles move when the product moves

Articles go stale because nothing connects changing the product to changing its documentation. Give each article a set of **feature keys** (the same keys as the topics above), and let product changes create review work:

- A flag's rollout starts or finishes → review articles with blocks for that variant; remove the old variant's block when the rollout ends.
- A release note or merged change mentions a feature key → its articles get a review task.
- An article hasn't been reviewed in, say, 12 months → it's flagged, and its view count decides whether it's reviewed, merged or retired.

**Screenshots are a maintenance cost.** Each one goes out of date at the next UI change. Use them where they genuinely help (finding something on a busy screen) and prefer text naming the exact label otherwise. Some teams generate screenshots automatically in the end-to-end test suite (Module 21), which keeps them current at the price of test infrastructure.

**Agents write too.** The method with a name is **Knowledge-Centered Service (KCS)**: agents search the knowledge base while solving a ticket, link the article they used, fix it if it was wrong, and write a new one if none existed, as part of the job rather than a separate documentation project. The macros that were better than the articles become the articles. That needs a light review step for accuracy and tone, and time budgeted for it.

### 5. Measuring whether it works

"Deflection" is a counterfactual: tickets that would have been filed but weren't. You can't observe it directly, and the easy proxy (visits not followed by a ticket) rises when customers give up or when contacting you gets harder.

Better signals, roughly in order of how much to trust them:

- **Contact-after-view rate per article.** Of people who read this article, how many contacted support about the same topic within a day? A rising rate means the article isn't answering the question.
- **Search success.** Share of searches that end in a click and no follow-up search or contact.
- **Ticket volume per topic, normalized by active accounts**, before and after an article is rewritten.
- **A holdout experiment.** When customers start a contact form, show suggested articles to most of them and none to a random slice (26.2b). The difference in how many go on to submit, and in their satisfaction afterward, is the closest thing to real deflection you can measure.
- **Article ratings ("Was this helpful?")** are weak: few people vote, and they vote on mood. Useful to spot very bad articles, not to rank good ones.

And one rule that isn't a metric: **keep the contact option visible.** Suggested articles alongside the contact form are helpful. Articles in front of the contact form, blocking it, produce the numbers from the problem section.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Recurring how-to questions.** If the same 40 questions make up half of all tickets, articles for those 40 pay for themselves quickly.
- **Several plans, regions or platforms**, where one generic article is wrong for most readers. Applicability metadata is what makes the knowledge base accurate at all.
- **Gradual rollouts and redesigns**, where two versions of the UI coexist for weeks or months.
- **Customers who work outside business hours** (salons open evenings and weekends), for whom self-service is the only support available at that moment.
- **A support team large enough that knowledge scatters** into macros, chat and people's heads.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **An early product that changes weekly.** Articles will be stale before they're read. A short FAQ plus fast human answers teaches you what the articles should eventually say.
- **Block-level variants for an article with one audience.** If a feature is the same on every plan and platform, article-level metadata (or none) is enough.
- **A full KCS program for three agents.** A shared habit of "fix the article when you notice" gets most of the benefit.
- **Heavy measurement for a help center with 200 visits a month.** Read the search log and the tickets; statistics won't tell you much at that size.
- **Self-service for problems customers can't solve themselves** (a failed payout, a billing error, a bug). An article that says "contact us" is fine; one that pretends they can fix it isn't.

## Trade-offs

- **Variant content multiplies writing and review.** Every block with an applicability rule is a case to write, check and eventually remove. Rollouts leave behind dead blocks unless removing them is part of finishing the rollout.
- **Personalized articles are harder to share.** An agent sending a link, or a salon owner forwarding one to a manager on a different plan, may see different content. Show which version the reader is seeing, and let them switch.
- **Coupling help content to product keys.** Topic and feature keys become a contract between the product and the help center. Renaming a key without the other side breaks links and review triggers.
- **Agent-written content needs review.** KCS spreads authorship, which raises accuracy on details and risks inconsistency in tone, policy statements ("we'll refund…"), and promises support can't keep.
- **Honest measurement is slower and less flattering.** Holdout experiments withhold help from some customers and produce smaller numbers than the old deflection metric. That's the point, but it has to be explained upward.
- **Search tuning is never finished.** Synonyms and titles drift as customer vocabulary and the product change.

## Alternatives

**AI answers over the knowledge base.** A retrieval-augmented assistant (39.4) answers questions directly, drawing on articles. It depends on everything above more, not less: an assistant that retrieves the Pro-only deposit article for a Basic salon states the wrong answer fluently, without the visual cues that tell a reader "this may not be me." Applicability metadata becomes a retrieval filter, and the measurement problem is the same, with "answered by the assistant" in place of "viewed an article."

**Community forums.** Customers answering each other covers the long tail of questions you'd never write articles for, and captures vocabulary. It needs moderation, and answers go stale with nobody responsible for them.

**Docs as code.** Keeping help content in the repository, reviewed in the same pull request as the feature, ties documentation to changes most tightly. It suits developer documentation and technical audiences; it shuts out agents and support writers unless the tooling is friendly to them.

**Hosted help-center platforms.** Most help desks include a help center with search, article feedback and suggested articles in the contact form. They're a sensible starting point. What they usually lack is applicability tied to your plans and flags; some support audience segments or custom templates that can get partway there.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — Read the search log and the top ticket topics; write or fix the ten articles behind the most tickets,** in customers' words. *Signal to move on:* tickets that say "I followed the article but…"

**L1 — Plan, region and platform labels on every article,** and a visible note when a signed-in reader doesn't have the feature. *Signal:* a redesign or major feature rolling out gradually.

**L2 — Variant blocks tied to flag keys, and in-app help links by topic key.** Removing old variant blocks is part of completing a rollout. *Signal:* articles found wrong via tickets rather than reviews.

**L3 — Feature keys on articles and review tasks triggered by releases, rollouts and age.** Agents link articles on tickets and fix them as they go. *Signal:* leadership asking whether self-service is working.

**L4 — Honest measurement:** contact-after-view per article, search success, and a holdout on suggested articles in the contact form. Retire the old deflection number. *Signal:* the remaining unanswered questions are about things the product doesn't do, which is feedback (27.3a).

## Implementation

TypeScript. Two pieces: rendering an article for the reader's context, and measuring suggested articles with a holdout instead of counting silence.

**Rendering for the reader:**

```ts
type Ctx =
  | { kind: "customer"; plan: Plan; region: Region; variants: Record<string, string> }
  | { kind: "anonymous" };

type Rule = { plans?: Plan[]; regions?: Region[]; variant?: { key: string; is: string } };
type Block = { id: string; rule?: Rule; body: string };
type Article = { id: string; applies: Rule; feature: string; blocks: Block[]; released: boolean };

export function render(a: Article, ctx: Ctx) {
  if (!a.released) return { status: 404 as const };           // private beta stays private

  if (ctx.kind === "anonymous") {
    // Everyone sees every variant, labeled. Nobody gets one silently wrong version.
    return { status: 200 as const,
             blocks: a.blocks.map((b) => ({ ...b, label: describe(b.rule) })) };
  }

  if (!matches(a.applies, ctx)) {
    // Answer the real question instead of giving instructions they can't follow.
    return { status: 200 as const, notice: availabilityNotice(a.applies, ctx), blocks: [] };
  }

  return { status: 200 as const,
           blocks: a.blocks.filter((b) => !b.rule || matches(b.rule, ctx)),
           seeingAs: { plan: ctx.plan, region: ctx.region } };        // shown, switchable
}

function matches(r: Rule, c: Extract<Ctx, { kind: "customer" }>) {
  return (!r.plans || r.plans.includes(c.plan)) &&
         (!r.regions || r.regions.includes(c.region)) &&
         (!r.variant || c.variants[r.variant.key] === r.variant.is);
}
```

> **Aside — where variants come from.** `ctx.variants` must be the same flag evaluation the product used for this account (26.2b), fetched from the flag service, not re-derived in the help center with its own rules. Two evaluations of "is this salon on the new calendar" will disagree during a rollout, which is exactly when the article matters. And an unmatched variant key (a block for a flag that's since been deleted) matches nobody, so the block silently disappears: list those on every flag cleanup.

**Measuring suggested articles with a holdout:**

```ts
// Contact form opened. Most people see suggestions; a stable 10% see none.
export async function onContactFormOpened(accountId: string, draftText: string) {
  const arm = bucket(`kb-suggest:${accountId}`) < 0.1 ? "holdout" : "suggest";
  const suggestions = arm === "suggest" ? await kbSearch(draftText, ctxFor(accountId)) : [];

  await analytics.track("contact_form_opened", {
    accountId, arm, topic: classify(draftText), suggested: suggestions.map((s) => s.id),
  });
  return { suggestions };        // the submit button is visible in both arms
}

// Later, per topic:
//   submit rate (holdout) − submit rate (suggest)      → tickets genuinely avoided
//   satisfaction and 7-day repeat contact, by arm       → whether avoided meant solved
```

> **Aside — what the holdout protects against.** Without it, every contact form that wasn't submitted counts as a success, including people who closed the tab and called. The holdout gives you the submission rate with no suggestions shown, so the difference is attributable to the suggestions. Checking repeat contact by arm catches the case where suggestions reduce tickets today and produce angrier ones next week. Keep the bucket stable per account, not per visit, or the same salon will switch between arms and blur the result.

## Further Reading

- **The Consortium for Service Innovation's KCS v6 Practices Guide**, the source for Knowledge-Centered Service: capturing and improving knowledge as part of solving tickets.
- **Diátaxis** (diataxis.fr), a framework separating tutorials, how-to guides, reference and explanation. Most help-center articles are how-to guides trying to also be the other three.
- **_Docs for Developers_ by Jared Bhatti and co-authors** (Apress, 2021), on planning, writing, measuring and maintaining documentation. Written for developer docs, but the maintenance and measurement chapters apply directly.
- **Nielsen Norman Group on help and documentation** (the tenth of Jakob Nielsen's usability heuristics, and their articles on contextual help), for why help at the point of need beats a help center home page.
- **Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang and Ya Xu, _Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments_** (Cambridge, 2020), for holdouts, stable assignment and the pitfalls of measuring what users didn't do.

The habit to take from this: **documentation is a view of the product, and it has as many versions as the product does.** Whenever you add a plan, a region or a flag, ask what the help says to people on each side of it.
