# Evidence, Not Votes: Turning Customer Feedback into Decisions

_Module 27.3a — Customer Support Integration_

## The Problem

Suppose you build inventory and order management for independent online retailers. About 2,200 merchants use it to sync stock across their web store and marketplaces, print pick lists and reorder from suppliers. There's a support team, a small sales team, three account managers for the largest merchants, and a product team of four deciding what gets built.

Everyone agrees customer feedback matters. Here's what the feedback actually looks like after two years.

**It's everywhere.** 1,800 tickets tagged `feature-request`, with nothing else recorded. Notes in the sales CRM. A `#customer-feedback` Slack channel where people paste screenshots. A public voting board. Each account manager's own spreadsheet. App marketplace reviews. The product team reads whichever of these it happens to see that week.

**The voting board measures the wrong crowd.** The top item is "dark mode," with 900 votes, mostly from trial accounts and a reseller forum that links to the board. "Transfer stock between warehouses" has 40 votes. Those 40 come from accounts that together pay 38% of revenue. The board's statuses say "Under review" on 120 items, some for over a year, and the comments have become a place to complain about being ignored.

**The same need in twenty words, and different needs in one.** "Bulk edit SKUs," "edit many products at once" and "CSV update for prices" are the same need, filed separately, each looking small. Meanwhile "multi-location" means physical warehouses to some merchants and bin locations inside one warehouse to others, and both groups have been counted together. Many requests are solutions rather than problems: "add an Excel export to the orders page" turns out, on a call, to mean "my accountant needs cost of goods sold each month," which an export doesn't solve.

**Promises nobody recorded.** To close a large merchant, sales promised warehouse transfers "in Q3." It's in an email thread. Product finds out in Q3, from the merchant.

**Shipped, and nobody told.** Warehouse transfers finally ship. Of the merchants who asked for it over two years, nobody is notified. Two months later, a merchant cancels. The cancellation reason: "Doesn't support moving stock between warehouses."

**A score nobody can explain.** The quarterly Net Promoter Score falls from 41 to 33. Leadership calls a meeting. Nobody can say why, or whether it's real. The survey went by email to account owners, 210 responded, and the account managers, whose bonuses are tied to the score, had been reminding their favorite customers to fill it in.

None of these is a shortage of feedback. It's a shortage of structure: no single record, no link between what someone said and who they are, and no way to tell a real change from noise.

## What It Is

**Customer feedback management** is capturing what customers say about what they need, from every channel, as evidence linked to real accounts; grouping it by underlying need; using it, weighted by who's asking, as one input to product decisions; and telling people what happened to their request.

The central idea is to separate three things that usually arrive mixed together:

- **Evidence**: one thing one person said, with a source, a date, an account and ideally their exact words. "We move stock between our two warehouses by editing counts in both, and get it wrong weekly."
- **Needs**: the underlying problem, which many pieces of evidence point to. "Move stock between warehouses without manual double entry."
- **Ideas**: possible solutions, including the one the customer suggested. "A transfer screen," "a two-step shipment between locations."

Evidence links to needs many-to-one, the same shape as tickets to issues in 27.1b. Needs link to ideas and roadmap items. Votes, when you have them, are just one weak kind of evidence.

This article also covers **surveys** (Net Promoter Score, satisfaction and effort scores), which are feedback you ask for rather than feedback that arrives. They answer different questions and have their own statistical traps.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Feature requests are a tag on tickets and nothing more, and nobody can list the top ten by affected revenue.
- Feedback lives in five or more places, and the product team reads whichever is in front of them.
- A public voting board's ranking disagrees with what your largest customers ask for.
- The same need appears under many names, or one name hides several needs.
- Requests are recorded as the customer's proposed solution, with no note of the problem behind it.
- Sales or account managers make promises that product learns about from customers.
- Customers find out a requested feature shipped from a changelog, or from a competitor.
- A survey score moves and nobody can say whether the change is real or why it happened.

## How It Works

### 1. Capture: evidence from every channel into one place

Each channel gets a low-friction way to create an evidence record:

- **From tickets:** an agent marks a passage as feedback in the help desk, which sends it with the ticket ID and verified account (27.1a).
- **From sales and account management:** a button in the CRM, or a forwarding address, that creates evidence for the account in question.
- **From Slack and email:** a shortcut on the message.
- **From surveys and reviews:** the open-text answers, imported with the respondent's account where known.
- **From the product itself:** an in-app "suggest an improvement" form, which knows the account and the screen it was opened from.

Each record holds the verbatim quote, source, person, account and date. It doesn't hold copies of revenue or plan. Those are looked up when needed (§3). Quotes are customer data like any other: they may name people, and they go on the erasure list (24.4).

### 2. Triage: link evidence to needs, and write needs as problems

Someone (a product manager, or a rotation) reviews new evidence weekly and links each piece to an existing need or creates a new one. Two disciplines make this work:

**Write needs as problems, not solutions.** "Excel export on orders" becomes, after reading or asking, "Accountant needs monthly cost of goods sold." Several different requests often turn out to be the same problem, and the best solution is frequently none of the ones requested.

**Split needs that share a word.** "Multi-location" becomes two needs: multiple warehouses, and bin locations within a warehouse. If the evidence can't tell you which a merchant meant, that's a question to ask them, not a guess to make.

Merging needs moves their evidence, as with issues in 27.1b.

### 3. Weighting: who's asking, looked up at decision time

With evidence linked to accounts, a need's weight is a query over those accounts, not a vote count:

```
Need: Move stock between warehouses           Need: Dark mode
  accounts: 34 (distinct)                       accounts: 212 (distinct)
  their revenue: 38% of total                   their revenue: 4% (most on trial)
  churned citing it: 3                          churned citing it: 0
  prospects citing it: 6 (one in late stage)    prospects citing it: 0
  segment: multi-warehouse merchants            segment: broad
  trend: +5 accounts last quarter               trend: flat
```

Three rules keep this honest:

- **Count distinct accounts, not evidence.** An account manager logging the same request three times, or 14 people at one merchant, is one account.
- **Look up account data when you ask, not when evidence was captured.** An account that has grown, downgraded or churned since should count as what it is now. Churned accounts are shown separately, not dropped: they're the strongest evidence you have.
- **Show the dimensions, not one blended score.** Revenue, account count, churn, prospects and segment answer different questions. A formula that combines them hides the judgment that should be made in the open. Weighting by revenue alone, for example, systematically ignores new segments you're trying to win and customers too small to have an account manager.

Weighting is an input to the roadmap (Module 28), alongside strategy, cost and product analytics (Module 16). Behavior often contradicts stated preference: many people ask for things they wouldn't use.

### 4. Commitments are explicit

When sales or account management promises something, it's recorded as a **commitment** on the need: which account, what exactly, by when, who promised and who in product agreed. Product can then see what's owed, and a commitment nobody in product agreed to is visible as exactly that before the customer finds out.

### 5. Closing the loop

When a need is addressed, everyone with evidence linked to it should hear, with the same care as fixes in 27.1b: once it has actually reached them. A feature on a plan they don't have should say so, and offer the upgrade path. A feature behind a staged rollout should wait until it's on for their account. Churned accounts that cited it are worth a message too: "you left partly because of this; it exists now" is one of the more effective win-back emails there is.

Needs you've decided not to address deserve an answer as well. "We're not planning this, here's why" leaves customers able to plan. "Under review" for a year leaves them resentful.

### 6. Surveys: what each one measures

Three common measures ask different questions:

- **Net Promoter Score (NPS):** "How likely are you to recommend us?" 0–10. Percentage of 9s and 10s minus percentage of 0s to 6s. A relationship measure, sent periodically, not after any particular event.
- **Customer Satisfaction (CSAT):** "How satisfied were you with…?" After a specific interaction, most often a solved ticket. Good at spotting problems with a specific process or agent.
- **Customer Effort Score (CES):** "How easy was it to…?" After a task or support interaction. Research has linked effort more closely to loyalty than delight.

Whatever you measure, three things decide whether the number means anything.

**Who answered.** A survey emailed to account owners measures account owners who read email and chose to answer. Warehouse staff using the product all day never see it. The response is not a random sample, and no amount of statistics repairs that; you can only compare response rates by segment and treat the gaps as unknown.

**How noisy the score is.** NPS on a few hundred responses has a wide margin of error. With 210 responses, 50% promoters and 17% detractors, the score is 33 with a 95% confidence interval of roughly ±10 points. A drop from 41 to 33 may be nothing at all. Report the interval, and compare segments with enough responses to mean something.

**What's done with it.** The open-text follow-up ("What's the main reason for your score?") is usually worth more than the number. Code those answers into needs like any other evidence. And don't tie compensation to the score: people will start managing who responds and what they say, and the score stops measuring customers. That is exactly what the account managers above were doing.

In-app surveys should appear after someone finishes a task, not in the middle of one, with a cap on how often any person sees one.

## When It's Appropriate

- **More feedback than one person can hold in their head,** roughly when it arrives from more than two channels or more than a few people collect it.
- **Customers of very different sizes,** where raw counts and revenue-weighted counts disagree. That's where linking evidence to accounts changes decisions.
- **Sales-led deals,** where promises to prospects need to be visible to product.
- **Churn you'd like to understand,** where cancellation reasons need to connect to specific needs.
- **Leadership reacting to survey scores,** where margins of error and segment comparisons prevent expensive overreactions.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **An early product with a few dozen customers.** The founders should be talking to all of them. A shared document of notes, reread weekly, beats a pipeline, and direct conversations reveal the problem behind a request in a way no form does.
- **Weighting formulas and dashboards before you have evidence linked to accounts.** A scored ranking of uncategorized tickets is precise nonsense.
- **A public voting board for a product whose customers are a few hundred businesses.** Talking to them tells you more, and a board sets expectations you then have to manage.
- **Quarterly NPS with 60 responses.** The interval is too wide to show any change you'd act on. Read the comments instead.

## Trade-offs

- **Triage is real work, forever.** Linking evidence, rewriting requests as problems and splitting ambiguous needs takes hours each week. Skipped, the system decays back into a tag.
- **Linking everything to accounts brings bias toward customers you already have.** Prospects, lost deals and people who never signed up are underrepresented, and new segments look unimportant until they're large.
- **Weighting can become an excuse.** A ranked list looks objective and can end up substituting for product judgment and strategy rather than informing them.
- **Public boards cost goodwill if you don't maintain them.** Every status, comment and "under review" is a public promise.
- **Closing the loop can misfire.** Telling someone their request shipped when what shipped doesn't solve their version of the need is worse than silence. Link carefully and word messages as "this may help with…" when unsure.
- **Surveys cost attention.** Each one interrupts someone. Too many lowers response rates and annoys exactly the customers you most want to hear from.
- **Feedback contains personal data.** Quotes name colleagues and customers, and they end up in a product tool with broad access. They need the same retention and erasure handling as tickets.

## Alternatives

**Dedicated feedback tools.** Products built for this (Productboard, Canny, UserVoice and similar) provide capture from common channels, linking evidence to features, voting and changelog notifications. They're a reasonable default. Check whether they can weight by your own account data, which usually means syncing it, and whether they model needs separately from features, or only features.

**User research.** Interviews, observation and usability tests find problems customers don't think to report and reveal the reasons behind requests. They complement collected feedback rather than replace it: research tells you why, collected feedback tells you how widespread.

**Product analytics** (Module 16). Behavior shows what people actually do, including the workarounds that imply unmet needs (exporting to a spreadsheet and reimporting every week). It can't tell you about things people wanted and never tried.

**Beta and customer advisory programs.** A small group of engaged customers who see early work and give structured feedback. Good for depth and for testing solutions; they're not representative.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — One place, one habit.** A single location for feedback, and a shortcut in the help desk and CRM that sends a quote with its account. *Signal to move on:* the same request appearing under several names.

**L1 — Needs written as problems, with weekly triage** linking evidence and splitting ambiguous needs. *Signal:* disagreements about what's most requested.

**L2 — Weighting at query time:** distinct accounts, current revenue, churned and prospect counts, segment, trend. Record commitments explicitly. *Signal:* shipping something many people asked for, and wondering who to tell.

**L3 — Close the loop** when a need is addressed (after it reaches them) and when it's declined. *Signal:* leadership reacting to survey scores.

**L4 — Surveys with intervals and segments,** CSAT after solved tickets, open-text answers coded into needs, and no scores in compensation. *Signal:* you're at the end of this module, and the next question is how to turn weighted needs into a backlog, which is Module 28.

## Implementation

TypeScript. Two pieces: weighting a need from its evidence at query time, and an NPS calculation that reports its own uncertainty. Notification when a need ships reuses the per-account "reached" check from 27.1b.

**Weighting a need at decision time:**

```ts
type Evidence = { id: string; needId: string; accountId?: string; prospectId?: string;
                  source: "ticket" | "crm" | "survey" | "in_app" | "slack"; at: Date };

export async function needImpact(needId: string) {
  const ev = await evidence.forNeed(needId);

  // Distinct accounts, not evidence rows: 14 people at one merchant count once.
  const accountIds = [...new Set(ev.flatMap((e) => (e.accountId ? [e.accountId] : [])))];
  const prospects = new Set(ev.flatMap((e) => (e.prospectId ? [e.prospectId] : []))).size;

  // Current facts, looked up now — not copied into evidence when it was captured.
  const accounts = await accountFacts(accountIds); // { status, mrr, plan, segment, churnReason? }
  const active = accounts.filter((a) => a.status === "active");
  const churned = accounts.filter((a) => a.status === "churned");
  const since = daysAgo(90);

  return {
    accounts: active.length,
    revenueShare: sum(active.map((a) => a.mrr)) / (await totalMrr()),
    churned: churned.length,                       // shown, never dropped
    churnedCitingIt: churned.filter((a) => a.churnReason?.needIds.includes(needId)).length,
    prospects,
    bySegment: countBy(active, (a) => a.segment),
    newAccountsLast90d: new Set(ev.filter((e) => e.at >= since && e.accountId)
                                  .map((e) => e.accountId)).size,
  };
  // Deliberately no single "score": the dimensions answer different questions.
}
```

> **Aside — two ways this count lies.** Evidence without an account (an anonymous board vote, a forwarded email nobody linked) disappears from every number here. That's correct for weighting, but it should be visible as a count of its own, or a need with 300 unlinked votes looks like it has none. The opposite mistake is duplicate accounts: a merchant with two accounts (one per store brand) counts twice. Grouping accounts into customers belongs in the account model, not in this function.

**NPS with its uncertainty:**

```ts
export function nps(scores: number[]) {
  const n = scores.length;
  const pPro = scores.filter((s) => s >= 9).length / n;
  const pDet = scores.filter((s) => s <= 6).length / n;
  const score = pPro - pDet;

  // Each response is +1, 0 or −1; this is the variance of their mean.
  const variance = (pPro + pDet - score ** 2) / n;
  const margin = 1.96 * Math.sqrt(variance);        // ~95% interval, normal approximation

  return { n, nps: Math.round(score * 100), margin: Math.round(margin * 100) };
}

// nps(q3) → { n: 210, nps: 33, margin: 10 }   "33 ± 10"
// nps(q2) → { n: 240, nps: 41, margin: 10 }   "41 ± 10"
```

> **Aside — comparing two scores.** Overlapping intervals don't tell you directly whether two quarters differ. The difference between them has its own interval, roughly the square root of the sum of the two variances, and here it's about ±14 points, so an 8-point drop is well within noise. But the interval only covers sampling noise. It says nothing about who didn't respond, or about account managers nudging their favorite customers. Report the response rate by segment next to the score.

## Further Reading

- **Teresa Torres, _Continuous Discovery Habits_** (Product Talk, 2021), particularly the opportunity solution tree, a close relative of the evidence → need → idea structure here.
- **Rob Fitzpatrick, _The Mom Test_** (2013), on asking customers about their problems and past behavior rather than their opinions of your ideas, which is how you turn requests into needs.
- **Frederick Reichheld, "The One Number You Need to Grow"** (_Harvard Business Review_, 2003), the article that introduced NPS, and **Keiningham and co-authors, "A Longitudinal Examination of Net Promoter and Firm Revenue Growth"** (_Journal of Marketing_, 2007), which tested its central claim and found it much weaker than presented. Read both.
- **Matthew Dixon, Karen Freeman and Nicholas Toman, "Stop Trying to Delight Your Customers"** (_Harvard Business Review_, 2010), the basis of the Customer Effort Score.
- **The documentation of a feedback tool you're considering** (Productboard's model of notes linked to features is a good example), for how capture and linking look in practice.

---

The habit to take from this: **for every request, ask who said it, what problem is behind it, and how you'll tell them what happened.** A request without the first is a rumor, without the second is a guess, and without the third is a customer who stops asking.
