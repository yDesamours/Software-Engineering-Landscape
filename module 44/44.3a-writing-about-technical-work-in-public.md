# Writing About Technical Work in Public: Decisions, Postmortems and Performance Stories

_Module 44 · Portfolio & Showcase · 44.3a_

## The Problem

Suppose you work at a company that sells a document-signing API. Engineering is 40 people. Leadership decides to start an engineering blog, for hiring, credibility with developer customers, and because "other companies do it." Each team is asked for one post a quarter. After a year, here's what came of it.

**The victory lap.** The first post is titled "How We Scaled Our Signing Pipeline 10x." It describes moving PDF rendering from a monolith to a queue of workers, with an architecture diagram and a chart going up and to the right. It never says what was slow before, what else the team considered, what the change cost, or what 10x measures (throughput? on what hardware? compared with what?). A commenter asks whether the old system had simply been given too few machines. Nobody can answer from the post. It gets 300 views, mostly from employees.

**The leak.** The second post, about a database migration, includes a terminal screenshot. It shows an internal hostname, the name of the largest customer (in a table name), and the connection pooler's version, which has a known vulnerability the team hasn't patched yet. A security researcher mentions all three politely by email. The post is taken down and republished with blurred screenshots two weeks later.

**The benchmark that backfires.** A performance post claims the team's new JSON parser is "3x faster than the standard library." It's posted to a link aggregator. Within an hour someone reproduces it and shows the benchmark measured a warm cache against a cold one, and that the real difference is 1.2x. The top comment is about the benchmark, not the parser. That's what the company is known for, among exactly the developers it wanted to hire.

**The incident nobody explains.** A 4-hour outage in March stops every customer's signing flow. Customers ask what happened. The draft postmortem goes to legal, then to marketing, then back to legal. It's published 23 days later. By then it says "a configuration change caused elevated error rates; we have implemented additional safeguards." Customers' own engineers, who have to explain the outage to *their* customers, learn nothing they can use. Two of them write in to ask for "the real one."

**The post that named someone.** An internal retrospective is turned into a "lessons learned" post by a manager. It says the incident happened "because a junior engineer ran a migration without review." The engineer is identifiable to anyone who follows the company. The team stops volunteering details in retrospectives.

Every post took real effort. What went wrong wasn't the prose. Each one was written without deciding **who it was for and what they should be able to do after reading it**, without a way to tell what's safe to publish, and without the evidence that makes a technical claim believable to technical readers.

## What It Is

**Public technical writing** is writing about real engineering work (decisions, incidents, performance work, migrations, lessons) for readers outside your team: other engineers, customers, candidates. The common forms are engineering blog posts, public postmortems (also called incident reports), conference talks written up as articles, and case studies.

It's related to the internal writing covered elsewhere, and different enough to need its own treatment:

- **Internal written communication** (32.1a) and **architecture documentation** (31.4b) are for people who share your context. Public writing is for people who have none of it, and who might be competitors, attackers, or customers deciding whether to trust you.
- **The postmortem process itself** (24.3b) is how a team investigates and learns from an incident. A public postmortem is a separate document derived from it, for a different audience, with different rules about what goes in.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Posts announce what you built, but not why, what it replaced, or what it cost.
- Numbers in posts have no baseline, method or hardware attached.
- Readers' questions in comments can't be answered from the post.
- Screenshots and code samples go out without anyone checking what's in them.
- Posts regularly get pulled or quietly edited after publication.
- Public incident reports take weeks and say almost nothing.
- Customers' engineers ask for "the real" explanation after an incident.
- "Lessons learned" posts contain a person's name next to a mistake.
- Posts are written because a quota says so, and the traffic comes mostly from colleagues.

## How It Works

### 1. Start from the reader and what they'll do next

Before writing, finish this sentence: _after reading this, a [reader] will be able to [act]_. Some examples:

| Reader | What they should be able to do |
|---|---|
| An engineer facing the same problem | Decide whether your approach fits their constraints |
| A customer's engineer after an outage | Explain the impact to their own users, and judge whether it'll happen again |
| A candidate | Tell what working on your team is actually like |
| A developer evaluating your product | Trust your claims about performance or reliability |

The sentence decides what goes in. The victory-lap post had no reader in mind, so it contained only what the team was proud of. For "an engineer facing the same problem," the essential content is the context and the alternatives. The final architecture matters much less.

### 2. Four shapes, each with its own essential parts

Most technical posts are one of four kinds. Each has parts that, when missing, make the post useless to its reader.

**The decision story.** Why you chose X. Essential: the constraint that forced a decision, the options you considered, why the others lost, and what the choice cost you. The cost is what makes it credible. "We picked Postgres over DynamoDB because our access patterns change monthly, and we pay for that in manual sharding work" is useful to a reader. "We picked Postgres because it's great" isn't. A decision story is an architecture decision record (2.5) rewritten for outsiders.

**The public postmortem.** What happened and what you're changing. Essential:
- impact in the reader's terms (which features, which regions, how long, whether data was lost)
- a timeline with timestamps and time zones
- the causal chain, from trigger to failure, with the conditions that let it spread
- what stopped it, and what you're changing, with the difference between "done" and "planned"

**The performance story.** How you made something faster or cheaper. Essential: the baseline, the method of measurement, the workload, the hardware or environment, the change, the result with its variance, and the attempts that didn't work. The failed attempts are what other engineers learn the most from.

**The lessons-learned piece.** What you'd do differently. Essential: the specific situation, the mistake as a decision that seemed reasonable at the time, and a lesson general enough to apply elsewhere. "Don't make mistakes" isn't a lesson. "Treat a schema migration as a deploy, with a rollback plan" is.

### 3. Translate from inside to outside

Internal documents assume the reader knows your systems, your acronyms and your history. Turning one into a public post is mostly a translation job:

- **Replace names with roles.** "The ledger service" becomes "the service that records every signature event."
- **State the constraints that insiders take for granted.** "We have to keep signed PDFs byte-identical for seven years" explains decisions that otherwise look strange.
- **Say the transferable lesson out loud.** Readers won't reliably draw it themselves, and it's why they came.
- **Cut what only matters internally**: team reorganizations, who approved what, tooling names nobody outside uses.

### 4. Evidence: claims technical readers can check

Technical readers look for the evidence first. Some habits that hold up:

- **Numbers come with their conditions.** "p99 latency went from 840 ms to 190 ms, on production traffic, over the two weeks before and after, same instance types." Averages alone hide the tail.
- **Benchmarks come with the setup** (hardware, versions, data, warm-up, number of runs) and ideally the code, so someone can reproduce them. A claim that can be reproduced survives criticism. One that can't invites it.
- **Compare against the strongest alternative**, configured well. Beating a default configuration of a competitor proves little.
- **Include what didn't work.** It shows the result wasn't luck, and it's the part readers are least likely to find elsewhere.
- **Say what you don't know.** "We believe the regression came from X; we haven't confirmed it" is honest and loses nothing.

### 5. Decide what's safe to publish

A public post is read by everyone, including people looking for weaknesses. Check every draft, including images and code samples, for:

- **Security details before they're fixed**: vulnerable versions, internal hostnames and IP ranges, network layout, which checks you don't do. After a fix, how you found and fixed a vulnerability is often valuable to publish, and coordinated disclosure norms apply if it affects others.
- **Customer information**: names, logos, data, identifiable table or tenant names, without written permission.
- **Secrets**: keys and tokens in screenshots or code, which is more common than it sounds.
- **Commercial terms and unreleased plans**: vendor pricing under contract, roadmap items, anything under an NDA.
- **People**: a named individual next to a mistake.

Some disclosures aren't optional at all. Personal-data breaches can require notifying regulators within tight deadlines (GDPR's is 72 hours, 34.1a), and public companies in the US must disclose material cybersecurity incidents to the SEC. Those have their own process; a blog post doesn't replace them, and it shouldn't get ahead of them.

### 6. Review without killing the post

A review pipeline that takes three weeks turns every post into the March incident report. Two things keep it fast:

- **Written rules instead of case-by-case approvals.** A short list of what's always fine (architecture described by role, anonymized numbers, open-source code), what's never fine (the list in section 5), and what needs legal or security review. Most posts then need only a technical reviewer and a quick check against the list.
- **For incidents, publish in two steps.** A short, factual initial report within hours to a day, saying what's known and what isn't, followed by a detailed one once the investigation is done. Cloudflare published its account of the November 18, 2025 outage, signed by its CEO, on the same day. It explained that a database permissions change had doubled the size of a configuration file its proxies loaded, and that the file then exceeded a hard-coded limit. Speed and detail aren't opposites when the rules are decided in advance.

### 7. Postmortems: blameless in public too

A blameless internal postmortem (24.3b) describes what happened in terms of systems and decisions, because blaming individuals makes people hide information. The public version needs this even more. Describe the action and the conditions that allowed it: "a migration ran without review, because our tooling didn't require review for migrations." That's both kinder and more useful. The fix is in the tooling, and readers can check their own.

Radical openness is possible. In January 2017, GitLab.com lost several hours of production data when an engineer deleted a database directory on the wrong server, and then found that none of its backup methods produced a usable backup. GitLab published a running document during the recovery and livestreamed it, followed by a detailed postmortem. Few companies will go that far, and it's a useful reference for how much trust detail can build.

### 8. Shape the text for how it's read

Readers skim before they commit. Put the result and the lesson in the first paragraph, then the story. Use a title that says what the reader will learn ("Why our queue workers ran out of memory at 3 a.m. every Sunday"), not what you did ("Improving our pipeline"). Use one diagram where a flow or a before/after is easier to see than read. Date every post, and when facts change later, add a dated update rather than editing silently.

## When It's Appropriate

- **After an incident customers noticed.** Their engineers need facts to act on, and silence is read as either incompetence or hiding.
- **When you made a decision others will face.** Choosing between two databases, migrating a large system, dropping a popular tool. The considered alternatives are the valuable part.
- **When your claims are part of what you sell**: performance, reliability, security. A post with its method is more convincing than a marketing page.
- **Hiring**, when the post shows real work and real constraints.
- **Individual engineers building a portfolio**, about side projects, open-source work, or employer work with permission and under the same disclosure rules.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **When there's nothing transferable.** A migration that went exactly as planned, using standard tools, teaches readers nothing they couldn't find in the docs.
- **Posting to fill a quota.** A calendar of posts produces victory laps. One good post a year beats four empty ones.
- **Every minor incident.** A status-page entry is enough for a short blip nobody depended on. A full public postmortem makes sense when customers were affected enough to need an explanation.
- **When you can't say anything true yet.** If legal or security constraints would leave only vague statements, say briefly that an investigation is ongoing and publish when you can say something real.

## Trade-offs

- **It takes senior time.** A good decision post or postmortem takes a few days of an experienced engineer's time, plus reviews.
- **Detail reveals things.** Every architectural description gives attackers and competitors something. The rules in section 5 reduce this, and they don't remove it.
- **Public claims create commitments.** "We've implemented safeguards so this can't happen again" will be quoted back at you the next time it happens. Precise statements ("we now reject configuration files larger than N") age better than promises.
- **Criticism is public.** A flawed benchmark or a questionable decision will be discussed where your customers and candidates can see it.
- **Posts go stale.** Architecture posts describe a past system. Readers will assume they describe the current one unless they're dated and updated.
- **Honesty can be uncomfortable internally.** Writing "we chose wrong" in public needs leadership that's willing to have it said.

## Alternatives

- **Conference talks.** Reach a focused audience and allow questions. They reach fewer people than a post, unless they're also written up.
- **Open-sourcing the code.** Sometimes the most credible way to share a technique is the working tool, with a short README explaining why it exists.
- **Customer-only reports.** A detailed incident report sent to affected customers under NDA gives them what they need without publishing security details. Good for incidents with sensitive causes.
- **Contributing upstream.** Fixing a bug in the open-source project that caused your incident, with a clear issue, helps more people than a post about working around it.
- **Staying quiet.** Reasonable for companies whose customers aren't technical and whose hiring doesn't depend on reputation. It has a cost after incidents.

## How to Achieve It

**Step 1: write one post using the four shapes' essential parts.** Pick a recent decision or piece of performance work with a real lesson. Get one engineer outside the team to read it and say what they'd do differently afterwards. _Signal to move on:_ the post answers its readers' questions, but getting it approved was slow and inconsistent.

**Step 2: write the disclosure rules**, one page, agreed with security and legal. Add an automated check for the mechanical parts (secrets, hostnames, customer names). _Signal:_ posts get approved quickly, but incidents are still explained late and vaguely.

**Step 3: set up a public incident process**: who writes the initial report, how quickly, what's published at which stage, and a template for the detailed report. _Signal:_ you publish reliably, but posts don't reach their readers.

**Step 4: date and maintain what you publish.** Add updates when facts change, link related posts, and retire posts that describe systems that no longer exist.

## Implementation

A pre-publication check, in TypeScript, for the mechanical part of section 5. It scans a draft (Markdown, including code blocks) for things that should never go out: secrets, internal hostnames and private IP addresses, customer names, and unreleased project names. It doesn't replace a human reviewer; it catches what humans reliably miss.

```ts
type Rule = { name: string; pattern: RegExp; severity: "block" | "review" };

// Loaded from config files the security team maintains, not hard-coded.
const customers: string[] = ["Acme Legal", "Northwind"];
const codenames: string[] = ["Project Quill"];

const escape = (s: string) => s.replace(/[.*+?^${}()|[\]\\]/g, "\\$&");
const anyOf = (words: string[]) => new RegExp(`\\b(${words.map(escape).join("|")})\\b`, "gi");

const rules: Rule[] = [
  { name: "aws-access-key", pattern: /\bAKIA[0-9A-Z]{16}\b/g, severity: "block" },
  { name: "private-key", pattern: /-----BEGIN [A-Z ]*PRIVATE KEY-----/g, severity: "block" },
  { name: "internal-host", pattern: /\b[\w-]+\.(internal|corp|svc\.cluster\.local)\b/g, severity: "block" },
  { name: "private-ip", pattern: /\b(10(\.\d{1,3}){3}|192\.168(\.\d{1,3}){2}|172\.(1[6-9]|2\d|3[01])(\.\d{1,3}){2})\b/g, severity: "review" },
  { name: "customer-name", pattern: anyOf(customers), severity: "block" },
  { name: "unreleased", pattern: anyOf(codenames), severity: "block" },
];

export function checkDraft(markdown: string) {
  const findings = markdown.split("\n").flatMap((line, i) =>
    rules.flatMap(r => [...line.matchAll(r.pattern)].map(m =>
      ({ line: i + 1, rule: r.name, severity: r.severity, match: m[0] }))));
  return { ok: !findings.some(f => f.severity === "block"), findings };
}
```

> **Aside: images are where it leaks.** The leak in the migration post came from a screenshot, and this check can't read screenshots. Two options: run OCR on every image before publishing and pass the text through the same rules, or have a rule that terminal and dashboard screenshots are recreated from redacted text instead. The second is more reliable.

> **Aside: the lists are the policy.** The patterns for keys and IP addresses are generic. The customer and codename lists are what make the check specific to you, and they go stale. Generate the customer list from the CRM or billing system instead of maintaining it by hand, so a new large customer is protected on the day they sign.

The same check can run in CI on the blog's repository, so a post can't be merged with a blocking finding. Anything marked "review" goes to a person, who either fixes it or records why it's fine.

## Further Reading

- **Google, _Site Reliability Engineering_, chapter 15, "Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure"** (sre.google). The source for blameless postmortems, and why blame destroys the information you need.
- **Cloudflare, "Cloudflare outage on November 18, 2025"** (blog.cloudflare.com). A detailed public postmortem published the same day, with the causal chain, code and timeline.
- **GitLab, "Postmortem of database outage of January 31"** (about.gitlab.com, February 2017). A public postmortem written after a live, public recovery.
- **Increment, "The benefits of transparency: Interview with Sid Sijbrandij"** (increment.com). GitLab's CEO on why the company chose to recover in public.
- **Dan Luu, "A collection of postmortems"** (github.com/danluu/post-mortems). Hundreds of public incident reports, useful for seeing what good and poor ones look like.
- **The Downtime Project** (downtimeproject.com). A podcast analyzing public postmortems, including GitLab's, with attention to what the reports leave out.
- **Brendan Gregg, "Active Benchmarking"** (brendangregg.com). How to run benchmarks you can defend, and why most published ones are wrong.
- **Julia Evans's blog** (jvns.ca). Clear, honest technical writing about learning and debugging, useful as a model of tone.
- **Google Technical Writing courses** (developers.google.com/tech-writing). Free, short courses on clear technical prose.
- **In this collection:** 2.5 (architecture decision records), 24.3a (incident response) and 24.3b (internal postmortems), 31.4b (architecture documentation), 32.1a (written communication inside a team), 34.1a (breach-notification deadlines), 43.4a (claims versus evidence).

No sibling in another language is proposed: the substance is editorial, and the check would look the same in any language.

---

The habit to take from this: when you read someone else's engineering post, look for the baseline, the rejected alternatives and the cost. When they're missing, you've learned what they did, but not whether you should do it. Write your own posts so readers can make that decision.
