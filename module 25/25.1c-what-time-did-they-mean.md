# What Time Did They Mean? Instants, Local Times, and Time Zones

_Module 25.1c — Internationalization & Localization_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a scheduling platform for language schools. One school has campuses in Mexico City, Madrid and Warsaw, each running in-person classes, plus online lessons with teachers who can be anywhere. Students see a timetable, get reminders, submit homework against due dates, and pay per term. The team adopted the rule most engineers learn early: **store every time in UTC.** Every time column is a `timestamptz`, and the interface converts to the viewer's zone using the viewer object from 25.1b.

Four things go wrong, none of them on the same day.

- **Classes booked months ahead move by an hour.** In September 2022, the Mexico City campus schedules its spring term: evening conversation classes at 18:00, starting in April 2023. The booking converts 18:00 to UTC under the rules of the day. April was daylight saving time in Mexico, UTC−5, so it stores 23:00Z. Five weeks later Mexico abolishes daylight saving time, the servers pick up a tz database update, and April becomes UTC−6. 23:00Z now means 17:00. Every spring class shows 17:00 in the app. Teachers who trust the app arrive an hour early, and students who remember the printed timetable arrive at 18:00. Nothing in the database changed, and every row is wrong.
- **A weekly class drifts when the clocks change.** A Madrid class at 19:00 every Tuesday is stored as a first instant plus a seven-day interval. In winter, 19:00 in Madrid is 18:00Z. After the clocks go forward on 29 March 2026, 18:00Z is 20:00 in Madrid, and the class shows 20:00 for the rest of the summer.
- **Due dates are a day early west of Greenwich.** An assignment due on 15 May is stored as `2026-05-15T00:00:00Z`, midnight UTC. In Mexico City that's 18:00 on 14 May, so students there see "due 14 May", and anything submitted on the evening of the 14th is marked late. Birthday greetings arrive a day early for the same reason.
- **The daily revenue report disagrees with the bank.** The Mexico City campus owner reconciles payments against the bank statement day by day. The report groups by `date_trunc('day', paid_at)` in a UTC database session, so the UTC day ends at 18:00 local and everything paid in the evening lands on the next day. Evening classes are the most popular, so a third of every day's takings is filed under tomorrow, and the month-end totals never match.

The team followed the rule correctly. The rule is right for one kind of time value, and the product had four: moments that already happened, plans at a place, dates with no time of day, and days as someone lived them. UTC is the right answer only for the first.

## What It Is

A few terms do most of the work.

- An **instant** is a point on the global timeline: the moment a payment was captured, the same moment everywhere. UTC is a way of writing instants.
- A **local date-time**, also called wall-clock or civil time, is what a clock on the wall shows somewhere: `2027-04-12 18:00`. On its own it doesn't identify a moment. It needs a place.
- A **time zone** is a named set of rules, such as `America/Mexico_City`, that maps instants to local times for a region, including its whole history and its announced future. An **offset** (`−06:00`) is different: it's the gap between local time and UTC *at one moment*. A zone produces different offsets at different times, and the rules that decide them change. Abbreviations like "CST" are worse still: it means Central Standard Time, China Standard Time, or Cuba Standard Time, depending on who wrote it.
- A **local date** is a calendar date with no time and no zone: a birthday, a due date, a holiday.
- The **tz database** (IANA's, often called tzdata) is the shared data that defines every zone's rules. It's updated several times a year, and every operating system, JDK, browser and database ships its own copy.

The principle this article builds on is: **store what the person meant, in the type that means it, and derive everything else.** A school that says "18:00 in Mexico City" means a local time at a place. The instant is a calculation from that intent, and the calculation's inputs can change.

Two neighbors are covered elsewhere. 10.3a dealt with the *system's* own schedule, meaning when a job should run across daylight-saving changes. 25.1b dealt with showing a time to a viewer. This article is about representing and storing the times users care about.

## Recognizing the Problem

- "Store everything in UTC" applied to future appointments, with no zone column next to them.
- Date-only fields (due dates, birthdays, contract dates) stored as timestamps at midnight.
- `new Date("2026-05-15")` in front-end code.
- A user's time zone stored as an offset (`+02:00`) or an abbreviation (`CET`).
- Recurring events computed by adding 7 × 24 hours.
- "Tomorrow" computed by adding 86,400 seconds.
- Reports grouped by `date_trunc('day', ...)` without an explicit zone.
- Bugs that arrive twice a year, or right after an OS, JDK or database upgrade.
- Events that "moved" after a country changed its clocks.
- The server's default zone (`ZoneId.systemDefault()`, `TZ` unset) used anywhere in business logic.
- Time zone guessed from the user's IP address.
- Laptops and CI disagreeing about test results near midnight.

## How It Works

### Three questions for every time value

Before choosing a column type, ask:

1. **Is it a fact or a plan?** Something that happened is fixed on the timeline. Something that's going to happen is an intention, and intentions are usually expressed in someone's local time.
2. **Is the time of day part of the meaning?** A due date and a birthday have none. An appointment does.
3. **Whose clock?** A place's (the campus), the viewer's, nobody's in particular (a medication reminder at 08:00 wherever the patient happens to be), or the timeline's own (a livestream announced for 17:00 UTC worldwide).

The answers map to a small set of representations:

```
value                                   store                                   Postgres                 java.time
payment captured, message sent          instant                                 timestamptz              Instant
class at a campus next April            local date-time + zone ID               timestamp + text         LocalDateTime + ZoneId
                                        (+ derived instant, for queries)        (+ timestamptz)
global livestream "17:00 UTC"           instant                                 timestamptz              Instant
birthday, due date, holiday             local date                              date                     LocalDate
deadline "end of 15 May, campus time"   local date + zone → instant             date + text              LocalDate + ZoneId
every Tuesday 19:00 in Madrid           rule + local time + zone                text + time + text       expanded to LocalDateTime
medication at 08:00 wherever you are    floating local time                     time                     LocalTime
"1 month trial" vs "48 hours"           calendar period vs elapsed duration     interval                 Period vs Duration
```

The rest of this section explains each row that isn't "store an instant".

### Facts are instants

Anything that already happened is an instant, and UTC is the right way to store it. In PostgreSQL, a `timestamptz` column converts its input to UTC on the way in and to the session's zone on the way out. The name misleads people, because it **doesn't store a time zone**. That's fine for facts. Displaying a fact means converting it to the viewer's zone (25.1b).

If a fact has to be shown "as it was locally", such as a receipt printed with the time at the shop, store the offset or the zone alongside the instant. For the past, an offset is fine: the offset in force at that moment is itself a fact. Offsets are only wrong for the future.

### Plans are local times in a zone

A plan made in local time has to be stored in local time, with the zone. The instant it corresponds to is derived, and it can change after you've computed it:

```
Sept 2022   booked: "2023-04-12 18:00, America/Mexico_City"
            rules then: April is UTC−5            → stored instant 23:00Z

Oct 2022    Mexico abolishes daylight saving time; tz database updated

2023        rules now: April is UTC−6
            stored UTC only:     23:00Z → shown as 17:00     the class "moved"
            stored local + zone: 18:00 → recomputed 00:00Z   the instant moves, the class doesn't
```

Rule changes aren't rare or exotic. Mexico abolished daylight saving time in 2022, Egypt reinstated it in 2023, Kazakhstan moved to a single time zone in 2024, and Brazil dropped daylight saving in 2019. The notice can be short: Egypt once cancelled a clock change three days before it was due.

Queries still need instants ("which classes start in the next hour?" for reminders), so the working design stores the local time and zone as the **source of truth** and a **derived instant** as an indexed column. Whenever the tz database updates, you recompute the derived instant for every future plan. When the recomputed instant differs from the stored one, you also have a list of exactly who needs to hear about it.

**Every multi-zone plan has an anchor zone.** An online lesson between a teacher in Warsaw and a student in Mexico City is stored in one zone, usually the organizer's, and when that zone's rules change, the event moves for everyone in other zones. Calendar systems work the same way. You can't avoid this. You can choose the anchor deliberately and show it ("18:00 Mexico City time"), so the person whose clock it isn't knows which clock to trust.

**Some plans really are anchored to the timeline.** A worldwide livestream, a product launch "at 17:00 UTC", or a trading window defined in UTC should be stored as instants. The test is what should happen if a country changes its rules: should the event keep its local time or its global moment? That answer decides the representation.

**Some local times don't exist, and some happen twice.** On the night the clocks go forward, 02:30 doesn't exist in many zones. On the night they go back, 01:30 happens twice. For plans, this needs a policy. At booking time, refuse a nonexistent time and ask the person what they meant. For an ambiguous time, choose one occurrence deliberately (usually the first). For existing rows that fall into a gap *because the rules changed*, shift forward by the length of the gap, which is what `java.time` does by default. 10.3a has the same two nights from the job scheduler's side.

### Dates are not midnights

A due date or a birthday isn't a moment. Storing it as midnight UTC invents a time and a zone the value never had, and the value then shows up on a different day depending on where it's viewed. That was the opening's off-by-one. Store it as a `date`, send it over the wire as `"2026-05-15"`, and never pass it through a type that means an instant.

JavaScript's `Date` makes this especially easy to get wrong. `new Date("2026-05-15")` is midnight *UTC*, while `new Date("2026-05-15T00:00")` is midnight in the *browser's* zone, so the same product can be off by one day in one screen and correct in the next.

When a date is really a **deadline**, it needs a stated meaning, which is a product decision:

- **End of the day where the course is taught.** Submissions are compared against an instant computed from the date and the campus's zone. It's fair to everyone in one physical place and hard on a student who is travelling.
- **End of the day wherever the student is.** Everyone gets the same local experience, and the actual cut-off is spread over about 26 hours of real time. That's fine for homework, and wrong for an exam with released answers.
- **Anywhere on Earth (AoE).** The deadline is the end of the day in the last time zone to finish it (UTC−12). Academic conferences use this to make "15 May" mean "as long as it's still 15 May somewhere".

### Whose day is it?

Every "day" is a day somewhere, and reports, streaks, daily limits, "today's classes" and billing periods all need to say where. There are three common answers:

- **The viewer's day**, for personal views: my classes today, my streak.
- **An entity's day**: the campus's, or the tenant's accounting day. Revenue reports belong here, because they have to match a bank statement and an accountant who both work in local days.
- **UTC**, for global operational aggregates where no single local day is meaningful.

For the opening's revenue report, grouping by the campus's day is one expression in SQL:

```sql
select (p.paid_at at time zone c.zone_id)::date as local_day, p.currency, sum(p.amount)
from payment p join campus c on c.id = p.campus_id
where p.paid_at >= $1 and p.paid_at < $2          -- the local day's boundaries, as instants
group by 1, 2
order by 1;
```

Two details catch people. **Pre-aggregated rollups fix the zone forever.** If you roll payments up into UTC days, you can never recover Mexico City days from them. Aggregate per entity zone, or at a granularity that divides every offset you serve: hourly buckets don't work for India (UTC+5:30) or Nepal (UTC+5:45), and 15-minute buckets do. **Days aren't always 24 hours long.** Days on which the clocks change are 23 or 25 hours, so "per hour" charts have a missing or doubled hour, and a "once per 24 hours" limit is not the same as "once per day".

### A day is not 24 hours

That last point generalizes. **Durations** are elapsed time: 90 minutes, 48 hours. **Periods** are calendar amounts: one day, one month. They agree most of the time, and they disagree on exactly the days that generate support tickets:

```
start: 2026-03-28 19:00 Europe/Madrid (the night before the clocks go forward)
+ Duration 24 hours  → 2026-03-29 20:00      elapsed time; the wall clock skips an hour
+ Period   1 day     → 2026-03-29 19:00      same wall-clock time, 23 hours later

start: 2026-01-31
+ Period   1 month   → 2026-02-28            there is no 31 February; java.time clamps
```

A class that "happens every day at 19:00" is a period. A token that "expires in 24 hours" is a duration. A "14-day trial" is whichever one your billing terms say it is, so decide (Module 15).

### Recurrence is a rule expanded in a zone

"Every Tuesday at 19:00 in Madrid" is a rule, not a list of instants. Store the rule (the day, the local time, the zone, the start date and the end), and expand it into local date-times in the zone, converting each one to an instant with the rules for *that* date. Adding seven days' worth of seconds to the first instant is exactly the drift from the opening.

The standard vocabulary is iCalendar (RFC 5545): a start with a `TZID`, a recurrence rule (`FREQ=WEEKLY;BYDAY=TU`), exceptions (`EXDATE`) for holidays, and overrides for single occurrences that were moved. Calendar invites you send should carry the zone ID, so the recipient's calendar does the conversion with its own copy of the rules. Real recurrence has many more cases (every second Tuesday, the last Friday of the month, time zone changes midway through a series), and that's why it belongs to a library rather than a hand-written loop.

### Where zones come from

- **For users, from the device, as an IANA ID.** Browsers report it with `Intl.DateTimeFormat().resolvedOptions().timeZone`, and mobile platforms have equivalents. Store it on the profile, let the user override it, and update it (or ask) when the device reports a different zone. Don't use `getTimezoneOffset()`, which is today's offset and not a zone, and don't guess from the IP address, which is wrong for VPN users and travellers in exactly the way that sends 3 a.m. reminders (Module 14).
- **For places, from an administrator.** A campus's zone is set when the campus is created, chosen from a list of zone IDs rather than offsets. It can be suggested from the address, but a person should confirm it.
- **Never from the server.** A container's default zone is usually UTC, a laptop's is wherever the developer lives, and any code that depends on either behaves differently in the two. Pin servers and database sessions to UTC so any default you've missed at least fails consistently, then pass zones explicitly everywhere.

### The tz database is a dependency

The rules live in data that your JVM, your database, your operating system, your users' browsers and their phones each carry separately, updated on separate schedules. After a rule change, they disagree for days or weeks: the server computes one instant, and a phone that hasn't updated shows a different local time for it. Treat tz data like any other dependency whose changes affect correctness: know which version each runtime has, update promptly (the IANA announcements list gives notice), and run the recompute job whenever the version changes. The implementation below records the version that produced each derived instant, so "which rows are stale?" is a query rather than a guess.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Bookings more than a few weeks ahead at physical places:** classes, clinics, venues, tours, travel. The further ahead a plan is, the more likely a rule change or a DST transition falls between booking and the event.
- **Date-only business values:** due dates, birthdays, contract start and end dates, holidays, invoice dates. That covers almost every product.
- **Reports and limits by day** for any customer outside UTC, which is most customers.
- **Recurring schedules** of any kind.
- **Collaboration across zones,** where people see one event on different clocks.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Facts.** Logs, audit trails, payments and messages are instants and nothing more.
- **Short-horizon future times.** A reminder in 20 minutes, a token that expires in an hour, a lease or a retry delay are elapsed durations from now. Instants are the correct representation, and rule changes don't reach them.
- **Per-row zone machinery for a single-location business.** A school with one campus and no online lessons can keep the zone as one configured value. It should still store future classes as local times, because that zone's rules can change too.
- **Floating times before a feature needs them.** Alarms and medication reminders need them; most products never do.
- **A hand-written recurrence engine.** Use a library (ical4j or lib-recur in Java, rrule.js in JavaScript).
- **Showing every time in several zones at once.** One clearly labelled zone per view, plus the anchor zone where it differs, is enough for most people.

## Trade-offs

- **Two representations to keep in sync.** Between a tz update and the recompute job, derived instants are stale, and reminders fire at the old time. If the recompute job is forgotten altogether, you have the opening's bug back. The difference is that it's now recoverable, because the intent is stored.
- **Many copies of the rules.** The JVM, PostgreSQL, the OS and every client can disagree for a while after a change. The server's reminder email and the phone's calendar can show different times for the same class.
- **Rule changes become product events.** Someone has to decide who gets notified and what they're told. The anchor zone means that for somebody, the event really has moved.
- **More types means more decisions.** Every time field needs the three questions answered. ORMs and drivers map types inconsistently, and code review has to ask "which kind of time is this?" instead of assuming.
- **Queries need both forms.** Sorting and reminders need the derived instant. "What's on at 18:00 local at every campus?" needs the local column. Either one alone makes some queries awkward.
- **Per-entity days fragment aggregates.** A global "revenue per day" across campuses in three zones has no single correct answer, and pre-aggregation forces you to decide early.
- **Deadlines need a stated meaning,** and that's a product conversation, not a column type.
- **Testing is harder.** Tests need an injected clock (21.2a), explicit zones, transition dates, and cases that fall in gaps and overlaps. Rule changes are the hardest to simulate. Using real historical transitions, such as Mexico's in 2022, keeps the data honest.

## Alternatives

**UTC everywhere, knowingly.** It's reasonable when plans are short-horizon, all your users are in one stable zone, and a wrong hour is cheap. Write it down as a decision, and keep the zone and tz version at booking time anyway, so that a repair script is possible if a rule change does arrive.

**Instant plus zone ID.** Storing the computed instant and the zone lets you notice that the local time *would* change under new rules, but it can't tell you which of the two the person meant. Adding the original local time resolves that, at the cost of a third column. It's the same information as the design here, from the other direction.

**Offsets instead of zones.** An offset is correct for past events and wrong for future plans, because it records what the rules said once and never learns that they changed.

**Floating local times only.** Store "18:00" with no zone and interpret it everywhere as local. It works for a single location right up to the first remote participant.

**Delegating to a calendar system.** Google and Microsoft calendar APIs, or a dedicated scheduling service, already handle recurrence, exceptions and tz updates. You get correctness for free, and in exchange you're coupled to their model and their availability.

**Zoned strings on the wire (RFC 9557).** The extended format `2027-04-12T18:00:00-06:00[America/Mexico_City]` carries the local time, the offset and the zone together. The offset lets a receiver detect that its rules disagree with the sender's. It's what JavaScript's `Temporal` uses, and it's a good wire format for plans between systems that both understand zones.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0: defaults everywhere.** The server's zone, timestamps for everything.

**Level 1: make time explicit.** Pin the JVM and database sessions to UTC, inject a `Clock`, store facts as `timestamptz`, and ban `systemDefault()` and argument-less `now()` in business code.
_Next when:_ bugs on the days the clocks change, or tests that pass on a laptop and fail in CI.

**Level 2: dates become dates.** Migrate due dates, birthdays and contract dates to `date` columns, and fix the front end so these values never pass through `Date`.
_Next when:_ off-by-one-day reports from users far from UTC.

**Level 3: zones become data.** Users get IANA zone IDs from their devices, and places get zones chosen by administrators. Reports and deadlines use a stated zone.
_Next when:_ customers in a second zone, or the first reconciliation that doesn't match.

**Level 4: plans become intent.** Future bookings are stored as local time plus zone, with a derived instant. Recurrence is a rule expanded in the zone. Bookings in a gap are refused.
_Next when:_ bookings more than a few weeks ahead, or any recurring schedule.

**Level 5: tz data as a managed dependency.** Track the version in each runtime, update the JDK, OS and database promptly, and recompute derived instants and notify people automatically when the version changes.
_Next when:_ the first rule change that affects your users, or right away once Level 4 exists, because rule changes arrive on their own timetable.

## Implementation

Java with `java.time`, over PostgreSQL. `java.time` has a separate type for each kind of value in this article, it makes gap and overlap handling explicit, and it maps directly onto PostgreSQL's types through JDBC. That makes it the clearest place to see the model.

The schema stores intent, facts and derived values as different columns with different types:

```sql
create table class_session (
  id           bigint generated always as identity primary key,
  campus_id    bigint      not null references campus,
  local_start  timestamp   not null,   -- what the school meant: 2027-04-12 18:00
  zone_id      text        not null,   -- 'America/Mexico_City', copied from the campus at booking
  duration     interval    not null,   -- 90 minutes of elapsed time
  starts_at    timestamptz not null,   -- derived from the two above; recomputed on tz updates
  rules_ver    text        not null    -- tz database version that produced starts_at
);
create index on class_session (starts_at);

create table assignment (
  id        bigint generated always as identity primary key,
  course_id bigint not null references course,
  due_on    date   not null            -- a date, not midnight anywhere
);

create table payment (
  id        bigint generated always as identity primary key,
  campus_id bigint        not null references campus,
  paid_at   timestamptz   not null,    -- a fact
  amount    numeric(12,2) not null,
  currency  char(3)       not null
);
```

> **Aside: copying `zone_id` onto the row is a choice.** A copy freezes what was meant at booking time, so correcting a campus's zone later (an administrator picked the wrong one) doesn't move existing classes. Sometimes that's what you want, and sometimes the correction should apply to every future class. Decide which, and make the correction screen say what it will do.

The time rules live in one small class, so every conversion follows the same policy:

```java
final class Times {
    /** Booking: refuse times that don't exist; choose the first of two deliberately. */
    static LocalDateTime validatePlan(LocalDateTime requested, ZoneId zone) {
        ZoneOffsetTransition t = zone.getRules().getTransition(requested);
        if (t != null && t.isGap()) {
            throw new NonexistentLocalTime(requested, zone,
                t.getDateTimeBefore().toLocalTime(), t.getDateTimeAfter().toLocalTime());  // "02:00 → 03:00"
        }
        return requested;
    }

    /** Derived instant under the rules this JVM has right now. May differ after a tz update. */
    static Instant instantOf(LocalDateTime local, ZoneId zone) {
        // Gap (only possible for rows booked before a rule change): moved later by the gap's length.
        // Overlap: the earlier of the two moments, stated rather than left to a default.
        return ZonedDateTime.of(local, zone).withEarlierOffsetAtOverlap().toInstant();
    }

    /** A local day as a half-open range of instants: [start of day, start of next day). */
    static Instant[] dayRange(LocalDate day, ZoneId zone) {
        return new Instant[] {
            day.atStartOfDay(zone).toInstant(),
            day.plusDays(1).atStartOfDay(zone).toInstant(),
        };
    }

    /** "Due 15 May" at this school: before the end of 15 May where the course is taught. */
    static Instant deadline(LocalDate dueOn, ZoneId courseZone) {
        return dayRange(dueOn, courseZone)[1];            // exclusive; no 23:59:59.999
    }

    /** Every <day> at <time> in <zone>: the local time stays put, and the offset does whatever it does. */
    static List<LocalDateTime> weekly(LocalDate from, LocalDate until, DayOfWeek day,
                                      LocalTime at, Set<LocalDate> skipped) {
        return from.with(TemporalAdjusters.nextOrSame(day))
                   .datesUntil(until.plusDays(1), Period.ofWeeks(1))
                   .filter(d -> !skipped.contains(d))     // holidays: the EXDATEs of RFC 5545
                   .map(d -> d.atTime(at))
                   .toList();
    }
}
```

The drifting version from the opening fits on one line: `first.plus(Duration.ofDays(7 * i))`. `Duration.ofDays(7)` means 168 hours of elapsed time, not "next week at the same time". `Period.ofWeeks(1)` applied to a `LocalDate` means the latter.

> **Aside: `atStartOfDay(zone)`, not `atTime(0, 0)`.** In zones that change their clocks at midnight (Brazil did until 2019), midnight doesn't exist on the day of the change. `atStartOfDay(zone)` returns the first moment that does exist, while building midnight by hand and converting it moves you an hour in a direction that depends on the default policy.

The recompute job is what makes storing intent pay off when the rules change:

```java
void recomputeAfterTzUpdate(Instant now) {
    String current = ZoneRulesProvider.getVersions("Etc/UTC").lastKey();   // e.g. "2024b"
    List<Moved> moved = new ArrayList<>();

    // A day of margin: a stale starts_at can be off by an hour or more in either direction.
    for (Session s : sessions.futureWithRulesVersionOtherThan(now.minus(Duration.ofDays(1)), current)) {
        Instant fresh = Times.instantOf(s.localStart(), s.zone());
        if (!fresh.equals(s.startsAt())) moved.add(new Moved(s, s.startsAt(), fresh));
        sessions.updateDerived(s.id(), fresh, current);      // reminders now query the right instant
    }
    // People at the campus see no change: 18:00 is still 18:00. People in other zones
    // (the Warsaw teacher of a lesson anchored in Mexico City) now see a different time.
    notifier.tellParticipantsOutsideAnchorZone(moved);
}
```

Run it at startup whenever the version differs from the last run, and after every JDK or tzdata update. The `rules_ver` column makes it safe to rerun and cheap to check: `select count(*) from class_session where rules_ver <> ?` shows how many rows are stale.

> **Aside: the JVM's rules aren't PostgreSQL's rules.** The report query's `at time zone` uses PostgreSQL's copy of the tz database, while `instantOf` uses the JVM's. They're updated separately, so after a rule change they can disagree about the same local time for a while. Doing all conversions for plans in one place (here, the JVM) and giving the database only instants and local values keeps the disagreement down to reporting, where it's easier to notice.

> **Aside: JDBC and the session zone.** Read `local_start` with `getObject(col, LocalDateTime.class)` and `due_on` with `getObject(col, LocalDate.class)`: neither goes through a zone. Write instants as `OffsetDateTime` at UTC. The PostgreSQL driver sets the session's zone from the JVM's default when it connects, which is one more reason to pin the JVM to UTC even though no code should depend on it.

## Further Reading

- **IANA Time Zone Database** (iana.org/time-zones), its announcements list, and the "Theory and pragmatics" document in the tz distribution, which explains what a zone is, how they're named, and why the data changes.
- **Jon Skeet, "Storing UTC is not a silver bullet"** (codeblog.jonskeet.uk, 2019): the classic worked example of a future event broken by a rule change, and of the local-plus-zone alternative.
- **`java.time` API documentation:** `ZonedDateTime` (the gap and overlap rules), `ZoneRules` and `ZoneOffsetTransition`, `Period` versus `Duration`, and `ZoneRulesProvider`.
- **PostgreSQL documentation, "Date/Time Types" and "AT TIME ZONE":** including its explicit note that `timestamp with time zone` doesn't store a zone.
- **RFC 5545 (iCalendar):** `DTSTART` with `TZID`, `RRULE`, `EXDATE` and `VTIMEZONE`, the shared vocabulary for recurrence.
- **RFC 9557:** the extension to RFC 3339 timestamps that adds a bracketed zone ID.
- **Oracle's TZUpdater tool** and your JDK vendor's tzdata release notes, for updating the JVM's rules without waiting for a new JDK.
- **Noah Sussman, "Falsehoods programmers believe about time"**: short, and a good checklist for code review.
- **In this collection:** 10.3a (the system's own schedules across daylight-saving changes), Module 14 (quiet hours and zones reported by the client), 21.2a (injecting the clock), 21.5 (pinning `TZ` in CI and running in `Pacific/Chatham` on purpose), 25.1b (formatting with an explicit viewer), Module 15 (billing periods: calendar or elapsed).

A **TypeScript** sibling would be worth writing now that `Temporal` is arriving, because JavaScript's `Date` is where most of these bugs are born. `Date` has one type, an instant with methods that silently use the browser's zone, and it parses date-only strings as UTC and date-times without an offset as local. `Temporal` provides `PlainDate`, `PlainDateTime` and `ZonedDateTime` with explicit gap and overlap handling (`disambiguation: "earlier" | "later" | "reject"`). It shipped in Firefox and Chrome and is enabled by default in recent Node, but not yet in Safari, so the article would also need to cover polyfilling and the migration path from `Date`. Not yet written.

The habit to take from this: whenever you store a time, ask what the person meant by it — a moment, a local time at a place, or a date — and store that. Treat everything else, including the UTC instant, as something you calculate from it and may have to calculate again.
