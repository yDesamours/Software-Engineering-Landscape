# Built for Latin: Direction, Length, and Scripts in the Interface

_Module 25.2b — Internationalization & Localization_

## The Problem

Suppose you build a customer-support helpdesk. Agents work through a queue of tickets, open conversations, reply by email, and send customers PDF summaries of their cases. The interface has been English-only, but the helpdesk is used by support teams around the world, so customer messages already arrive in every language. Now the company launches Hebrew and Arabic interfaces for customers in Israel and the Gulf, alongside German and Thai. The messages are properly written and translated (25.1a, 25.2a), and for Hebrew and Arabic someone sets `dir="rtl"` on the page.

- **Half the layout mirrors.** Flexbox rows and paragraphs flip on their own. Everything positioned with `margin-left`, `float` or absolute coordinates stays where it was. The unread badge sits on top of the ticket subject, the sidebar's collapse button is on the wrong edge, and the "back" arrow in the conversation header points forward. Arabic-speaking agents describe it as the English layout with the words moved.
- **Mixed-direction text rearranges itself.** This was already happening in the English interface, and nobody had noticed. A notification reads *Dana replied to "…"* with a Hebrew ticket subject inside the quotes, and the subject's closing exclamation mark shows up on the wrong side of it. In the Hebrew interface, customers' phone numbers display with their groups in reverse order and the `+` at the far end, and an agent dials a wrong number.
- **German buttons are cut off.** "Save changes" becomes "Änderungen speichern", which doesn't fit in the fixed 120-pixel button and displays as "Änderungen spei…". In the reply toolbar, the icon-only fallback kicks in and the labels disappear altogether.
- **Thai and Japanese break in different ways.** Thai vowel marks above the consonants are clipped by the ticket list's fixed 20-pixel rows with `overflow: hidden`. Japanese customers' names appear in Chinese letterforms, because the page declares `lang="en"` and on many systems the browser then picks a Chinese font for Han characters. Japanese agents notice at once.
- **The Arabic PDFs are unreadable.** The PDF library draws Arabic letters one at a time, left to right, unjoined.

Nothing was translated wrong. The stylesheet assumed text runs left to right. The templates assumed that interpolated text runs in the same direction as the sentence around it. The components assumed labels are as short as English ones, the fonts and line heights assumed Latin letters, and the PDF library assumed each character is drawn as one fixed shape. Each of those assumptions is invisible as long as everything is in English.

## What It Is

This is the part of internationalization that happens after the words are right: **making the interface render correctly for any writing system.** It has four concerns.

- **Direction.** Arabic, Hebrew, Persian, Urdu and a few other scripts are written right to left (RTL), so a right-to-left interface is laid out as a mirror image of a left-to-right one.
- **Bidirectional text (bidi)**, meaning text that contains both directions, such as an English sentence quoting a Hebrew subject or an Arabic sentence containing a phone number. The Unicode Bidirectional Algorithm decides the order characters are displayed in. Your job is to tell it where each piece of text starts and ends.
- **Length.** Translated text is longer or shorter than English, sometimes much longer.
- **Script-specific typography**: fonts that contain the right characters, the right letterforms for the language, enough line height for stacked marks, and line breaking that suits scripts without spaces between words.

A distinction runs through all four. A **script** is a writing system (Arabic, Latin, Han, Thai), a **language** is what's written in it, and a **locale** is a language plus regional conventions. Direction belongs to the script: Azerbaijani is written left to right in Latin script and right to left in Arabic script. Letterforms can depend on the language: Japanese and Chinese share many characters but draw some of them differently.

Neighbors: translating messages is 25.2a. Formatting the numbers inside them, including which digits to use, is 25.1b. Counting, truncating and comparing text correctly (graphemes, normalization) is 25.2c.

## Recognizing the Problem

- `margin-left`, `padding-right`, `left:`, `float: left` or `text-align: left` in stylesheets.
- Arrows, chevrons, "reply" and progress icons with no right-to-left variant.
- User-supplied names and subjects interpolated into sentences with nothing marking where they start and end.
- Phone numbers, IDs, URLs and code shown inside right-to-left text.
- Fixed widths on buttons and tabs, and `white-space: nowrap` with an ellipsis on labels.
- Fixed `height` with `overflow: hidden` on anything that holds text.
- `<html lang="en">` hard-coded whatever the interface language, and no `lang` on user content.
- A web font that only covers Latin characters, or boxes (□) where characters should be.
- `word-break: break-all` added somewhere to stop text overflowing.
- `letter-spacing` or `text-transform: uppercase` used for emphasis.
- PDFs, images, charts or canvas drawings with text produced by a library nobody has tested in Arabic.

## How It Works

### Direction starts at the root

```html
<html lang="ar" dir="rtl">
```

A single attribute on the root element sets the base direction for the whole page, and a great deal follows from it automatically: paragraph direction, `text-align: start`, the order of items in flexbox rows and grid columns, table column order, and the inline flow of text. That's why half the helpdesk flipped on its own. The other half was code that named a physical side (left or right) instead of a logical one (start or end).

The direction comes from the locale's script, not from a list of languages. `new Intl.Locale("ar").maximize()` fills in the likely script (`ar-Arab-EG`), and you check whether that script is written right to left. The implementation does exactly this. Set `lang` alongside `dir`, because `lang` does work of its own, as later sections show.

### Logical CSS, not physical

Every physical property has a logical equivalent that resolves against the current direction:

```
physical                 logical
margin-left              margin-inline-start
padding-right            padding-inline-end
left: 0                  inset-inline-start: 0
float: left              float: inline-start
text-align: left         text-align: start
border-left              border-inline-start
border-top-left-radius   border-start-start-radius
width / height           inline-size / block-size
```

Written with logical properties, the badge's `margin-inline-start: auto` pushes it to the end of the row in both directions. A stylelint rule can forbid physical properties in new code, and utility frameworks offer logical classes (Tailwind's `ms-*` and `pe-*`, for example).

Some things have no logical form and need handling by hand. **Transforms and background positions** name physical axes. **JavaScript that positions elements**, such as tooltips, drag-and-drop or popovers that compute `left`, has to read the direction. Positioning libraries that use "start" and "end" placements handle it for you. **Third-party components** such as date pickers, rich-text editors, charts and carousels support right-to-left layout anywhere from fully to not at all, and the time to find out is before you adopt one, not the week of the Arabic launch.

### What to mirror, and what not to

Mirroring is about meaning, not geometry. Things that express *reading order* or *progress through the interface* flip. Things that represent the physical world or a fixed convention don't:

```
mirror                                          don't mirror
navigation order, sidebars, breadcrumbs         media controls (play still points right)
back and forward arrows, list chevrons          clocks and clockwise "refresh" arrows
progress bars, sliders, steppers                checkmarks, logos, most brand marks
reply and forward icons, "send" arrows          phone numbers, code, math, URLs
carousels and swipe direction                   photographs and illustrations of real things
```

Charts are contested. Whether a time axis should run right to left depends on the audience and the kind of data, so ask real users in the target market rather than guessing. In code, the decision belongs with each icon, recorded once in the icon set ("directional: yes") and applied everywhere with CSS, rather than handled screen by screen.

### Bidirectional text: mark the boundaries

The Unicode Bidirectional Algorithm (UBA) assigns every character a type. Letters are **strong**: Hebrew letters are right to left, Latin letters are left to right. Digits are **weak**. Spaces and most punctuation are **neutral**. Runs of strong characters are laid out in their own direction, and each neutral character takes its direction from its neighbors. Inside a single-language sentence, that works perfectly. At the boundary between a sentence and a value inserted into it, the neutral characters have to guess which side they belong to, and they often guess wrong:

- A Hebrew subject ending in "!" inserted into an English sentence: the exclamation mark sits between Hebrew letters and the closing quote of the English sentence, resolves to the sentence's direction, and is displayed on the far side of the subject from where a Hebrew reader expects it.
- A phone number in right-to-left text: the digits in each group stay left to right, but the spaces between groups are neutral in a right-to-left paragraph, so the groups themselves are laid out right to left. `+972 50 123 4567` displays with its groups reversed.
- A name in the opposite direction followed by a count, such as "(3 tickets)": the parenthesis and digits can attach to the name's direction instead of the sentence's.

The fix is the same every time: **isolate every value whose direction you don't control.** An isolated piece of text is laid out as a single unit in its own direction, and it looks like a single neutral character to the sentence around it, so nothing inside it can pull on the sentence and nothing in the sentence can pull on it. The tools depend on the medium:

```html
<!-- user text inside a sentence: <bdi> isolates it and detects its direction -->
<p>Dana replied to “<bdi>{subject}</bdi>”</p>

<!-- an element that is entirely user text: take the direction from its content -->
<td class="subject" dir="auto">{subject}</td>
<textarea dir="auto"></textarea>

<!-- content that is always left-to-right, even on a right-to-left page -->
<span dir="ltr">+972 50 123 4567</span>
<input type="tel" dir="ltr">
```

- `dir="auto"` sets an element's direction from the first strong character in it. That's right for ticket subjects, comments, chat messages, and every input and text area where people might type in either direction.
- For multi-paragraph user content, `unicode-bidi: plaintext` in CSS gives each paragraph its own direction.
- Phone numbers, order IDs, URLs, email addresses and code are left-to-right islands, and should be marked that way explicitly on a right-to-left page.

**Plain text has no markup**, and a surprising amount of text goes out as plain text: email subject lines, push notifications, SMS, the page `<title>`, `aria-label` attributes, text copied to the clipboard. There, Unicode has invisible **isolate characters**: FIRST STRONG ISOLATE (U+2068) before the value and POP DIRECTIONAL ISOLATE (U+2069) after it do what `<bdi>` does. Some message libraries insert them around every placeholder by default (Fluent does). Others leave it to you, and the implementation shows the wrapper.

Invisible characters have costs of their own. They end up in text people copy into search boxes, they count against length limits, and in SMS a single character outside the basic GSM alphabet switches the whole message to a 16-bit encoding, cutting a segment from 160 characters to 70. Add isolates at the last moment, for the medium that needs them. Don't store them, for the same reason 25.1b gave for not storing formatted numbers.

**Directional controls are also an attack.** The older bidi *override* characters (such as RIGHT-TO-LEFT OVERRIDE, U+202E) force a direction onto everything after them. In a file name, `invoice` + U+202E + `fdp.exe` displays as `invoiceexe.pdf`. In source code, the same trick hides logic from reviewers, a technique published as "Trojan Source" in 2021. Strip embedding, override and isolate controls from user-supplied names, file names and titles when you store them, and isolate the text when you display it.

### Text length: design for growth

Translations of English are usually longer, and short strings grow the most, proportionally. An often-quoted IBM guideline, cited by the W3C, estimates that text up to 10 characters can grow to two or three times its English length, 11–20 characters to about twice, and paragraphs over 70 characters by about 30%. "Save changes" (12 characters) becoming "Änderungen speichern" (20) is an ordinary case, not an extreme one. Finnish, Russian and Greek also run long. Chinese, Japanese and Korean are usually much shorter in characters, but they need a larger font size to stay legible, so they don't save as much space as the character count suggests.

What survives expansion:

- **Let things grow.** Use `min-inline-size` instead of `width` on buttons and tabs, allow labels to wrap onto a second line, and let rows be as tall as their content.
- **Don't truncate labels.** An ellipsis on a user's long ticket subject is fine, because the rest is a click away. An ellipsis on the "Save" button hides what the button does.
- **Stop clipping vertically.** Thai, Vietnamese and Burmese stack marks above and below letters, and Arabic and Devanagari reach further up and down than Latin. A fixed `height` with `overflow: hidden` clips them. For those scripts, a line height of around 1.5–1.7 is more comfortable than typical Latin settings.
- **Test with a stand-in before you have translations.** A pseudo-locale (25.2a) that lengthens every message by about 40% and adds accented letters above and below finds most of these problems in a morning.

### Fonts: coverage, weight, and the `lang` attribute

**Coverage.** A brand font often contains only Latin characters. For anything else, the browser falls back to some other installed font, so Cyrillic or Vietnamese appears in a visibly different typeface. Where no installed font has the character, users see "tofu": empty boxes. Pick font families that cover your target scripts (Google's Noto family was designed for coverage), and define a font stack per script using `:lang()` selectors.

**Weight.** Chinese, Japanese and Korean fonts contain tens of thousands of glyphs and run to several megabytes. Either use the operating system's fonts for those scripts, or serve the font in slices using `unicode-range`, so the browser downloads only the parts of the font that the page actually uses.

**`lang` chooses letterforms.** Unicode gives Chinese, Japanese and Korean the same code points for thousands of characters that are drawn slightly differently in each tradition ("Han unification"). Fonts, and browsers choosing fonts, select the right forms from the `lang` attribute, so Japanese text on a page marked `lang="en"` can come out with Chinese letterforms. That's what the Japanese agents saw. The same attribute controls hyphenation, the quotation marks used by `<q>`, case mapping in `text-transform` (Turkish dotted and dotless i), spell checking, and which voice a screen reader uses. Set it on `<html>` from the interface locale, and on blocks of user content whenever you know their language, such as a message from a customer whose profile says Japanese.

**Emphasis doesn't carry across scripts.** Uppercase doesn't exist in Arabic, Hebrew, Thai or the CJK scripts, so `text-transform: uppercase` simply stops being emphasis. Italic isn't a convention in most non-Latin scripts. `letter-spacing` can break the joins between Arabic letters in some renderers. Design emphasis with weight, color or size, which work everywhere.

### Line breaking: let the browser do it

Browsers already break lines correctly for most scripts, provided you don't override them. Thai, Lao, Khmer and Burmese don't put spaces between words, and browsers use dictionaries to find valid break points. Japanese and Chinese can break between most characters, subject to rules about punctuation that the CSS `line-break` property controls. Korean uses spaces, and `word-break: keep-all` keeps its words intact.

The usual damage is a global `word-break: break-all`, added to stop a long URL overflowing a card. It breaks every word in every language at any character. `overflow-wrap: anywhere` breaks only when there's no other opportunity, which fixes the URL and leaves everything else alone. `hyphens: auto` only works if `lang` is set, because hyphenation dictionaries are chosen by language.

### Every surface that draws text

The browser does bidi, shaping (choosing each Arabic letter's connected form from its neighbors) and line breaking for you. Other surfaces may not.

- **HTML email** needs `dir` and `lang` on its outer elements, just like a page. The subject line and the plain-text part need isolate characters around inserted values.
- **PDF generators** often do neither shaping nor bidi unless configured to, and some can't do them at all. That produces the unjoined, reversed Arabic from the opening. Verify with a real Arabic document. The robust option is to render HTML to PDF with a headless browser, which uses the browser's own text engine.
- **Canvas and SVG charts**: canvas has a `direction` property that must be set, and chart libraries vary widely.
- **Text in images** can't be translated, mirrored or read by screen readers. Draw it as text, or produce a separate image for each locale.
- **Native mobile** layouts follow the same principle with their own vocabulary: *leading* and *trailing* constraints on iOS, *start* and *end* attributes on Android.

## When It's Appropriate

- **A right-to-left locale on the roadmap.** Arabic alone has hundreds of millions of readers, and Hebrew, Persian and Urdu are common targets.
- **Any product that shows user-supplied text, even with an English-only interface.** Isolating user content is needed the day the first customer types in Hebrew or Arabic, and helpdesks, CRMs, chat and marketplaces get that customer on day one.
- **Any translation at all**, because every language changes lengths.
- **Chinese, Japanese, Korean or Southeast Asian markets**, for fonts, letterforms and line breaking.
- **Documents and messages generated on a server**: PDFs, emails, SMS and push notifications.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Converting an entire legacy stylesheet to logical properties before any right-to-left locale is planned.** Write new CSS logically, since it costs nothing, and convert the old code when right-to-left layout is actually coming. Isolating user text, however, is worth doing now regardless.
- **Vertical writing** (traditional Japanese or Chinese set in columns). It matters for publishing and reading apps, and application interfaces almost never need it.
- **Custom web fonts for every script** when the operating system's fonts cover them well. Ship web fonts where brand appearance matters, and accept system fonts elsewhere.
- **Mirroring charts and data visualizations** without asking users in the target market.
- **A pixel-perfect design for each locale.** Flexible components designed once for both directions and variable length scale. Hand-tuned screens per language don't.

## Trade-offs

- **Components have two designs.** Every directional component needs its right-to-left behavior specified, and the design system has to show both. Designers who work only in left-to-right layouts miss things that right-to-left readers see instantly, such as a backwards arrow.
- **Two layouts to test.** Screenshot and end-to-end tests roughly double for directional screens, and some bugs only show up with real content in the opposite direction.
- **Flexible layouts are less precise.** Designs that let labels grow and wrap give up exact alignment, and some designers resist that.
- **Invisible characters leak.** Isolate characters in plain text affect copy and paste, search, length limits and SMS cost, and they're hard to see when they cause trouble.
- **Fonts cost bandwidth and time.** Fonts for Chinese, Japanese and Korean are large, and splitting them has a latency cost, and a flash of fallback font while slices load.
- **Third-party components set your ceiling.** A component without right-to-left support is either replaced, patched or wrapped, and each option costs time.
- **Logical CSS has gaps.** Transforms, background positions and positions computed in JavaScript stay physical, so a codebase is never entirely logical, and the exceptions need attention.

## Alternatives

**Generating a flipped stylesheet.** Tools such as RTLCSS read a left-to-right stylesheet and produce a right-to-left one by swapping physical properties, with comments to exempt rules that shouldn't flip. It's a reasonable path for a large legacy codebase, at the cost of a build step and two stylesheets that can drift apart. For new code, logical properties are simpler.

**A component library with built-in right-to-left support.** Several mature design systems handle direction in every component. Adopting one moves most of the work to the library's authors, as long as its components actually match what you need.

**Not supporting right-to-left interfaces.** It's a legitimate business choice. Even then, isolating user content and setting `lang` correctly are still needed, because your users' text arrives in every direction regardless of the interface language.

**Mirroring the whole page with `transform: scaleX(-1)`.** It's sometimes suggested. It mirrors the text as well, which produces backwards letters. It's mentioned here only so it can be ruled out.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0: Latin-only assumptions.** Physical CSS, `lang="en"` hard-coded, fixed-size components, user text inserted with no isolation.

**Level 1: `lang`, `dir` and isolation.** Set `lang` and `dir` on `<html>` from the locale. Put `dir="auto"` on every container and input that holds user text, wrap inserted values in `<bdi>`, and strip override characters from user input. This is worth doing even for an English-only product.
_Next when:_ the first customer who writes in Hebrew or Arabic, which may already have happened.

**Level 2: let text grow.** Remove fixed widths and heights on text, allow wrapping, and run a lengthening pseudo-locale in CI with screenshot tests.
_Next when:_ the first translated locale.

**Level 3: logical CSS and icon metadata.** Enforce logical properties in new code with a lint rule and convert components one by one. Mark each icon as directional or not, and check third-party components for right-to-left support.
_Next when:_ a right-to-left locale is on the roadmap.

**Level 4: right-to-left testing.** Add a mirrored pseudo-locale (Android's `ar-XB` is a model), screenshot tests in both directions, and review by native readers before launch.
_Next when:_ a few weeks before the right-to-left launch.

**Level 5: every other surface.** Isolate inserted values in emails, SMS and push notifications. Render PDFs through a real text engine. Set up font stacks per script with `:lang()`, and check charts and canvas drawings.
_Next when:_ right-to-left or CJK customers start receiving documents and messages, not just screens.

## Implementation

TypeScript for the logic, plus the CSS it drives. Most of this work is markup and styling, so the code is small: working out direction from a locale, isolating inserted values in both HTML and plain text, and cleaning user input.

Direction comes from the script, which `Intl.Locale` can supply for any locale tag:

```ts
// i18n/direction.ts
const RTL_SCRIPTS = new Set(["Arab", "Hebr", "Thaa", "Syrc", "Nkoo", "Adlm", "Rohg"]);

export function directionOf(locale: string): "ltr" | "rtl" {
  const script = new Intl.Locale(locale).maximize().script;   // "ar" → "Arab", "az-Arab" → "Arab"
  return script && RTL_SCRIPTS.has(script) ? "rtl" : "ltr";
}

// at the root, server-side or on locale change:
// <html lang={locale} dir={directionOf(locale)}>
```

A list of right-to-left *languages* gets Azerbaijani, Kurdish and Punjabi wrong in one direction or the other, because each is written in more than one script. A list of *scripts* is short and doesn't change. Some runtimes also expose this directly on `Intl.Locale` as text info, and that can replace the set where it's available.

Inserted values are isolated differently in HTML and in plain text, but at the same point: where messages are formatted.

```ts
// i18n/isolate.ts
const FSI = "\u2068", PDI = "\u2069";                         // first strong isolate, pop directional isolate

/** For plain-text media (email subjects, push, SMS, <title>, aria-label). */
export function isolateForText(args: Record<string, unknown>): Record<string, unknown> {
  return Object.fromEntries(
    Object.entries(args).map(([k, v]) => [k, typeof v === "string" ? FSI + v + PDI : v]),
  );
}

/** For React: wrap string arguments in <bdi> elements; the message library places them. */
export function isolateForHtml(args: Record<string, unknown>) {
  return Object.fromEntries(
    Object.entries(args).map(([k, v]) => [k, typeof v === "string" ? <bdi key={k}>{v}</bdi> : v]),
  );
}

// t(locale, "notify.replied", isolateForText({ actor, subject }))   → push notification body
// intl.formatMessage(messages.replied, isolateForHtml({ actor, subject }))   → the notification list
```

Only strings are wrapped. Numbers and dates go through the formatter (25.1b) and come out in the locale's own form.

> **Aside: `.tsx`, not `.ts`, for the second function.** The JSX in `isolateForHtml` means this file (or that half of it) is a React module. Keeping the plain-text half free of React means email and push code on the server can import it without pulling in the UI library.

User input is cleaned when it's stored, and isolated again when it's shown:

```ts
// i18n/user-text.ts
// Embeddings and overrides (U+202A–U+202E) and isolates (U+2066–U+2069) typed or pasted by users.
// Left-to-right and right-to-left *marks* (U+200E, U+200F) stay: people writing Hebrew and Arabic
// use them legitimately, and they can't reorder text outside the string they're in.
const BIDI_CONTROLS = /[\u202a-\u202e\u2066-\u2069]/g;

export const cleanUserText = (s: string) => s.replace(BIDI_CONTROLS, "");
```

That one line stops the `invoice` + U+202E + `fdp.exe` trick in file names and display names. Rendering with `<bdi>` or `dir="auto"` handles everything that's left.

The stylesheet carries the rest:

```css
/* Rows and badges: logical properties follow the page direction. */
.ticket-row   { display: flex; gap: .75rem; padding-inline: 1rem; }
.ticket-row .badge { margin-inline-start: auto; }            /* was margin-left: auto */

/* Directional icons flip; everything else is left alone. */
.icon[data-directional]:dir(rtl) { transform: scaleX(-1); }

/* Labels grow and wrap instead of being cut off; long URLs still break. */
.button       { min-inline-size: 7rem; white-space: normal; }
.card         { overflow-wrap: anywhere; }                    /* not word-break: break-all */

/* User content finds its own direction, paragraph by paragraph. */
.message-body { unicode-bidi: plaintext; }

/* Scripts with tall stacks get room; Japanese gets Japanese letterforms. */
:lang(th), :lang(vi), :lang(my) { line-height: 1.7; }
:lang(ja) { font-family: "Noto Sans JP", system-ui, sans-serif; }
```

> **Aside: `:lang()` only works if `lang` is set.** Every rule at the bottom depends on the attribute being present, on `<html>` from the interface locale and on user-content blocks when their language is known. It's the cheapest fix in this article, and it fixes the most problems.

> **Aside: render PDFs with a browser.** The unjoined Arabic in the opening came from a PDF library drawing characters one by one. Rendering the same HTML template with a headless browser (for example, Playwright's `page.pdf()`) uses the browser's shaping, bidi and line breaking, and makes the PDF match the page. The cost is running a browser on the server, which is heavier than a PDF library, but it's the difference between Arabic customers being able to read their summaries or not.

## Further Reading

- **W3C Internationalization articles**, especially "Structural markup and right-to-left text in HTML", "Inline markup and bidirectional text in HTML", "Unicode controls vs. markup for bidi support", and "Text size in translation", which is the source of the IBM expansion figures.
- **Unicode Standard Annex #9, "Unicode Bidirectional Algorithm"**, the definitive description of strong, weak and neutral characters and isolates. **Annex #14, "Unicode Line Breaking Algorithm"** is the counterpart for line breaks.
- **Material Design, "Bidirectionality"**: a practical, illustrated guide to what to mirror and what not to.
- **MDN**: CSS logical properties and values, the `:dir()` pseudo-class, `<bdi>`, `unicode-bidi`, and `overflow-wrap`.
- **W3C layout requirements documents**: "Requirements for Japanese Text Layout", "Requirements for Chinese Text Layout", and the Arabic and Persian gap analyses. They're what to read when a native reader tells you something looks wrong and you need to know why.
- **RTLCSS** (rtlcss.com), for generating right-to-left stylesheets from legacy CSS.
- **Nicholas Boucher and Ross Anderson, "Trojan Source: Invisible Vulnerabilities"** (2021): directional control characters as an attack on source code review.
- **Google Noto fonts**: coverage for nearly every script, and a reference for which scripts need special line heights.
- **In this collection:** 25.1a (the messages these values are inserted into), 25.1b (digits and number formats), 25.2a (pseudo-locales in CI, including mirrored ones), 25.2c (counting and truncating text by what users see as characters), Module 14 (emails, SMS and push notifications as surfaces), Module 21 (screenshot testing).

A **Kotlin** sibling for Android would be worth writing, because the platform has named tools for all of this. `android:supportsRtl` with `start` and `end` attributes handles mirroring, and `BidiFormatter.unicodeWrap` is a standard-library function for isolating inserted strings. Android's `ar-XB` pseudo-locale mirrors an app without anyone translating anything. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: whenever you position something, insert a value into a sentence, or give text a fixed box, ask what happens if the text runs the other way, comes out twice as long, or is written in a script whose letters reach higher and lower than Latin.
