# A Form Is a Model of One Country: Names, Addresses, and Phone Numbers

_Module 25.2d — Internationalization & Localization_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a marketplace for handmade goods. Sellers in Japan, Germany, Brazil, Spain, Iceland, Indonesia, Hungary and the United Arab Emirates ship to buyers everywhere. Checkout asks for a first name, a last name, an address and a phone number for the courier. The interface is fully translated (25.1a), with prices and dates in local conventions (25.1b). The forms were designed in the US, and nobody revisited them.

- **Real names are rejected.** "Last name" is required, must be at least two characters, and must match `[A-Za-z'-]+`. That rejects José and Nguyễn for their letters, and the Chinese family name 李 for being one character. An Indonesian seller who, like many Indonesians, has only one name enters it in both fields and becomes "Suharti Suharti" on every shipping label.
- **Greetings and sorting assume Western name order.** A Japanese buyer enters 山田 as the last name and 太郎 as the first, so the order email opens with "Hi 太郎". Addressing a customer by his given name alone is jarringly familiar in Japanese; 山田様 or 山田太郎様 is expected. A Hungarian seller, used to writing his family name first, types "Nagy" in the first-name box and gets greeted as "Hi Nagy". The support tool sorts Japanese customers by the code points of the kanji, which have nothing to do with how the names are pronounced, so Japanese staff can't find anyone in the list.
- **Addresses are shaped like the US.** The form requires a state and a five-digit ZIP code. Buyers in the UAE, where most addresses have no postal code, type "00000". British postcodes like "SW1A 1AA" are rejected. Brazilian addresses lose the *bairro* (neighborhood) because there's no field for it. The label printer puts every address in the American order, so a Japanese address written from prefecture down to building comes out upside down. 3.1% of international parcels come back undeliverable, against 0.4% domestically.
- **Phone numbers lose their country.** The field accepts `^\d{10}$` and stores the digits in an integer column. The leading zero of the UK number "07700 900123" disappears, and the SMS provider, which defaults to the US, sends courier updates to +1 7700 900123. A later "fix" strips a leading zero from every number before adding the country code. That's right for the UK and wrong for Italian landlines, which keep their zero (+39 06 …).
- **The country list is out of date and English-only.** It's a hard-coded array of English names that still includes Swaziland and the Netherlands Antilles, shows English names to German and Japanese buyers, and is stored in the database as those names, so "Czech Republic" and "Czechia" now both appear in the sales reports.

Every field, pattern and template was a small model of one country. None of these broke in the US.

## What It Is

Forms and schemas for personal data encode assumptions about how people are named, how places are addressed, and how phones are reached. Assumptions that hold in one country are false somewhere else. Treating this as data modeling means three things:

- **Store what the person gave you, in their own form.** A name as they write it, an address as it would be written on an envelope in their country, a phone number with its country.
- **Ask only for the structure you'll actually use**, such as a name to greet someone by or a phonetic reading to sort by. Ask for it explicitly rather than trying to derive it.
- **Drive validation and formatting from data per country, not from one pattern.** Standards and maintained datasets exist for exactly this: ITU E.164 for phone numbers, ISO 3166 for countries and their subdivisions, Google's libphonenumber for phone metadata, the address metadata behind Google's libaddressinput, and CLDR for country names in every language.

Neighbors: formatting numbers and dates is 25.1b, sorting and comparing text is 25.2c, and phone numbers inside right-to-left text are 25.2b. What personal data you may collect at all, and why, is a legal question (Module 34). "Ask only for what you'll use" is also data minimization.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Required "First name" and "Last name" fields, with minimum lengths or `[A-Za-z]` patterns.
- Greetings built as `"Hi " + firstName`, or formal ones as `"Dear Mr. " + lastName`.
- A "State" field that is always required, a numeric ZIP pattern, or a single postal-code regex.
- Address labels assembled in one fixed order for every destination.
- Phone numbers stored as integers, stored without a country code, or validated with a digit-count regex.
- Countries stored as English names, or a country list maintained by hand.
- Gender inferred from names or titles.
- Customer lists in Japanese that nobody can search by pronunciation.
- Courier failures and returned parcels concentrated in particular countries.
- Support tickets saying "your form won't accept my name".

## How It Works

### Names: store the name as written, ask for what you'll use

Some real names, and what a form built around "first name, last name" does to them:

```
person                          family name            addressed as             what the US form does
山田 太郎 (Japan)                first: 山田              山田様, or 山田太郎様      "Hi 太郎": far too familiar
Nagy Péter (Hungary)            first: Nagy             Péter (informally)       parts in the wrong boxes
María José García López (Spain) two: García López       Sra. García (López)      second surname dropped
Björk Guðmundsdóttir (Iceland)  none; a patronymic      Björk                    "Ms. Guðmundsdóttir"; sorted by it
Suharti (Indonesia)             none; one name          Ibu Suharti              "Suharti Suharti"
Nguyễn Văn An (Vietnam)         first: Nguyễn           An                       "Mr. Nguyen", a name that ~40% of Vietnamese share
李明 (China)                     first, one character: 李  李先生 (Mr. Li)          rejected as too short
```

There's no split into parts that works for all of these, so the durable model doesn't split by default:

- **Full name**, required, as the person writes it, in any script. This is what goes on labels, receipts and the profile.
- **What we should call you**, optional, prefilled with a guess the person can correct. This is what goes into greetings. It turns "which part is the given name?", which can't be answered in general, into a question the person answers for themselves.
- **A phonetic reading** where sorting needs one. Japanese names written in kanji are sorted and searched by their pronunciation, written in kana (山田 太郎 is やまだ たろう), and Japanese forms routinely ask for it in a second field. Chinese sorting uses pinyin or stroke order, which collation can derive (25.2c). Japanese readings can't be derived reliably, because the same kanji have many readings in names.
- **A Latin-script version** only when something downstream requires it, such as some couriers, payment processors and travel documents, and only as the person writes it. Automatic romanization is lossy and ambiguous: 李 is Li, Lee, Lie, Ri or Yi depending on language and system, and a legal name spelled by an algorithm doesn't match anyone's passport.
- **Structured parts** (given name, family name, and so on) only for processes that genuinely require them, such as bank transfers, airline tickets and identity checks, with labels and order appropriate to the country.

**Validation should reject almost nothing.** Accept letters and combining marks of any script, spaces, apostrophes (both `'` and `’`), hyphens and periods. The minimum is one character, as users see characters (graphemes, 25.2c), and the maximum should be generous: a few hundred characters. Reject control characters and directional overrides (25.2b), and clean the text to NFC (25.2c). A pattern that permits only the names its authors thought of is a list of people you've decided not to serve.

**Formal address is a translation question.** Greeting someone formally depends on language, so it belongs in messages, and the messages need both names. English might use "Hi {calledName}". Japanese uses "{fullName}様", which is correct without splitting the name at all. German might use "Hallo {calledName}", or, in a formal product, a salutation the translator chooses. This is 25.1a's principle again: pass the facts, and let each language decide.

**Titles and gender are not derivable from names.** Offer a title or salutation field only if the product needs one, and make it optional and localized. Never infer gender from a name, a title or a photo. If the product needs gender, for grammar (25.1a) or for a legal or medical reason, ask for it, and offer the options that the relevant jurisdictions recognize (several countries issue passports with an "X" marker, for example).

### Addresses: one model, formats and rules per country

Addresses differ in which parts they have, what the parts are called, which ones are required, and the order they're written in. Google's address metadata, the dataset behind libaddressinput and Chrome's address autofill, describes each country with a format string and a few rules. Abridged:

```
country  format (%N name, %O organization, %A street lines, %D district,     required   notes
          %C city, %S state/province, %Z postal code, %n line break)
US       %N%n%O%n%A%n%C, %S %Z                                                A C S Z    ZIP: 5 digits, optional +4
DE       %N%n%O%n%A%n%Z %C                                                    A C Z      postal code before city; no state
GB       %N%n%O%n%A%n%C%n%Z                                                   A C Z      postcode on its own line; no state
JP       〒%Z%n%S%n%A%n%O%n%N              (Latin form: %N%n%O%n%A, %S%n%Z)   A S Z      largest to smallest; "prefecture"
BR       %O%n%N%n%A%n%D%n%C-%S%n%Z                                            A C S Z    %D is the bairro (neighborhood)
AE       %N%n%O%n%A%n%S                                                       A S        "emirate"; no postal code
```

That table is most of the design:

- **Pick the country first, then render the form.** Choosing the country changes which fields appear, what they're labelled ("State", "Province", "Prefecture", "Emirate", "County"), which are required, what the postal code is called ("ZIP code", "Postcode", "PIN code", "Eircode", "CEP"), and which subdivisions are offered in a list. The labels themselves are translated messages, keyed by the metadata's type names, such as `address.adminArea.prefecture`.
- **Store the union of all fields, with the country code and language.** Every country's address fits one structure: recipient, organization, street lines, dependent locality, locality, administrative area, postal code and sorting code, plus the ISO 3166-1 country code and the language or script the address is written in. Google's own APIs use exactly this structure (`google.type.PostalAddress`). The language matters because a Japanese address may be stored in Japanese for domestic delivery or in Latin script for international shipping, and the two are formatted with different patterns.
- **Format for the destination.** A shipping label is read by the destination country's postal service, so it uses the destination's format. International mail also puts the destination country's name on the last line, in capitals.
- **Check format; verify separately.** Required fields and the postal-code pattern come from the metadata and can be checked instantly. Whether the address *exists* is a separate question. Address verification services (commercial ones, or Google's address validation API) answer it with coverage that varies by country. Use them to suggest corrections, not to block checkout, since new buildings and informal addresses are often missing from their data.
- **Don't treat postal codes as regions.** US ZIP codes are delivery routes rather than areas, UK postcodes don't line up with administrative boundaries, and many countries don't use postal codes at all. Taxes, shipping zones and delivery estimates need their own data (Module 15).
- **Let the browser help.** The HTML `autocomplete` attribute (`name`, `address-line1`, `address-level2`, `address-level1`, `postal-code`, `country`, `tel`) lets browsers fill addresses they already know, in whatever shape the country uses. Names like "state" and "city" are deliberately avoided in favor of numbered address levels, because not every country has states or cities in the US sense.

### Phone numbers: E.164, parsed with a region

A phone number is only meaningful with its country. **E.164**, the ITU's international numbering format, writes every number as `+`, a country calling code, and the national number, up to 15 digits: `+447700900123`. That's the form to store. Keep an extension separately, and never use an integer column, which drops the `+`, any leading zero, and eventually overflows.

Getting from what someone typed to E.164 needs a parser with per-country rules. **libphonenumber**, Google's library, is the standard, with ports for most languages. The rules it encodes are the ones hand-written code gets wrong:

- **Input needs a default region.** "07700 900123" is a UK mobile number only if you know the person is in the UK. Use the country chosen in the form (or the address, or the profile) as the default, and accept input written in international format regardless.
- **Trunk prefixes differ.** Most countries drop the leading 0 when writing a number internationally (UK 07700 → +44 7700). Italy keeps it for landlines (+39 06 …). The library knows which countries do what.
- **+1 is not the United States.** It's the North American Numbering Plan: the US, Canada and many Caribbean countries. The library can tell you which region a number belongs to.
- **"Possible" is not "valid".** A *possible* number has a plausible length for its country. A *valid* number also falls in a range that the library's metadata says has been allocated. Numbering plans change several times a year, so a stale library rejects newly issued numbers as invalid. For sign-up forms, check possibility and then verify ownership by SMS or a call (Module 4). Keep strict validity checks for cases where a wrong number is expensive, and keep the library updated.
- **Number types are uncertain.** The library can often tell a mobile number from a landline, which matters for SMS. In North America it often can't, and it reports "fixed line or mobile". Don't block SMS on a type the data can't determine.
- **Display depends on the viewer.** Show a number in national format ("07700 900123") to someone in the same country, and international format ("+44 7700 900123") to everyone else. Links use `tel:` with the E.164 form. In right-to-left pages the number is a left-to-right island (25.2b).

### Countries are codes, shown in the viewer's language

Store **ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 codes** (`CZ`, `SZ`, `TR`), never names. Display names come from CLDR in the viewer's language: `Intl.DisplayNames` in JavaScript, and `Locale.getDisplayCountry` in Java. Names change: Swaziland became Eswatini in 2018 and Macedonia became North Macedonia in 2019, with their codes unchanged, and CLDR updates follow. Occasionally codes change too: the Netherlands Antilles (`AN`) was dissolved in 2010 into `BQ`, `CW` and `SX`, so stored data can contain codes that are no longer current and need a migration rule.

Some names are political. How certain territories are named, and whether they appear in a list of countries at all, carries legal or commercial weight in some markets. Decide it deliberately for each market, with legal input, and don't inherit whatever an old hard-coded array said. Sort the list with a collator in the viewer's language (25.2c), and put the most likely countries (from the address or the locale) at the top.

Country isn't language, currency or phone region (25.1b). A Belgian user may read French or Dutch, a UK resident may have a French phone number, and a buyer in Germany may ship to Austria.

### Other fields that assume one country

- **Dates of birth.** Not everyone knows theirs exactly, and some official documents record only a year or a placeholder date. Ask for it only if you need it, and then ask whether you need the day.
- **National identity and tax numbers** have per-country formats and check digits, and many countries have no single national number. Validation is per country (for EU VAT numbers, through the EU's own validation service), and billing is Module 15's topic.
- **Sizes and paper.** Clothing and shoe sizes differ between the EU, UK, US and Japan. Printable templates need A4 and US Letter.
- **Input methods.** Japanese, Chinese and Korean are typed through an input method editor (IME), which composes text over several keystrokes before committing it. Search-as-you-type that fires on every keystroke searches for half-composed text, and a form that submits on Enter submits when the user presses Enter to *accept an IME candidate*. Check `event.isComposing` (and use `compositionend` events) before acting on key presses in text fields.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any product with users, customers or recipients in more than one country**, which includes most products that take payments, ship goods, send messages, or let people sign up.
- **Physical delivery.** Returned parcels are the most measurable cost of US-shaped addresses.
- **SMS, voice calls or phone verification** to more than one country.
- **Products whose staff search and sort people**: CRMs, support tools, HR systems, clinic software.
- **Anything that greets people by name.** That's every email template.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Collecting structure you don't use.** Don't split names for a product that only ever displays them, and don't ask for a title nobody reads.
- **Address verification services for digital-only products.** A billing country and postal code may be all that tax and fraud checks require (Module 15), and a full verified address is friction without a purpose.
- **Strict phone validity checks at sign-up.** Possibility plus verification by SMS is more robust and rejects fewer real people.
- **Supporting every country's address format before you ship there.** Driving the form from metadata makes adding countries cheap, but the list you *enable* can follow the business.
- **Building your own address parser or phone number rules.** The datasets are maintained, and updating them is the work. Use them.

## Trade-offs

- **Less structure means some things are harder.** Without a family-name field, "sort by last name" isn't available. That's usually fine, because it was wrong for many people anyway, but some legacy integrations expect structured names, and their fields have to be filled with a documented best effort.
- **Asking more questions costs conversion.** A phonetic reading or a "what should we call you?" field adds friction. Ask for them where they're needed (the phonetic reading in Japan) and prefill where you can.
- **The metadata is a dependency.** Phone and address rules change several times a year, and a library frozen at the version you started with slowly rejects real people. Updating it is routine maintenance, and it needs a test that exercises real numbers and addresses.
- **Forms that change with the country are harder to build and test.** Every supported country is a form variant with its own labels, required fields and validation, and it needs its own test cases.
- **Loose validation lets junk through.** Accepting any name means accepting "asdf". The defense is verification where it matters (email, phone, payment), not a character whitelist that also blocks real people.
- **Formatting for the destination requires knowing it.** Labels, greetings and phone displays all depend on the country, the language, and sometimes the script, and each has to be stored rather than assumed.
- **Political naming is a standing decision**, with legal and commercial consequences, that someone has to own.

## Alternatives

**Free-text addresses.** A single multi-line box plus a country selector accepts every address in the world. It's what many international shops do for countries they don't model in detail. What you give up is validation, structured reporting, and anything that needs the postal code or region as a separate field.

**Parsing free text into fields.** Open-source parsers such as libpostal, trained on global address data, can split a free-text address into components. That's useful for importing messy data or for search, but less reliable than asking for structured input at the point of entry.

**Address autocomplete from a provider.** Map and address APIs suggest complete, structured addresses as people type. It's excellent where their coverage is good, and it needs a manual fallback for everything else.

**Delegating to your payment or shipping provider.** Many checkout and courier services collect and validate addresses and phone numbers in their own hosted forms. You get their maintained rules, at the cost of control over the experience and the data model.

**Full structured names everywhere.** Some regulated domains (banking, travel, government) need legal names in a specified structure. There, collect the parts, with country-appropriate labels and order, *and* keep the full name as written.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0: one country's form.** Required first and last names, a required state, a numeric ZIP, 10-digit phones, and English country names.

**Level 1: stop rejecting people.** Remove character whitelists and minimum lengths from names, make the state and postal code optional unless the country requires them, and store countries as ISO codes shown with CLDR names.
_Next when:_ the first "your form won't accept my name" ticket, or any international sign-ups at all.

**Level 2: phone numbers as E.164.** Parse input with libphonenumber and a default region, store E.164 plus the extension, display by viewer country, and migrate the integer column (reconstructing the country from the address where you have one).
_Next when:_ SMS or calls to more than one country.

**Level 3: names as written, plus what we call you.** Replace the first/last split with a full name and a "called" name. Add a phonetic reading where you serve Japan. Pass both names to messages so each language chooses its form of address.
_Next when:_ customers in markets with different name orders, or the first greeting complaint.

**Level 4: addresses from metadata.** Choose the country first, render fields, labels and requirements from per-country metadata, store the union model with country and language, format labels for the destination, and suggest verified corrections without blocking.
_Next when:_ physical shipping to more than a couple of countries, or returns concentrated in particular ones.

**Level 5: maintenance.** Scheduled updates of phone and address metadata, tests with real sample data for each supported country, a migration rule for retired country codes, and an owner for decisions about territory names.

## Implementation

Java. libphonenumber's reference implementation is in Java, Google's address tooling started there, and the JDK has CLDR country names built in. The snippets are the three models from How It Works: a name that is stored as written, a phone number parsed to E.164 and displayed for the viewer, and an address validated and formatted from per-country metadata.

A name is mostly a decision about what *not* to do:

```java
/** A person's name as they write it, plus what they want to be called. */
record PersonName(String fullName, String calledName, String phoneticName /* nullable */) {
    PersonName {
        fullName = Text.clean(fullName);                        // NFC, no directional controls (25.2c)
        calledName = calledName == null || calledName.isBlank() ? fullName : Text.clean(calledName);
        if (Text.graphemeCount(fullName) < 1 || fullName.length() > 300)
            throw new InvalidName("A name is required.");
        if (fullName.codePoints().anyMatch(cp -> Character.getType(cp) == Character.CONTROL))
            throw new InvalidName("The name contains invisible control characters.");
        // No character whitelist and no minimum beyond one character: 李 and Suharti are both complete names.
    }
}

// Greeting: both facts go to the message; each language picks.
//   en: "Hi {calledName},"     ja: "{fullName}様"     de: "Hallo {calledName},"
String greeting = messages.format(locale, "email.greeting",
        Map.of("calledName", name.calledName(), "fullName", name.fullName()));
```

Phone numbers are parsed with a region, stored as E.164, and formatted for whoever is looking:

```java
import com.google.i18n.phonenumbers.PhoneNumberUtil;
import com.google.i18n.phonenumbers.PhoneNumberUtil.PhoneNumberFormat;
import com.google.i18n.phonenumbers.Phonenumber.PhoneNumber;

record StoredPhone(String e164, String extension, String region, PhoneNumberUtil.PhoneNumberType type) {}

final class Phones {
    private static final PhoneNumberUtil util = PhoneNumberUtil.getInstance();

    /** "07700 900123" with region "GB" → +447700900123. International input ignores the region. */
    static StoredPhone parse(String input, String defaultRegion) throws NumberParseException {
        PhoneNumber n = util.parse(input, defaultRegion);
        if (!util.isPossibleNumber(n)) throw new InvalidPhone(input);   // not isValidNumber: see aside
        return new StoredPhone(
            util.format(n, PhoneNumberFormat.E164),
            n.hasExtension() ? n.getExtension() : null,
            util.getRegionCodeForNumber(n),                  // "GB"; for +1 numbers, "US", "CA", "JM"...
            util.getNumberType(n));                          // MOBILE, FIXED_LINE, or FIXED_LINE_OR_MOBILE
    }

    /** National format for someone dialling from the same country, international for everyone else. */
    static String display(StoredPhone p, String viewerRegion) throws NumberParseException {
        PhoneNumber n = util.parse(p.e164(), null);          // E.164 needs no default region
        boolean sameCountry = n.getCountryCode() == util.getCountryCodeForRegion(viewerRegion);
        return util.format(n, sameCountry ? PhoneNumberFormat.NATIONAL : PhoneNumberFormat.INTERNATIONAL);
    }
}
```

`display` compares *country calling codes*, not regions, so a Canadian viewer sees a US number in national format, which is how people dial between the two.

> **Aside: why `isPossibleNumber` at sign-up.** `isValidNumber` checks the number against the allocated ranges in the library's metadata. When a country opens a new mobile range, every copy of the library older than that update rejects those numbers, and so does every sign-up form using it. Checking possibility (plausible length) and then sending a verification code tests the thing you actually care about: whether the person can receive messages at that number.

> **Aside: migrating the integer column.** The opening's stored digits have already lost their `+` and leading zeros. Where the account has an address, parse the digits with the address's country as the default region, and where the result is a possible number, keep it. Where there's no address, or the result isn't possible, don't guess. Flag the account and ask the person to confirm their number the next time they sign in.

Addresses are one record for every country, validated and formatted from that country's metadata:

```java
record PostalAddress(
        String regionCode,              // ISO 3166-1 alpha-2: "JP"
        String languageCode,            // "ja", or "ja-Latn" for the romanized form
        String recipient, String organization,
        List<String> addressLines,
        String dependentLocality,       // bairro, district
        String locality,                // city, town
        String administrativeArea,      // state, prefecture, emirate
        String postalCode, String sortingCode) {}

/** One country's rules, loaded from the address metadata dataset. */
record AddressFormat(String fmt, String latinFmt, String required, String upper,
                     Pattern postalCode, String adminAreaType, String postalCodeType) {}

final class Addresses {
    static List<String> problems(PostalAddress a, AddressFormat f) {
        List<String> out = new ArrayList<>();
        for (char field : f.required().toCharArray())
            if (isBlank(value(a, field))) out.add("missing:" + field);          // UI label from f's type names
        if (!isBlank(a.postalCode()) && f.postalCode() != null
                && !f.postalCode().matcher(a.postalCode().strip()).matches())
            out.add("postal-code-format:" + f.postalCodeType());               // "postal", "zip", "pin"...
        return out;
    }

    /** Lines for a label, in the destination's own order. */
    static List<String> label(PostalAddress a, AddressFormat f, boolean latin, Locale printedIn) {
        String fmt = latin && f.latinFmt() != null ? f.latinFmt() : f.fmt();
        StringBuilder sb = new StringBuilder();
        for (int i = 0; i < fmt.length(); i++) {
            char c = fmt.charAt(i);
            if (c != '%') { sb.append(c); continue; }
            char field = fmt.charAt(++i);
            String v = field == 'n' ? "\n" : value(a, field);
            sb.append(f.upper().indexOf(field) >= 0 ? v.toUpperCase(Locale.ROOT) : v);
        }
        List<String> lines = sb.toString().lines()
            .map(String::strip).filter(l -> !l.isEmpty() && !l.equals("〒") && !l.equals(","))
            .collect(Collectors.toCollection(ArrayList::new));
        // International mail: destination country on the last line, in capitals.
        if (!a.regionCode().equals(printedIn.getCountry()))
            lines.add(Locale.of("", a.regionCode()).getDisplayCountry(printedIn).toUpperCase(printedIn));
        return lines;
    }

    private static String value(PostalAddress a, char field) {
        String v = switch (field) {
            case 'N' -> a.recipient();          case 'O' -> a.organization();
            case 'A' -> String.join("\n", a.addressLines());
            case 'D' -> a.dependentLocality();  case 'C' -> a.locality();
            case 'S' -> a.administrativeArea(); case 'Z' -> a.postalCode();
            case 'X' -> a.sortingCode();        default -> "";
        };
        return v == null ? "" : v;
    }
}
```

The same `label` call produces an American block ("SPRINGFIELD, IL 62701" on one line), a German one (postal code before the city), a Japanese one (from the postal code and prefecture down to the recipient), and a Brazilian one with the *bairro* on its own line, without an `if` for any country. Adding a country means loading its metadata, not writing code.

> **Aside: the country line is in the *sender's* language.** The label's last line tells the postal service in the country of *origin* where to send the parcel, so it's written in a language that postal service reads, which is why `label` takes the locale it's printed in. Everything above that line is for the destination's postal service, in the destination's format and script.

> **Aside: the metadata itself.** Google publishes the address data that libaddressinput uses as a public JSON service with one entry per country, and the library bundles and caches it. Either way, treat it like phone metadata: pin a version, update it on a schedule, and keep a test address per supported country that must still validate and format correctly after each update.

## Further Reading

- **Patrick McKenzie, "Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names"** (2010): the list that started the genre, and still the fastest way to question a name field.
- **W3C Internationalization, "Personal names around the world"**: name structures by region, with concrete guidance on form design.

- **Google libaddressinput** and its address metadata service: per-country formats, required fields, postal-code patterns and subdivision lists. Also **`google.type.PostalAddress`**, the storage model used across Google's APIs.
- **Universal Postal Union addressing standards**, and its per-country descriptions of postal addressing systems.
- **libphonenumber**, especially its FAQ and its **"Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Phone Numbers"** document, which explains most of the rules above. Also **ITU-T Recommendation E.164**, the numbering standard itself.
- **ISO 3166** (country and subdivision codes, via ISO's Online Browsing Platform), and CLDR's territory names, as exposed by **`Intl.DisplayNames`** and **`Locale.getDisplayCountry`**.
- **The HTML specification's "Autofill" section**: the `autocomplete` tokens for names, addresses and phone numbers, and why they use address levels instead of "state" and "city".
- **libpostal**: open-source international address parsing, for imports and search.
- **In this collection:** 25.1a (passing facts to messages, and formal address), 25.1b (country is not currency or locale), 25.2b (phone numbers and names in bidirectional text), 25.2c (cleaning, comparing and sorting names), Module 4 (verifying phone numbers), Module 14 (SMS delivery), Module 15 (billing addresses and tax IDs), Module 34 (data minimization and what you may collect).

A **TypeScript** sibling for the front end would be worth writing, because half of this lives in the form. An address form that re-renders fields and labels when the country changes, `autocomplete` tokens that let browsers fill it, libphonenumber-js formatting a number as it's typed, and IME-aware key handling are all browser problems. The server-side model here only works if the form collects what it needs. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: whenever you add a field about a person or a place, ask which country you pictured while designing it. Then either drive the field from per-country data, or stop requiring the structure that picture assumed.
