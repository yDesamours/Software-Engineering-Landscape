# Which Locale, Says Who? Negotiating and Carrying the Locale

_Module 25.3a — Internationalization & Localization_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a marketplace for booking local tours and activities. Public pages list tours and are found through search engines. Customers sign in to book, and they get confirmation, reminder and cancellation emails. Tour operators run their side of the business in the same app. The product supports American and British English, German, French, Spanish for Spain and for Latin America, Brazilian and European Portuguese, Simplified and Traditional Chinese, and Japanese. The messages are translated (25.2a). How the product picks a language grew one piece at a time.

- **Language follows the IP address.** The first version picked the language from geolocation. A German family on holiday in Lisbon gets Portuguese. Visitors in Geneva get German, because the lookup table maps Switzerland to its most-spoken language. An American on a company VPN that exits in Frankfurt books in German. The language switcher is a row of flags, so the Genevans look for a Swiss flag that means French, and Mexican users wonder why Spanish is represented by Spain's flag.
- **Fallback picks the wrong neighbor.** A later version matches the browser's language exactly, and otherwise strips the region. `es-MX` isn't in the list, so it becomes `es`, Spain's Spanish, although Latin American Spanish is supported and is what Mexicans expect. `zh-HK` becomes `zh`, which the code maps to Simplified Chinese, the mainland script, for Hong Kong users who read Traditional characters. `en-AU` becomes `en`, which means American English, and because the same value drives formatting, Australians see 4/3/2026 for 4 March. That's the date bug from 25.1b, reintroduced by a fallback.
- **Emails go out in the wrong person's language.** When a German-speaking operator cancels a tour, the cancellation job is enqueued from the operator's request and carries the operator's locale, so 30 customers from Japan, Brazil and the US get a cancellation email in German. Reminder emails are sent by a scheduled job with no request at all, so they go out to everyone in the server's default, English.
- **The CDN serves one visitor's language to the next.** Public tour pages choose a language from the `Accept-Language` header without telling the CDN, so a French visitor gets the German page that the previous visitor caused to be cached. The fix, `Vary: Accept-Language`, splits the cache by the exact text of the header. The logs show over 4,000 distinct values. The hit rate drops from 91% to 38%, and traffic to the origin servers triples.
- **Search engines only ever see English.** Public pages redirect visitors to `/de/`, `/fr/` and so on based on `Accept-Language`. Search crawlers mostly don't send that header, so they're always redirected to English. German and Japanese tour pages never appear in German or Japanese search results, and the localized landing pages get no organic traffic.

Every part of the system answered "which locale?" on its own, from whatever it had to hand: an IP address, an exact string match, the server's default, whoever triggered the job. And that one answer was being used for several different things at once: which translation to show, how to format dates, and what the cache stored.

## What It Is

**Locale negotiation** chooses the best locale for a person by combining what they've told you, directly or through their device, with what you actually support. **Locale propagation** carries that decision, once made, through every layer that produces text: request handlers, templates, background jobs, emails, notifications and caches.

Four terms carry most of the weight:

- A **BCP 47 language tag** (RFC 5646) names a locale: a language, optionally a script, optionally a region, plus extensions. Examples: `en-AU`, `zh-Hant-HK`, `sr-Latn`, `es-419` (Spanish for Latin America), `en-GB-u-hc-h23` (British English with a 24-hour clock).
- **Language matching** finds the best supported tag for a requested one. Exact matching and "strip the region" are the naive versions. CLDR defines a matching algorithm based on distances between languages, scripts and regions, which knows, for example, that an Australian is better served by British than by American English.
- A **fallback chain** is the sequence of locales to try when a particular message isn't available in the chosen one, such as `es-MX` → `es-419` → `es` → `en`.
- The **viewer** is the resolved result: the settings everything downstream uses to produce text for one person. 25.1b introduced it as an object passed to formatters. This article is about where its values come from.

Messages (25.1a) and formatting (25.1b) consume the locale. Time zones (25.1c) are resolved alongside it. Whether a given piece of *content* exists in a locale is 25.3b.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Language chosen by geolocation.
- `Accept-Language` compared to your list by exact string, or by splitting on `-`.
- `zh` mapped to one script, or `pt` and `es` mapped to one region, without looking at the rest of the tag.
- Date and number formats that change when a translation falls back.
- Background jobs or emails using the server's default locale, or the locale of whoever triggered them.
- `Vary: Accept-Language` on cached pages, or pages that change language with no change in their URL.
- Automatic redirects based on `Accept-Language` or IP address.
- A language switcher made of flags, or language names shown in the current interface language.
- Each service resolving the locale in its own way.
- Users who change their language and find some emails still arriving in the old one.

## How It Works

### "Locale" is several settings

The opening's bugs share a root: one value was standing in for several decisions that have different sources and change for different reasons.

```
setting            decides                                  usual source                            override
UI language        which translations                       choice, URL, device languages           language switcher
formatting region  dates, numbers, first day of week,       the region in the user's own tag        "regional format"
                   hour cycle, units                        (en-AU → AU), even if UI falls back
time zone          what "18:00" and "today" mean            the device (25.1c)                      profile setting
market             catalog, prices, taxes, legal terms      billing or shipping country (Mod. 15)   country/market picker
currency           how an amount is labelled                the amount itself (25.1b)               never "the user's"
```

The UI language and the formatting region are the pair most often conflated. You might translate into British English only, but an Australian reading British English should still get Australian dates, and a Mexican reading Latin American Spanish should get Mexican number formats. Formatting data (CLDR) exists for hundreds of locales, far more than you'll ever translate into, so the formatting locale should be built from **your** language and the **user's** region.

### Where the signals come from, in order

Different signals are reliable for different questions:

1. **An explicit choice.** A language the person picked in your switcher, saved on their profile if they're signed in, or in a cookie if they aren't. Nothing else is as reliable, and nothing should override it silently.
2. **The URL, on public, shareable pages.** `/de/touren/lissabon-altstadt` is German for everyone who opens it: the person who shared it, the person they sent it to, and search crawlers. A link should show what the sender saw.
3. **The device's languages.** The browser's `Accept-Language` header and `navigator.languages` on the web, and the operating system's app-language settings on mobile (both iOS and Android now let users set a language per app). These are a ranked list, not a single value, and they're usually right. They're sometimes wrong in ways the user can't easily fix, such as an English-only corporate laptop used by a German speaker, which is why the explicit choice comes first.
4. **An organization's default**, in B2B products where the customer company sets a default language for its staff.
5. **Geolocation: never for language.** An IP address tells you roughly where a device is, and that's weak evidence for currency, market or shipping country. It says almost nothing about language: Switzerland has four national languages, travellers are abroad, expatriates live abroad, and VPNs route traffic through other countries. At most, it can suggest a market in a dismissible banner.
6. **Your default.**

The explicit choice and the URL can disagree, for example when a German customer opens a French link from a friend. Show the page in French, because that's what the URL promises, and offer a one-click banner: "Diese Seite auf Deutsch ansehen?".

### Tags: parse them, don't split them

`Accept-Language: fr-CH, fr;q=0.9, de;q=0.8, en;q=0.5` is a ranked list with quality weights. Parse it with a library rather than splitting on commas and hyphens. Tags need **canonicalizing** too: case normalized (`en-us` → `en-US`), deprecated codes replaced (`iw` → `he`, `in` → `id`), and redundant parts removed. Store canonical tags on profiles, so that comparisons, cache keys and analytics all agree.

Tags can also carry preferences as **Unicode extensions** after `-u-`: `hc` for the hour cycle, `ca` for the calendar, `nu` for the numbering system, `fw` for the first day of the week, and `rg` for a region override. A user who chooses a 24-hour clock can be represented as `en-US-u-hc-h23`, and formatters that understand the extension (`Intl` does) apply it without any extra code.

### Matching: the closest, not an exact string

Exact matching fails for nearly everyone who didn't choose one of your exact tags, and "strip the region" fails in specific, predictable ways. CLDR's matching algorithm fills in likely subtags and measures distances. Using the opening's supported list:

```
requested    exact / strip-region            CLDR matching           why
en-AU        en → American English           en-GB                   Commonwealth English is closer
es-MX        es → Spain                      es-419                  Latin American Spanish exists and is closer
zh-HK        zh → Simplified                 zh-Hant                 Hong Kong's likely script is Traditional
pt-AO        pt → (whichever pt was first)   pt-PT                   Angolan Portuguese is closer to European
nb           no match                        (nb or no, if offered)  Norwegian Bokmål and "no" are treated as equivalent
fr-CH, de    fr-CH: no match → default en    de                      the user's *second* choice beats your default
```

Two properties matter as much as the distances. **The whole list counts.** A French-Swiss user who also reads German should get German before your default English, because they said so. **Script mismatches are expensive.** A matcher should never serve Simplified Chinese to someone who asked for Traditional without saying so. Treat a low-confidence match as "we don't have this language": fall back to your default, and show the switcher prominently rather than guessing across a script boundary.

### Fallback chains: per message, and never across scripts

Matching chooses the UI language. Individual messages can still be missing in it, and 25.1a's `t()` needs a chain to walk:

```
es-MX user  →  UI language es-419  →  chain:  es-419  →  es  →  en
en-AU user  →  UI language en-GB   →  chain:  en-GB   →  en
zh-HK user  →  UI language zh-Hant →  chain:  zh-Hant →  en          (never zh-Hans)
```

This makes **regional catalogs cheap.** `es-419` only needs the messages that differ from Spain's Spanish (*ustedes* rather than *vosotros*, *computadora* rather than *ordenador*), and `en-GB` only needs the ones where British English differs from American (*colour*, *cancelled*, *holiday*). Each regional catalog overlays its parent, and a missing entry falls through to the parent. CLDR records which locale is the parent of which: `es-MX`'s parent is `es-419`, not `es`, and `en-AU`'s parent is `en-001`, international English.

Fallback happens **per message**, never within one. A message is shown completely in one language. And the formatting locale doesn't follow the chain: the Australian gets British text with Australian dates at every step.

### Decided once, carried everywhere

The negotiation runs **once per request**, in middleware, and the result goes into the request's context. Everything downstream reads the viewer from there: message lookup, formatters, templates, `<html lang>` and `dir` (25.2b), and the `Content-Language` response header. Nothing reads a process-wide default. In Go the context is an explicit parameter, in Node it's `AsyncLocalStorage`, and in Java it's a request-scoped object, which needs care when work moves between threads.

**Background work resolves the recipient, not the actor.** A job that sends a message carries *who* it's for, never *which language*. When it runs, it loads the recipient's stored locale and time zone and resolves a viewer for them, at the moment the message is written. That fixes both of the opening's email bugs: the operator's German context never reaches the customers' emails, and the reminder job has a viewer for each customer instead of the server's default. It also handles the cases in between correctly: a customer who switches language after booking gets the reminder in the new one, and a locale you've since retired falls back through today's matcher rather than yesterday's catalog.

This only works if the locale is **stored with the person**, not just in a browser cookie. Save it on the profile when a signed-in user changes language. Save it on the booking for guest customers, who had a session when they booked and won't when the reminder is sent. People who have never used the product, such as someone who has been invited, get the inviter's locale as a guess, a link in the email to switch language, and their own browser's languages as soon as they click through.

**Between services**, pass the resolved viewer rather than re-negotiating. A service that renders an email for the booking service should receive the recipient's resolved tags, in the job payload or in an internal header or trace context, instead of guessing again from partial information. Treat those values as internal: never trust a viewer passed in by an external caller without checking it against the supported list.

**Logs and internal errors stay in one language** (25.1a). API errors carry codes that clients translate (Module 7). An API that does return display text, such as a backend rendering content for a mobile app, accepts `Accept-Language`, returns `Content-Language`, and says so in its documentation.

### URLs, caches and crawlers

**Public, indexable pages put the locale in the URL**, and `/de/...`, `/fr/...` and so on are separate, stable pages. The URL *is* then the cache key, so no `Vary` header is needed, the CDN hit rate is unaffected, and every link shows the language it promises. Each page lists its equivalents in the other languages so search engines can connect them:

```html
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="de" href="https://example.com/de/touren/lissabon-altstadt">
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="fr" href="https://example.com/fr/visites/lisbonne-vieille-ville">
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="es-419" href="https://example.com/es-419/tours/lisboa-casco-antiguo">
<link rel="alternate" hreflang="x-default" href="https://example.com/tours/lisbon-old-town">
```

**Don't redirect automatically** from a neutral URL to a language based on `Accept-Language` or IP. Crawlers, and shared links, end up in the wrong place. Serve the neutral page (the `x-default`) and show a banner offering the visitor's language, or make the neutral URL a language chooser.

When a page must adapt to the header without a URL change, **negotiate at the edge**. An edge function runs the same matcher against the same supported list and adds the *resulting* locale, one of your eleven, to the cache key. That gives eleven cache variants, not 4,000. Raw `Vary: Accept-Language` caches by the header's exact text and destroys the hit rate, which is what happened in the opening.

**Signed-in pages** get the locale from the profile and don't need it in the URL. Any server-side cache of rendered fragments must include the locale in its key (Module 13's cache-key table).

### The switcher

- **Show each language in itself**: "Deutsch", "Español (Latinoamérica)", "中文（繁體）", "日本語". A person looking for their language may not read the current one. CLDR's display names provide these names (Go's `display.Self`, or `Intl.DisplayNames` constructed in each language).
- **No flags.** A flag is a country, not a language. There's no right flag for English, Spanish, Arabic or Portuguese, and choosing one takes a side. Flags are fine in a *country* or *market* picker.
- **Make it reachable from every page**, with an icon recognizable in any language (a globe, or "文A").
- **Keep the page.** Switching language on `/de/touren/lissabon-altstadt` should land on the French version of the same tour, which needs a mapping between translated URLs (25.3b), not a jump to the home page.
- **Keep language and market separate** where they're separate decisions: a German speaker in Zürich wants German text and Swiss prices.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any product with more than one supported language.** Negotiation, propagation and the recipient rule are the minimum.
- **Regional variants or scripts:** English, Spanish and Portuguese in several regions, and Chinese in two scripts. Proper matching and fallback chains are the difference between "close enough" and "the wrong country".
- **Anything sent outside a request**: emails, push notifications, SMS, PDFs and exports generated by background jobs.
- **Public pages meant to be found through search in several languages**, which need locale URLs, `hreflang` and no automatic redirects.
- **Cached, server-rendered pages in several languages.**

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Locale prefixes in URLs for signed-in application pages.** Nobody finds them through search, and the profile already says which language to use. They add routing complexity with no benefit.
- **CLDR-grade matching for two languages with no regional variants.** If you support English and Japanese, a prefix match on the language subtag is enough. The matcher starts earning its keep with the first regional variant.
- **Edge negotiation when you already have locale URLs.** The URL has done the negotiation.
- **Per-user formatting overrides nobody has asked for.** The region's defaults, plus a 12/24-hour choice, cover most people (25.1b).
- **Geolocation for language, at all.**

## Trade-offs

- **Locale URLs multiply routes and maintenance:** translated slugs, a sitemap per language, redirects when slugs change, and shared links that fix a language for whoever receives them. In exchange you get cacheability, search visibility and predictable links.
- **Separating the UI language from the formatting region adds a setting to explain and tests to run.** "British English with Australian formats" is correct, and it's one more combination that can break.
- **Regional overlays save translation cost** but add fallback layers, and a change to the parent catalog affects every regional child that doesn't override it. Each regional variant needs its own review.
- **Resolving the recipient at send time costs a lookup per message.** For bulk sends, batch the lookups. The upside is that it's always the current preference.
- **Every guess is sometimes wrong, and every question is friction.** Banners and switchers are the compromise, and they need restraint: a banner offered on every page is worse than a wrong guess.
- **Consistency across services takes discipline.** If two services negotiate independently, they'll eventually disagree, and a customer will get a German confirmation for a booking made in French. Share one resolver library and one list of supported locales, or pass the resolved viewer along.
- **Stored preferences go stale.** A locale saved years ago may not be supported anymore, or a better regional variant may now exist. Re-matching stored preferences against today's supported list handles both.

## Alternatives

**Ask on first visit.** A full-screen language chooser before anything else is explicit and never wrong. It's friction for everyone, including the majority whose browser would have answered correctly. It suits kiosks, installers and apps with a small number of very different languages.

**A domain per market** (`example.de`, `example.fr`). Country-code domains send a strong local signal to users and search engines, and let markets differ in catalog and legal terms. They cost more to operate: certificates, sessions and cookies across domains, and duplicated infrastructure. They also still need a language choice inside multilingual countries.

**Subdomains** (`de.example.com`). These are functionally similar to path prefixes for caching and search, with cookie and cross-origin details to handle. Which to use is mostly an operational preference.

**Device language only, with no setting in the product.** It's the simplest option and correct for most people. It fails for shared and corporate devices, and for people whose device language isn't their preferred reading language. An explicit choice is cheap insurance.

**Machine-translated long-tail locales**, to cover languages you don't translate professionally, are a translation decision (25.2a, 25.3b), but they interact with negotiation: mark them clearly in the switcher, and rank them below human-translated alternatives the user also reads.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0: defaults and exact matches.** The server's default locale, `Accept-Language` compared by string in some places, and geolocation in others.

**Level 1: one resolver, one viewer, carried in context.** Middleware negotiates once, and everything reads the viewer from the request context. Remove every reliance on the process default. Set `<html lang>`, `dir` and `Content-Language` from the viewer.
_Next when:_ a second supported language.

**Level 2: real matching and chains.** Canonicalize tags, use a CLDR matcher over the user's whole list, build fallback chains from parent locales that never cross scripts, and build the formatting locale from your language plus the user's region.
_Next when:_ regional variants, Chinese in two scripts, or complaints from users in regions you don't translate for specifically.

**Level 3: store the preference, resolve the recipient.** Save the locale on profiles and guest records, and make every job and email resolve the recipient's viewer when it runs.
_Next when:_ emails and notifications, which means immediately.

**Level 4: public pages.** Put the locale in URLs, add `hreflang` alternates and an `x-default` page, remove automatic redirects in favor of banners, and key CDN caches on the negotiated locale.
_Next when:_ search traffic matters in a second language, or cached pages start serving the wrong language.

**Level 5: settings as separate decisions.** Separate language, market and regional-format controls, organization defaults for B2B customers, regional overlays, and one resolver shared by every service.
_Next when:_ multi-market commerce, or B2B customers who want to set defaults for their staff.

## Implementation

Go, with `golang.org/x/text/language`. Its matcher implements CLDR's matching algorithm, including likely subtags, regional distances and script protection, and Go's explicit `context.Context` makes propagation visible: a function either receives the viewer or it doesn't. The same design fits other languages, but this is where the mechanics are easiest to see.

The supported list and the resolver:

```go
package locale

import (
	"net/http"
	"time"

	"golang.org/x/text/language"
)

// The first entry is the default. Order otherwise doesn't matter to the matcher.
var supported = []language.Tag{
	language.AmericanEnglish, language.BritishEnglish,
	language.German, language.French,
	language.EuropeanSpanish, language.LatinAmericanSpanish,
	language.BrazilianPortuguese, language.EuropeanPortuguese,
	language.SimplifiedChinese, language.TraditionalChinese,
	language.Japanese,
}
var matcher = language.NewMatcher(supported)

type Viewer struct {
	UI       language.Tag   // one of `supported`: which catalog to use
	Format   language.Tag   // our language, the user's region: which conventions
	TimeZone *time.Location // 25.1c
	Sure     bool           // false: a guess worth offering the switcher for
}

// Resolve gathers signals in priority order and lets the CLDR matcher weigh them.
func Resolve(r *http.Request, u *User) Viewer {
	var wanted []language.Tag
	add := func(s string) {
		if t, err := language.Parse(s); err == nil {
			wanted = append(wanted, t)
		}
	}
	if p := fromPath(r.URL.Path); p != "" { add(p) }                            // URL on public pages
	if u != nil && u.Locale != "" { add(u.Locale) }                            // saved choice
	if c, err := r.Cookie("locale"); err == nil { add(c.Value) }               // anonymous choice
	if tags, _, err := language.ParseAcceptLanguage(r.Header.Get("Accept-Language")); err == nil {
		wanted = append(wanted, tags...)                                       // device, already ranked by q
	}
	if u != nil && u.Org.DefaultLocale != "" { add(u.Org.DefaultLocale) }      // organization default
	// No geolocation here, deliberately.

	_, i, conf := matcher.Match(wanted...)
	ui := supported[i]
	return Viewer{
		UI:       ui,
		Format:   formatTag(ui, wanted),
		TimeZone: timeZoneFor(u, r),
		Sure:     conf >= language.High,
	}
}

// formatTag keeps our language and script but takes the user's own region, where they gave one:
// en-GB text for an en-AU user formats as en-AU; zh-Hant for zh-HK formats as zh-Hant-HK.
func formatTag(ui language.Tag, wanted []language.Tag) language.Tag {
	base, _ := ui.Base()
	for _, t := range wanted {
		if b, _ := t.Base(); b != base {
			continue
		}
		if region, conf := t.Region(); conf == language.Exact {
			if f, err := language.Compose(ui, region); err == nil {
				return f
			}
		}
		break
	}
	return ui
}
```

With this list, `Accept-Language: en-AU` resolves to `UI: en-GB, Format: en-AU`. `es-MX` resolves to `UI: es-419, Format: es-MX`. `zh-HK` resolves to `UI: zh-Hant, Format: zh-Hant-HK`. And `fr-CH, de;q=0.8` resolves to German, because the user's second choice is better than your default. None of those needed a special case.

> **Aside: `Sure` drives the banner.** A low-confidence match means the matcher fell back to the default, or only found a distant relative. That's the moment to show the switcher, or a banner listing the user's own languages that you *do* support, rather than presenting a guess as if it were an answer.

Propagation is two small functions and a middleware:

```go
type ctxKey struct{}

func WithViewer(ctx context.Context, v Viewer) context.Context {
	return context.WithValue(ctx, ctxKey{}, v)
}

// MustViewer has no default on purpose. Code that produces text without a viewer
// is a bug, and a panic in tests is better than English in production.
func MustViewer(ctx context.Context) Viewer {
	v, ok := ctx.Value(ctxKey{}).(Viewer)
	if !ok {
		panic("locale: no viewer in context")
	}
	return v
}

func Middleware(next http.Handler) http.Handler {
	return http.HandlerFunc(func(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
		v := Resolve(r, userFrom(r))
		w.Header().Set("Content-Language", v.UI.String())
		next.ServeHTTP(w, r.WithContext(WithViewer(r.Context(), v)))
	})
}
```

A background job carries who a message is for, and resolves their viewer when it runs:

```go
// The job names a booking. It never carries a language.
type CancellationNotice struct{ BookingID int64 }

func (h *Handlers) SendCancellation(ctx context.Context, job CancellationNotice) error {
	b, err := h.bookings.Get(ctx, job.BookingID)
	if err != nil {
		return err
	}
	// The customer's stored locale (profile, or the guest booking's), matched against
	// today's supported list: a retired locale falls back correctly, a new one is used.
	v := ForRecipient(b.Customer.Locale, b.Customer.TimeZone)
	ctx = WithViewer(ctx, v)
	return h.mail.Send(ctx, b.Customer.Email, "booking.cancelled", map[string]any{
		"tour": b.TourTitle(v.UI), "date": b.StartsAt, // formatted with v.Format in v.TimeZone
	})
}

func ForRecipient(stored, tz string) Viewer {
	wanted := []language.Tag{}
	if t, err := language.Parse(stored); err == nil {
		wanted = append(wanted, t)
	}
	_, i, conf := matcher.Match(wanted...)
	loc, err := time.LoadLocation(tz)
	if err != nil {
		loc = time.UTC // and report it: a recipient without a zone is a data problem (25.1c)
	}
	return Viewer{UI: supported[i], Format: formatTag(supported[i], wanted), TimeZone: loc, Sure: conf >= language.High}
}
```

The operator who cancelled the tour isn't mentioned anywhere in this code, and that's the point. Their German request context ended when the job was enqueued, and nothing about the job depends on it.

> **Aside: the tour title is content, not a message.** `b.TourTitle(v.UI)` asks for the tour's title in the customer's language, and it may not exist yet, because an operator writes tours in their own language. Choosing what to show then (the original, a machine translation, or the operator's language with a note) is content translation, not message fallback, and it's 25.3b's topic.

> **Aside: the switcher's labels come from the same package.** `display.Self.Name(tag)` from `golang.org/x/text/language/display` returns each language's name in that language ("Deutsch", "日本語"), so the switcher's list is generated from `supported` and can't drift from it.

## Further Reading

- **RFC 5646 (BCP 47), "Tags for Identifying Languages"**, and **RFC 4647, "Matching of Language Tags"**: the tag grammar, and the basic lookup and filtering schemes that CLDR's matching improves on.
- **Unicode UTS #35 (LDML)**, Part 1, on Unicode locale extensions (`-u-hc`, `-u-rg`, `-u-fw` and the rest) and **language matching**, together with CLDR's likely-subtags and parent-locale data.
- **`golang.org/x/text/language` documentation**, especially the matcher's description of confidence levels and how it handles regions and scripts. For JavaScript, see `@formatjs/intl-localematcher`. For Java, see `Locale.lookup` with `LanguageRange`, or ICU's `LocaleMatcher`.
- **MDN, `Intl.Locale`** (including `maximize()` and extension options), **`Intl.getCanonicalLocales`**, and **`Intl.DisplayNames`**.
- **Google Search Central, "Managing multi-regional and multilingual sites"** and the **`hreflang`** documentation: how crawlers treat locale URLs, why automatic redirects hurt, and how `x-default` works.
- **MDN, `Content-Language`, `Accept-Language` and `Vary`**, the HTTP side of negotiation and why varying on a raw request header fragments caches.
- **W3C Internationalization, "Language tags in HTML and XML"** and **"Choosing a language tag"**: practical guidance on which tag to use, and why.
- **In this collection:** 25.1a (the `t()` whose fallback chain this builds), 25.1b (the viewer object, and currency belonging to the value), 25.1c (time zones resolved alongside the locale), 25.2b (`lang` and `dir` set from the viewer), 25.3b (content that may not exist in the chosen locale), Module 7 (error codes that clients translate), Module 13 (the locale in cache keys), Module 14 (email and notification pipelines), Module 15 (markets, prices and taxes as separate decisions).

A **TypeScript** sibling would be worth writing, because on Node the same design depends on `AsyncLocalStorage` rather than an explicit parameter, and that changes how you find the places that lose the viewer. Code that escapes the async context, such as a callback registered in one request and fired in another, or a pooled connection's event handler, can read another request's viewer silently rather than failing. On the client, it's `navigator.languages`, lazily loaded catalogs, and keeping server and client rendering on the same viewer (25.1b's hydration mismatch). Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: whenever code produces text for a person, ask whose locale it's using and where that came from. The answer should always be the person reading it, resolved once from signals they control, and never whatever happened to be nearby.
