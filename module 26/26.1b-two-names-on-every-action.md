# Two Names on Every Action: Impersonation, Suspension, and Account Locks

_Module 26.1b — Admin Dashboard & Internal Tools_

## The Problem

Suppose you run payroll software for small businesses. Owners add employees, approve timesheets, store bank details, and run payroll every two weeks. There are about 4,000 customer companies and a support team of twelve.

Support has two powerful buttons. **"Log in as"** on the admin account page creates an ordinary session for the chosen user and opens the product in a new tab. **"Suspend"** sets `companies.suspended = true`, and the login page refuses users of suspended companies. Both were built in an afternoon, and both work in the sense that they do what their labels say.

Over two months:

- **The disputed bank change.** An owner reports that her own bank details were changed the day before payroll, and an employee's pay went to the wrong account. The audit log says she did it herself. It was actually a support agent, logged in as her to reproduce a form bug, who saved the form with test data. The log can't tell the difference because the session *was* her.
- **The email from nobody.** While logged in as an owner, an agent clicks "Approve" on a timesheet to see what error appears. The employee receives "Dana approved your timesheet." Dana didn't.
- **The unknown device.** An owner opens her security settings and finds an active session from a city she's never been to, 22 days old. It's the support office. She reports a breach, rotates her password and calls her bank. Nothing was wrong, but nobody can prove that to her in a way she believes.
- **Metrics that lie.** The customer-success team flags a company as "highly engaged, low churn risk." Its only recent activity was support logging in 40 times to investigate why the product kept failing for them. They cancel the next week.
- **The question you can't answer.** A larger customer's security questionnaire asks: *Can your staff access our account? Will we be told? Can we see a record of it?* The honest answers are yes, no and no.
- **The suspension that didn't suspend.** A fraudulent company is caught using stolen card details to pay for accounts that then submit fake payroll. Support clicks Suspend. The login page now refuses them, but their existing sessions keep working for days, their API key keeps submitting payroll runs, and the scheduled payroll job runs on Friday as planned, because nothing in the job checks the flag.
- **The suspension that went too far.** A company two weeks late on payment is suspended by the dunning process with the same flag. Its employees now can't log in to download the payslips they need for a mortgage application, which in some jurisdictions the employer, and arguably you, are obliged to provide.

Two separate mistakes are behind these. **Impersonation erased one of two identities**: every action had an actor and a subject, and the system recorded only the subject. **Suspension was one flag checked in one place**, while the account could act through half a dozen other paths, and different reasons for stopping an account needed different things to stop.

## What It Is

**Impersonation** is a staff member using the product as a specific customer user sees it, usually to reproduce a problem that depends on that user's data, permissions or configuration. Done properly, it's **two-principal identity**: every request carries both the **subject** (the user whose view and permissions apply) and the **actor** (the staff member actually present), and everything downstream records both.

It comes in two strengths that are worth naming separately. **View-as** renders the product with the subject's permissions and data but allows no writes. **Act-as** allows actions, within limits. Most support needs are met by view-as.

**Suspension** and **locks** are the opposite capability: stopping an account from acting. The word covers several different states, such as a security lock on one user, suspension of a whole company for fraud, and restriction for non-payment. They differ in what they stop and what they leave running.

A neighbor to separate: **delegated access** (17.3a) is a third-party app acting for a user with authority the user granted. Impersonation is your own staff acting as a user, often without any grant from the user at all. They share the two-principal shape, and in fact RFC 8693's `act` claim models both, but the trust and consent questions are very different.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Your "log in as" creates a normal session that is indistinguishable from the user's own.
- Audit logs, activity feeds or notifications attribute support actions to the customer.
- Support sessions show up in the customer's device or session list, or last for days.
- "Last active" dates and engagement metrics move when support investigates an account.
- You can't tell a customer whether, when, or why your staff accessed their account.
- Suspending an account is checked at login only.
- API keys, OAuth tokens, webhooks or scheduled jobs keep running for suspended accounts.
- The same "suspended" state is used for fraud, non-payment and security incidents.

## How It Works

### Impersonation

**1. Carry two principals, everywhere.** The impersonation session is a distinct kind of credential that names both identities. Authorization, audit, domain events, notifications and analytics all receive the pair, not just the subject.

```
 normal session          impersonation session
 ┌──────────────┐        ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
 │ subject: Dana│        │ subject: Dana                   │
 └──────────────┘        │ actor:   staff:priya (support)  │
                         │ mode:    view-as | act-as       │
                         │ reason:  ticket #88213          │
                         │ expires: 30 min, no refresh     │
                         └─────────────────────────────────┘
```

The rule to enforce is that **nothing downstream may see the subject without the actor.** The dangerous bugs are in background work: an action taken during impersonation enqueues a job, the job carries only the user ID, and the actor is lost by the time the email goes out. The actor has to travel with the job payload and the domain event.

**2. Authority is the intersection, and some actions are off the table.** An impersonating staff member can do at most what the subject can do, *and* at most what their impersonation mode allows. On top of that, some actions should be **blocked during impersonation regardless of mode**, because they're exactly what an attacker or a mistake would do:

- Changing credentials or recovery options: password, login email, MFA, recovery codes.
- Changing where money goes or comes from: bank details, payment methods.
- Moving money or making commitments: running payroll, approving payments, accepting terms.
- Minting access: creating API keys, authorizing OAuth apps, inviting admins.
- Bulk export or deletion of data.

If support genuinely needs one of these, it should be a **named admin operation** (26.1a), with its own permission, reason, preview and customer notification, not something done by clicking around as the customer.

**3. Session hygiene.** An impersonation session should be short (15 to 60 minutes), non-refreshable, tied to the staff member's own session so it ends when theirs does, and excluded from the customer's device list or shown there clearly labeled. The product shows a **persistent banner** ("You are viewing as Dana Ruiz. Exit") so no one forgets which window they're in. Starting impersonation requires a reason, and ideally a ticket link.

**4. Side effects are labeled or suppressed.** Decide, per effect, what happens when the actor isn't the subject:

- **Notifications** caused by an act-as action say so: "Support (Priya, on ticket #88213) approved this timesheet on Dana's behalf." Or they're suppressed, if the action was a reproduction.
- **Activity feeds and audit logs** show both names.
- **Analytics and engagement signals** are tagged with the actor and excluded from product metrics, and "last seen" timestamps are not updated.
- **Third-party scripts** in the product, like session replay, chat widgets and marketing pixels, are disabled during impersonation. Otherwise your staff's browsing of customer data gets shipped to vendors under the customer's identity.

Masking (26.1a) still applies. Seeing the product as Dana sees it doesn't mean seeing her employees' full bank account numbers.

**5. Consent and transparency.** There's a range of positions, and customers increasingly ask which one you take:

| Model | How it works | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Terms of service only | Staff may access; the policy says so | Small consumer products, early stage |
| Customer-visible access log | Every impersonation appears in the customer's own settings, with who, when and why | Most B2B products; cheap and very reassuring |
| Per-tenant setting | Tenant admin chooses: support access off, on, or on with approval | Mid-market B2B |
| Explicit grant | The customer grants access for a window ("support can access for 72 hours") | Sensitive data, regulated industries |
| Approval per session | A tenant admin approves each access request | Enterprise contracts; this is what large cloud providers sell as access approval |

The access log is the step that most improves trust per unit of effort. It turns "did your staff look at my account?" from an uncomfortable question into a page the customer can open. Keep a separately documented **break-glass** path for incidents where waiting for consent isn't acceptable, and log its use loudly (26.3).

### Suspension and locks

**6. Different reasons stop different things.** One flag can't express the difference between "we think this is fraud" and "they're two weeks late paying." Name the states and write down, for each, what it stops:

| Effect | User security lock | Restricted (non-payment) | Suspended (abuse/fraud) |
|---|---|---|---|
| New logins | blocked (that user) | allowed | blocked |
| Existing sessions | revoked (that user) | kept | revoked |
| API keys, OAuth tokens | that user's revoked | read-only | revoked |
| Outbound webhooks, integrations | continue | continue | paused |
| Scheduled jobs (payroll runs) | continue | paused | paused |
| Read access, data export | blocked | allowed | blocked, except by request |
| Data retained | yes | yes | yes, with legal hold if needed |
| What the customer is told | "locked, verify to restore" | "update payment to restore" | a minimal notice, no detection details |

The exact entries are product decisions. The important part is that they're *decided*, per state, in one place. The restricted column matters: an account that can still read and export its own data is much less likely to become a dispute or a legal problem than one that's been locked out entirely.

**7. Enforce at every entry point.** An account can act through many doors: interactive sessions, API keys, OAuth tokens, webhook callbacks, inbound email, public share links, scheduled jobs and queued jobs created before the suspension. The status check belongs where **every credential type is resolved**, plus at the start of every job's execution, not only on the login form.

Two timing details:

- **Existing sessions and tokens must be revoked or rechecked.** A long-lived JWT carrying "company active" stays valid until it expires. Either check the account's status on each request (with a short cache, a few seconds to a minute), or keep tokens short-lived and revoke refresh tokens at suspension.
- **Jobs check at run time, not enqueue time.** The payroll run was scheduled while the company was active. It has to look again before it moves money.

**8. Suspension is reversible and never deletes.** Deletion is a separate, deliberate process with its own retention rules (24.4, 5.3). Every suspension records a coded reason, who or what triggered it (a staff member, the dunning process, a fraud rule), and the path back. Automated suspensions, such as for non-payment, come with grace periods and warnings (15.3). Security locks on an individual user usually come with a self-service recovery path.

## When It's Appropriate

- **View-as** becomes worth it once a meaningful share of tickets are "it looks wrong for me," where the cause depends on the user's permissions, locale, data or feature flags. Descriptions and screenshots get you partway; seeing it resolves the ticket in one step.
- **Act-as** is worth it when support regularly needs to walk through a multi-step flow to find where it fails, and the steps can't be reproduced on a test account.
- **The consent and access log machinery** is worth it as soon as you sell to businesses that send security questionnaires, or handle health, financial or children's data.
- **Named suspension states** are worth it as soon as you have two different reasons to stop an account. Most products reach that point with their first fraud case and their first failed payment.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Small teams with few customers.** A screen-sharing call where the customer drives is often better than impersonation. The customer sees what you see and nothing happens without them.
- **Act-as by default.** Most investigations are served by view-as. Adding write access "because support might need it" is a lot of risk for little benefit.
- **Consent workflows nobody asked for.** Per-session approval slows every ticket. Start with a clear policy and a customer-visible access log, and add grants or approvals when contracts require them.
- **A full state matrix on day one.** A single suspension flag checked centrally, at every credential type and job start, is a perfectly good first version. The central check matters more than the number of states.

## Trade-offs

- **Impersonation is the most dangerous admin feature.** It gives staff the ability to see and do what customers see and do, and it's the most direct path for an insider misusing access, or an attacker who has phished a support agent. Every safeguard above narrows that, but none removes it.
- **Two principals touch the whole codebase.** Audit, events, notifications, jobs and analytics all need to carry the actor. It's easy to get the synchronous path right and miss one job type, and that one job is where misattribution reappears.
- **Restrictions frustrate support.** Blocking bank-detail changes during impersonation means some tickets need an escalation, or a named operation, instead of a quick fix. That friction is intentional, but it should be measured, because too much of it leads people to find workarounds.
- **Consent reduces helpfulness.** Waiting for a customer's approval can turn a five-minute fix into a two-day one. Customers who ask for approval usually accept that; customers who didn't ask may not.
- **Per-request status checks cost something.** A lookup on every request adds latency and a dependency. Caching it reintroduces a window in which a suspended account can still act. You're choosing the length of that window.
- **Suspension has collateral effects.** Pausing a company's scheduled payroll protects you from fraud and hurts every employee of a legitimate company that was suspended by mistake. The state design has to make false positives cheap to reverse.

## Alternatives

**Screen sharing and co-browsing.** The customer drives, support watches, and nothing happens without the customer. No two-principal machinery is needed, and consent is built in. It needs a live customer, which doesn't scale and doesn't work for asynchronous tickets.

**Session replay.** Reviewing a recording of what the user did shows the problem as it happened. It's read-only and asynchronous, but it has its own serious privacy questions: recordings capture whatever was on the screen, so masking has to be configured carefully.

**Diagnostics on the account page** (26.2a). Showing the user's effective permissions, active flags, recent errors and failed jobs often answers "why does it look wrong for Dana?" without seeing her screen at all.

**Reproduce on a synthetic tenant.** Cloning the relevant configuration (not the data) into a test account gives engineers a safe place to act. It works well when the problem depends on configuration, and poorly when it depends on the specific data.

**For suspension:** revoking specific credentials (one API key, one user's sessions) is often more proportionate than suspending the whole account. Rate limiting (18) can hold back abusive traffic while you investigate.

## How to Achieve It

**Impersonation**

**L0 — no impersonation.** Screen sharing and account-page diagnostics.

**L1 — view-as only.** A distinct, short-lived, read-only credential carrying both principals, with a banner, a required reason and an audit record. Writes are rejected at the authorization layer, not hidden in the UI. *Signal:* tickets that repeatedly need "can you send a screenshot of what you see?"

**L2 — act-as with limits.** The restricted-action list, actor propagation through jobs and events, labeled or suppressed notifications, analytics excluded, third-party scripts disabled. *Signal:* support needs to walk through flows, not just look.

**L3 — customer-visible access log and a per-tenant setting.** *Signal:* the first security questionnaire, or the first customer who asks.

**L4 — grants and approvals, and just-in-time permission to impersonate** (26.3). *Signal:* enterprise contracts that require it, or regulated data.

**Suspension**

**L0 — one flag, checked centrally.** Every credential type and every job start checks it. Existing sessions are revoked when it's set. *Signal to do this immediately:* you have a suspend button at all.

**L1 — named states with a written effect matrix.** *Signal:* the second distinct reason for stopping an account.

**L2 — automated triggers** from billing and fraud rules, with grace periods, customer messaging per state, and a reversal path that's as easy as the suspension. *Signal:* suspensions happen often enough that staff shouldn't be the ones doing them.

## Implementation

Go, because context propagation makes the two-principal rule visible: the principal lives in the request's `context.Context`, and anything that drops it is visibly dropping it.

```go
type Principal struct {
	Subject UserID           // whose permissions and data apply
	Actor   *StaffActor      // nil for a normal session
	Mode    ImpersonationMode // ViewAs or ActAs; ignored when Actor is nil
	Reason  string
}

func (p Principal) Impersonated() bool { return p.Actor != nil }

// Actions nobody may take while impersonating, whatever the mode.
var blockedWhileImpersonating = map[Action]bool{
	ChangePassword: true, ChangeLoginEmail: true, ChangeMFA: true,
	ChangeBankDetails: true, RunPayroll: true, CreateAPIKey: true,
	ExportAllData: true, AcceptTerms: true,
}

func Authorize(ctx context.Context, action Action, res Resource) error {
	p := PrincipalFrom(ctx)
	if !permissions.Allows(p.Subject, action, res) { // the subject's own rights
		return ErrForbidden
	}
	if p.Impersonated() {
		if blockedWhileImpersonating[action] {
			return ErrNotDuringImpersonation // use a named admin operation instead
		}
		if p.Mode == ViewAs && action.IsWrite() {
			return ErrReadOnlyImpersonation
		}
	}
	return nil
}

// Every domain event carries both names, so audit, notifications and
// analytics downstream can't attribute a support action to the customer.
func NewEvent(ctx context.Context, kind string, payload any) Event {
	p := PrincipalFrom(ctx)
	return Event{Kind: kind, Payload: payload, Subject: p.Subject, Actor: p.Actor, At: time.Now()}
}
```

> **Aside: the actor dies at the queue boundary.** A handler that enqueues `SendApprovalEmail{UserID: p.Subject}` has just discarded the actor, and the email will say Dana approved it. The job payload has to carry the whole `Principal` (or the event that contains it), and the notification code has to branch on `Actor != nil`. This is the bug that survives every code review of the synchronous path.

Suspension is enforced where credentials become principals, for every kind of credential, and again when jobs start:

```go
// Called by the session, API-key, OAuth and share-link authenticators alike.
func (a *Authenticator) checkAccount(ctx context.Context, company CompanyID, via CredentialKind) error {
	st, err := a.status.Get(ctx, company) // cached for ~30s: the suspension window you're accepting
	if err != nil {
		return err // fail closed
	}
	if !st.Allows(via) { // the effect matrix, e.g. Restricted allows sessions, not payroll
		return AccountStateError{State: st.State, Message: st.CustomerMessage()}
	}
	return nil
}

// Jobs were scheduled when the account was active; check again before acting.
func RunPayroll(ctx context.Context, job PayrollJob) error {
	st, err := accountStatus.GetFresh(ctx, job.CompanyID) // uncached: money is about to move
	if err != nil {
		return err
	}
	if !st.Allows(ScheduledPayroll) {
		return jobs.Park(job, st.State) // held, not dropped; resumes on reinstatement
	}
	return payroll.Execute(ctx, job)
}
```

The payroll check skips the cache on purpose. A 30-second window is acceptable for page views and a real risk for a payment run.

## Further Reading

- **RFC 8693, OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange**, especially the `act` (actor) claim. A standard way to express "this token is for Dana, and Priya is acting," usable even if you never implement the rest of the RFC.
- **Google Cloud's Access Transparency and Access Approval documentation**, and **Microsoft's Customer Lockbox documentation**. Mature, public examples of the access log and per-access approval models, and a good picture of what enterprise customers will expect.
- **OWASP's Session Management Cheat Sheet**, for session lifetime, revocation and the problems with long-lived tokens.
- **Stripe's documentation on account restrictions and requirements**, a public example of graduated states (restricted before disabled) with the customer told exactly what's needed to restore access.
- Within this map: session storage and revocation in **4.4**, product roles in **4.3**, tenant deletion and retention in **5.3**, dunning and grace periods in **15.3**, delegated access and the actor question in **17.3a**, rate limiting in **18**, audit logs in **24.4**, named admin operations in **26.1a**, account diagnostics in **26.2a**, and permissions, just-in-time access and break-glass in **26.3**.

A Java sibling with Spring Security would be worth writing. Spring's `SwitchUserFilter` implements impersonation in a few lines of configuration, and it's close to the naive "log in as" in this article's Problem section: it swaps the security context and records the original user as an extra authority. Showing how to extend it into the two-principal model, with the actor carried into events and blocked actions enforced, would show exactly how far a framework feature gets you. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying forward: **for any action, ask "who did this?" and check that the system can give two answers when there are two.** A system that can only name the account will attribute every support mistake, every automated job and every compromised credential to the customer, and you'll be the one explaining it.
