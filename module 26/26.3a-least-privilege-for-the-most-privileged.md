# Least Privilege for the Most Privileged: Permissions for Staff

_Module 26.3a — Admin Dashboard & Internal Tools_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a marketplace where businesses hire freelancers. Freelancers verify their identity with a government ID, clients pay into escrow, and the platform pays freelancers out to their bank accounts. About 150 staff use the admin surface: an outsourced first-line support team in two countries, an in-house second-line team, trust and safety, finance, sales, and engineering. The admin surface is well built by the standards of this module: named operations, previews, reasons, an audit log, two-principal impersonation. Permissions are the weak part. There are two roles, `support` and `admin`.

Over a year:

- **Everyone sees everything.** Every first-line agent, including contractors at the outsourcing vendor, can open any freelancer's identity documents and full bank details. Almost no first-line ticket needs either.
- **The ex-partner.** An agent looks up her ex-partner's freelancer profile, including his new home address from his ID document. It comes to light only because he complains after she mentions something she shouldn't know. The read was in the audit log. Nobody reviews reads.
- **The payout.** A finance staff member changes a freelancer's payout bank account "at the freelancer's request," then approves the pending payout himself. The request was fake. His role allows both steps, and nothing checks that the same person did them.
- **Access that never ends.** An employee who moved from finance to sales still has finance permissions eight months later. Three contractors who left the outsourcing vendor still have working admin accounts two months later, because admin accounts are local to the admin tool and the vendor never told anyone they'd left.
- **Forty superusers.** Every engineer has `admin` "for debugging." One engineer's laptop is compromised through a malicious browser extension, and for two days an attacker has a session with every permission in the company.
- **The shared emergency password.** Because only two people can grant roles, there's a break-glass account for emergencies. Its password is in a shared vault. It was used 31 times last year. Nobody can say by whom, or why.
- **The review nobody reads.** For the SOC 2 audit, managers receive a spreadsheet of 400 access rows each quarter. They approve all of them in about five minutes.
- **The self-grant.** One of the two people who can grant roles grants himself finance access "temporarily" to look into a payment question, and never removes it. Nobody is told.

The thread here: **the people with the most power in the system had the least structure around it.** Staff access was broad, permanent, and self-administered, and the controls that existed either weren't applied (reads), weren't connected to anything (local accounts), or were theater (the review).

## What It Is

This is **staff authorization**: applying least privilege to the users who can step outside the product's rules. It's more than a role list. A complete version has these parts:

- **Permissions on operations and data classes**, grouped into roles that match job functions.
- **Scope**: which accounts a permission applies to.
- **Time**: standing access for routine work, **just-in-time** elevation for risky work.
- **Approvals and separation of duties**, so no single person can complete a dangerous sequence alone.
- **Lifecycle**: access that follows people as they join, move and leave.
- **Emergency access** that's fast and still accountable.
- **Monitoring** of what staff actually do with their access.

The neighbor is **product authorization** (4.3). The mechanisms are the same family, roles and attributes and policies, but three things differ. Staff permissions cross tenants, so scope is a question of which customers, not which resources inside one customer. Time-limited access is normal for staff and rare for customers. And staff can often change the permission system itself, so **permissions over permissions** need their own rules. Audit logs themselves, how to make them complete and tamper-evident, are covered in 24.4. This article is about what to record about staff specifically, and who looks at it.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Staff roles are few, broad and named by seniority ("admin", "super admin") rather than by job.
- Outsourced or first-line staff can see data their tickets never need.
- Engineers have permanent full access to the admin surface.
- Reads of sensitive data aren't logged, or are logged and never reviewed.
- One person can both make and approve a money movement or a credential change.
- Admin accounts exist outside your identity provider.
- People keep permissions from previous roles.
- Someone can grant themselves a role.
- Emergency access is a shared account.
- Access reviews are approved in bulk in minutes.

### 1. Permissions on operations and data classes

Every named operation (26.1a) declares a permission, like `payout.approve` or `trial.extend`. Reads need permissions too, and the useful unit for reads is the **data class**:

- **Basic account information**: name, status, plan, recent activity.
- **Contact details**: email, phone, address.
- **Financial details**: bank accounts, payout history, tax information.
- **Identity documents**: ID images and verification results.
- **Content and messages**: what users wrote to each other.

Pages show what the viewer's permissions allow and mask the rest, with reveal-on-reason for classes the viewer may see but shouldn't see by default (26.1a).

**Roles bundle permissions by job function**: first-line support, second-line support, trust and safety, finance operations, finance approver, sales. Keep them few, and resist per-person exceptions. Each exception is a role nobody will ever review.

### 2. Scope

A permission says *what*. Scope says *on which accounts*. Useful scopes:

- **Region**, for data residency and for outsourcing arrangements: the vendor team in one country sees accounts in the regions they support.
- **Customer tier**: enterprise accounts are handled by a named team only.
- **Assignment**: access only to accounts connected to a ticket assigned to you. This is **ticket-scoped access**, and it's one of the strongest controls available. It turns "can this agent see this account?" into "does this agent have a reason, right now, to see it?" The ex-partner lookup would have failed, because no ticket linked her to his account.

### 3. Standing and just-in-time access

**Standing access** is what someone has all the time. It fits frequent, low-risk work. **Just-in-time (JIT) access** is requested for a task: the person asks for a specific role or permission, gives a reason, receives it for a fixed period (typically one to eight hours), and loses it automatically. Some requests are approved automatically and logged; others need a person to approve.

JIT fits two groups especially well. **Engineers** should have no standing admin access. They elevate when debugging a specific ticket or incident, which would have limited the compromised laptop to whatever was active that day, if anything. And **rare, high-risk permissions**, like bulk export or identity-document access, can be JIT for everyone.

### 4. Approvals and separation of duties

**Maker-checker** (also called two-person approval or the four-eyes principle) splits a dangerous operation into a request and an approval by a different person. It suits money movement, credential and payout-destination changes, bulk exports, account deletion and privileged role grants.

For approvals to mean something:

- **The approver must differ from the requester**, and must hold the approval permission.
- **What's approved must be what runs.** Bind the approval to the exact request, using the preview fingerprint from 26.1a. If anything changes, it needs a new approval.
- **Approvals expire.** An approval from last week isn't an approval for today's state.

**Separation of duties** extends this to sequences and combinations:

- **Conflicting steps by the same person.** Whoever changed a payout destination can't approve a payout to it for some period. That rule would have stopped the fraudulent payout even though both permissions were legitimately his.
- **Toxic role combinations**, checked when roles are granted: nobody holds both "change payout destination" and "approve payouts" as standing access.
- **Conflicts of interest.** Staff can't act on accounts linked to themselves, such as their own customer accounts or those of relatives who declared, and can't take actions against their own staff account.

### 5. Permissions over permissions

Granting roles, changing policies and editing approval rules are the most powerful operations in the system. They get the strictest rules: **no one grants roles to themselves**, privileged roles require a second person to approve the grant, every change to permissions raises an alert to security, and the permission configuration itself is ideally code, changed through review.

### 6. Lifecycle: joiners, movers, leavers

Staff identity comes from the **workforce identity provider**, never from local accounts in the admin tool. Roles are derived from identity-provider groups, which are ideally derived from HR data: department, job function, employment type. Group membership is synchronized automatically (SCIM is the common standard for this). Then:

- **Leaving** disables the identity-provider account, which ends all access at once.
- **Moving** changes department, which removes the roles tied to the old department. Requested roles, as opposed to roles that come with a job, get re-confirmed on a move.
- **Contractors and vendor staff** have an end date by default. The vendor's access agreement requires timely notice of departures, and access expires anyway if nobody renews it.

### 7. Reviews that mean something

A quarterly review of 400 rows is theater. Reviews work when they're small and informed by evidence:

- **Show usage.** "Has finance-approver; used it 0 times in 90 days" should be pre-selected for removal.
- **Continuous pruning.** Permissions unused for 60 or 90 days are removed automatically, with a way to request them back.
- **Route to people who can judge.** The manager confirms the person still needs the job function; the owner of a sensitive permission confirms who holds it.
- **Unreviewed means revoked.** If a review isn't completed, access lapses rather than continuing.

### 8. Break-glass access

Emergencies need a way around approvals and JIT delays. The shared password is the wrong way. The right way is an **emergency elevation path per individual**: the person invokes it with a reason, gets broad access immediately for a short period (around an hour), security is paged, and a review afterwards is mandatory. It's fast, it's attributable, and its use is rare enough that each use gets looked at.

### 9. Monitoring what staff do

The audit log (24.4 covers making it complete and tamper-evident) records both reads and writes of sensitive data classes. Detection rules then turn it into something people act on:

- **Volume against peers**: an agent revealing ten times as many bank details per hour as colleagues on the same queue.
- **Access outside scope**, if scope is enforced softly anywhere.
- **Sensitive account lists**: access to accounts of public figures, of employees, or of people with protection flags always alerts.
- **Unusual timing or patterns**: bulk exports at 3 a.m., many accounts viewed in sequence without tickets.
- **Every break-glass use and every permission change.**
- **Honeytoken accounts**: fake customer accounts that no legitimate work ever touches. Any access to one is, by construction, worth investigating.

The customer-visible access log from 26.1b is also a detection control: customers who can see staff access will report access they didn't expect.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Roles by job function and per-operation permissions**: once more than about ten staff have admin access, or more than one team does.
- **Data classes and scope**: as soon as outsourced or first-line staff exist, or data is subject to residency rules.
- **JIT access**: for engineers from early on, since it's cheap with modern tools, and for high-risk permissions once there are enough staff that standing access means dozens of people.
- **Maker-checker and separation of duties**: whenever staff can move money, change where money goes, change credentials or export bulk data.
- **Automated lifecycle**: from the first contractor, and before any compliance audit.
- **Detection rules and honeytokens**: once there are enough staff that no one person knows everyone's normal patterns, or when the data would be valuable to an insider.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Five founders who all do everything.** SSO, individual accounts, no shared credentials and an audit log of writes cover the real risks. A role matrix at this size is a spreadsheet nobody updates.
- **JIT for routine work.** If first-line agents must request access for every ticket, the approval queue becomes the bottleneck and approvals become reflexive. Ticket-scoped standing access does the same job with less friction.
- **Two-person approval for small, reversible actions.** Extending a trial or resending an invite doesn't need a second person. Requiring one teaches everyone to approve without looking.
- **Attribute-based policies before roles are right.** Scoping rules on top of badly designed roles give you complexity with none of the benefit. Fix roles first.
- **Detection rules nobody will respond to.** Alerts that go to an unwatched channel are worse than none, because they create the impression of monitoring.

## Trade-offs

- **Friction, especially in emergencies.** JIT and approvals slow down legitimate work, and the cost is highest during incidents. Break-glass exists to handle this, but every step still costs something.
- **Approval fatigue.** The more approvals people see, the less attention each gets, and maker-checker degrades into a formality. Keep approvals rare so each one is read.
- **Policy complexity.** Roles, scopes, elevations and forbid rules interact, and "why can't I do this?" becomes a real support question for your own staff. Policies need the same explain-your-decision treatment as 26.2a gave the product.
- **False positives.** Detection rules flag legitimate but unusual work. Every alert that turns out fine costs an investigation, and some cost trust with the person investigated.
- **Tooling and integration cost.** Identity-provider group sync, JIT workflows and access reviews are real systems to run or buy, and the admin surface has to integrate with them.
- **Outsourcing adds a boundary you don't control.** Vendor staff join and leave on the vendor's schedule. Contracts, automatic expiry and scope limits reduce the risk; none of them remove it.

## Alternatives

**Identity-provider groups only.** Map a few groups straight to roles and stop there. That's a fine first step, and it solves the leaver problem. It doesn't provide scope, time limits or approvals.

**Privileged-access and access-governance products** (for example Teleport, StrongDM, ConductorOne, Opal, Okta Identity Governance, Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management). They provide JIT requests, approvals, reviews and session recording, mostly for infrastructure and SaaS tools. They can govern who gets a role in your admin surface, while the admin surface still enforces what the role allows.

**Policy engines** (Cedar, Open Policy Agent, OpenFGA, Casbin). These separate policy from application code, so rules can be reviewed, tested and changed without touching every operation. The implementation below uses one.

**Process instead of technology.** For a small team, a written policy, a monthly look at the audit log and a manual checklist when people leave can be proportionate. It stops working as soon as there are more people than one person can keep in their head.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — SSO, individual accounts, audit of writes.** No shared credentials and no local accounts. *Signal to do this immediately:* staff have admin access at all.

**L1 — per-operation permissions, roles by job function, groups synchronized from the identity provider.** Leavers lose access automatically. *Signal:* more than one team, or the first contractor.

**L2 — data classes, masking, logged reads, and scope** by region, tier and ticket. *Signal:* outsourced or first-line staff, or sensitive data like identity documents.

**L3 — JIT elevation for engineers and high-risk permissions; maker-checker and separation-of-duties rules** for money and credentials; no self-granting. *Signal:* money movement in the admin surface, or dozens of people with standing broad access.

**L4 — individual break-glass, detection rules, honeytoken accounts.** *Signal:* staff numbers where nobody knows everyone's normal behavior, or data valuable enough to attract insiders.

**L5 — usage-informed reviews and automatic pruning.** *Signal:* the first review that takes managers five minutes for four hundred rows.

## Implementation

Cedar, a policy language, because the most important property here is one the language provides directly: **a `forbid` always overrides a `permit`.** That's exactly how separation of duties should behave: no role, however broad, can grant its way past it. The application loads the entities (staff member, account, payout) and passes context such as the ticket and any active elevation.

Standing access for first-line support, limited by data class and scoped to assigned tickets:

```cedar
permit (
  principal in Group::"support_tier1",
  action in [Action::"ViewAccountBasic", Action::"ViewContactDetails", Action::"ResendInvite"],
  resource is Account
)
when {
  resource.region in principal.supportedRegions &&
  context.ticket.assignee == principal &&
  context.ticket.accounts.contains(resource)
};
```

High-risk access only through an active, unexpired elevation:

```cedar
permit (
  principal in Group::"engineering",
  action in Action::"AdminDebugActions",   // an action group
  resource is Account
)
when {
  context.elevation.grantee == principal &&
  context.elevation.expiresAt > context.now &&
  context.elevation.scope.contains(resource)
};
```

Separation of duties and conflicts of interest, which override every permit above and any added later:

```cedar
// Maker-checker: nobody approves their own request.
forbid (principal, action == Action::"ApprovePayout", resource is Payout)
when { resource.requestedBy == principal };

// Conflicting steps: whoever changed the destination can't approve payouts to it.
forbid (principal, action == Action::"ApprovePayout", resource is Payout)
when { resource.destination.lastChangedBy == principal };

// No one grants roles to themselves.
forbid (principal, action == Action::"GrantRole", resource is RoleGrant)
when { resource.grantee == principal };

// No acting on accounts linked to yourself.
forbid (principal, action, resource is Account)
when { resource.linkedStaff.contains(principal) };
```

> **Aside: a `forbid` that errors doesn't forbid.** Cedar skips any policy whose evaluation raises an error, such as an attribute that's missing. For a `permit`, that fails safe: no permission. For a `forbid`, it fails open: the prohibition silently stops applying. If the loader ever omits `destination.lastChangedBy`, the payout rule vanishes without a sound. Declare these attributes as required in the Cedar schema and validate policies against it, make the entity loader fail rather than omit, and write tests for each `forbid` that check it still denies. Separation-of-duty rules are exactly the ones nobody notices breaking, because when they break, everything still works.

The approval flow sits around these policies in the application: `ApprovePayout` is itself a named operation (26.1a), bound to the request's fingerprint and expiring after a set period, and both the request and the approval go into the audit log with their actors.

## Further Reading

- **NIST SP 800-53, controls AC-2 (Account Management), AC-5 (Separation of Duties) and AC-6 (Least Privilege).** The standard vocabulary for everything above, and the controls most audit frameworks trace back to.
- **The AICPA Trust Services Criteria, CC6.1 to CC6.3 (logical access).** What a SOC 2 auditor will ask about staff access, provisioning, removal and review.
- **RFC 7643 and RFC 7644 (SCIM).** The standard for synchronizing users and groups from an identity provider, which is what makes leaver handling automatic.
- **Google's BeyondCorp papers**, particularly the first ("BeyondCorp: A New Approach to Enterprise Security," 2014), for access decisions based on user, device and context rather than network location.
- **The Cedar documentation**, especially the sections on policy evaluation (forbid overrides permit, and how errors are handled) and schema validation.
- **Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management documentation.** A clear, public description of JIT elevation, approvals, expiry and access reviews as a product.
- **Thinkst's Canarytokens**, for the general idea of honeytokens and how cheap they can be.
- Within this map: product authorization models in **4.3**, tenant isolation in **5**, audit log mechanics and retention in **24.4**, named operations and preview fingerprints in **26.1a**, impersonation and the customer-visible access log in **26.1b**, explaining decisions in **26.2a**, flag governance by type in **26.2b**, and compliance obligations in **34**.

A sibling in Rego, the policy language of Open Policy Agent, would add a real contrast. Rego has no built-in "forbid wins" rule: deny-overrides has to be written explicitly, and policies reason over whole data documents rather than typed entities. Writing the same separation-of-duties rules there shows which guarantees came from Cedar's design and which have to be built and tested by hand. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying forward: **for every powerful capability, ask how it's granted, how it ends, and who would notice it being misused.** Access that's easy to get, never expires and is invisible in use will eventually be misused, by an insider, by an attacker with an insider's session, or by someone who simply forgot they still had it.
