# Content Isn't Messages: Translating What Lives in the Database

_Module 25.3b — Internationalization & Localization_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a vacation-rental platform. Hosts write their own listings (a title, a description, house rules, amenities), guests leave reviews, and the platform's staff write a help center and city guides. The interface is fully internationalized: messages are translated through a pipeline (25.2a), and each request resolves a viewer (25.3a). The content in the database was treated as if it were English. Then the platform grew to 1.2 million listings and 40 million listing views a month, across a dozen languages.

- **Portuguese translated as if it were English.** The schema had `title` and `description` columns, understood to be English, and the team later added `title_de`, `description_de`, `title_fr` and so on, filled by machine translation from the "English" columns. Hosts in Portugal write in Portuguese, which went into the English columns, so the translation service was told Portuguese was English. The German and French columns for 90,000 listings contain nonsense, and adding Japanese means a migration that adds two more columns to a 1.2-million-row table.
- **A translation still promises what the host withdrew.** After a bad experience, a host edits her description from "pets welcome" to "no pets, sorry". The German and French columns were translated months earlier, and nothing recorded which version of the text they came from, so they still say pets are welcome. A family from Munich arrives with a dog, and customer support spends a week on the refund dispute.
- **Translating on every page view.** To fix staleness, the team switches to translating descriptions when pages are requested. Each view of a listing in a language other than its own calls the translation API. At about 2,500 characters per listing and list prices around $20 per million characters, the projected monthly bill is roughly two million dollars. The 95th-percentile page latency rises by 700 milliseconds, and the provider's rate limits start returning errors every Saturday morning.
- **A machine speaks for the guest.** Reviews are machine-translated and shown as if the guest had written them in the reader's language. A Japanese guest's polite remark about a small bathroom becomes an English sentence that reads as a complaint about cleanliness. The host disputes the review, and there's no way for anyone to see the original.
- **Search and filters only work in English.** The search index covers the English columns with an English analyzer, so a German guest searching *Meerblick* ("sea view") finds nothing. Amenities are free text typed by hosts, which is why the "sea view" filter has 400 variants ("sea view", "Seaview", "ocean view", "vista al mar", "vista mar") and shows them in whatever language each host used.

Interface messages are few, are written by the team, and change at release time. Content is millions of items, written by thousands of people in whatever language they speak, and it changes every minute. The team applied message thinking to data (a column per language, with English as the assumed source) and data thinking to translation (fetch it when needed, and track nothing about where it came from).

## What It Is

**Content localization** means storing, translating, falling back and searching text that lives in your data rather than in your code. It shares ideas with the message pipeline (25.2a), such as source fingerprints, stale states and machine drafts with labels, but its scale, ownership and lifecycle are different enough that it needs its own design.

Not all content is alike. Four kinds cover most products, and each needs a different pipeline:

```
kind                     examples                          who writes it           volume     how it's translated
reference data           amenities, categories, property   you                     hundreds   like messages: IDs + catalogs (25.2a)
                         types, cities
editorial content        help center, city guides,         your staff              thousands  human workflow through a CMS and TMS
                         landing pages, legal terms
operator content         listings, product descriptions,   hosts, sellers,         millions   source language recorded; machine
                         menus, course descriptions        merchants                          translation, author or human overrides
user-generated content   reviews, comments, messages,      anyone                  tens of    shown in the original; translated on
                         Q&A                                                       millions   request, labelled
```

A few terms run through the rest of the article:

- The **source language** of a piece of content is the language its author wrote it in. It's a property of the content, not of the product.
- A **translation record** is a translation of one piece of content into one locale, with metadata saying which revision of the source it translates and whether a machine, a human translator, or the author produced it.
- **Stale** means the same thing as in 25.2a: the source changed after the translation was made.

The interface's messages, and the choice of the viewer's locale, are covered earlier in the module (25.1a, 25.3a).

## Recognizing the Problem

- Columns named `title_en`, `title_de`, or a single `title` column that is "assumed to be English".
- No record of what language each piece of content was written in.
- Translations with no record of which version of the source they came from.
- Machine translation called on every page view, or cached by nothing more specific than the listing ID.
- Machine-translated text shown without a label, or without a way to see the original.
- Categories, tags or amenities stored as free text typed by users.
- Cancellation terms, house rules or other promises that exist only as free text.
- Search that only finds content in its source language, or only in English.
- URLs with slugs in one language for every locale.
- Support disputes that turn out to be about translations.

## How It Works

### Every piece of content has a source language

Record the source language when content is written, and never overwrite the source with a translation. There are three ways to find out what it is:

- **Ask**, prefilled with the author's interface language. Authors often write in their interface language, but not always: a Portuguese host may use the platform in English and write the listing in Portuguese.
- **Detect** with a language-identification model, and confirm with the author when confidence is low. Detection is reliable for paragraphs and unreliable for short strings: a two-word title like "Casa Azul" is Portuguese, Spanish, or a proper name.
- **Inherit** from context, such as a review written in reply to a question.

Everything downstream depends on this one field. The translation service needs it, to be told the actual source language. The fallback policy needs it, to decide whether the viewer can read the original. Labels need it ("Translated from Portuguese"), and search needs it, to choose the right analyzer. It's also the basis for a policy decision that belongs in your terms: **the author's original is the authoritative version.** A translation is a convenience, and the original is what the host committed to.

### Storing translations: four data models

```
model                         shape                                         good for                      costs
column per language           title_en, title_de, ...                       2–3 fixed languages           a migration per language; no metadata;
                                                                                                          assumes one source language
JSON per field                title: {"pt-PT": "...", "de": "..."}          small, simple content         metadata needs nesting; per-locale indexes are
                                                                                                          expression indexes; the whole object is
                                                                                                          rewritten on every change
translation table             listing_translation(listing_id, locale,       most operator and UGC         a join on every read; more rows
                              title, description, source_rev, origin)       content
separate entity per locale    listing_de, listing_fr as full copies         editorial pages that differ   copies drift apart; what's shared
                                                                            by market beyond the text     (price, photos) has to be kept in sync
```

The translation table is the usual answer for content at scale. Each translation has its own row, with room for the metadata that makes it trustworthy: the source revision it was made from, who produced it (machine, human or author), and when. Adding a language is new rows, not a migration, and a missing translation is simply a missing row. Separate entities per locale are right for the editorial case, such as a landing page for Germany that has a different offer and different photos from the French one, because that isn't really a translation.

### Tracking which source a translation came from

This is 25.2a's stale tracking, applied to data. Every edit to the translatable fields increments the source's **revision number**. Every translation records the revision it translated. When the source's revision is higher, the translation is stale, which is a simple comparison in a query that stays correct no matter how many edits happened in between.

The granularity is a choice. A revision per row (title and description together) is simple, and it retranslates both fields when only one changes. A revision per field is more precise, at the cost of more columns. For short content like listings, per row is usually enough.

Stale translations need a display policy per field, just as messages do:

- **For descriptive text**, show the stale translation with a notice ("The host has updated this description since it was translated. See the original."), and queue a retranslation.
- **For anything that is a promise**, show the original or a fresh translation, never a stale one. Better still, take promises out of free text altogether (see "Structured fields for promises" below).

### When to translate: at write time, at read time, or on request

The opening's cost problem comes from translating at the wrong moment. There are three options, each with its own cost model:

```
when            triggered by              cost grows with               latency for readers    fits
at write time   every edit                edits × target locales        none                   operator content in your main languages;
                                                                                               anything you want indexed by search engines
lazily on read  first view in a locale,   distinct items × locales      the first reader       long-tail languages; content that's rarely
                then cached               actually viewed               waits (or sees the     viewed outside its own language
                                                                        original meanwhile)
on request      the reader clicks         clicks                        after the click        user-generated content: reviews, comments,
                "Translate"                                                                    messages
```

The cost difference is large. The platform has 40 million listing views a month but only about 50,000 listing edits a month. Translating each edit into five main languages costs a fraction of what translating each view does, and the translation can be stored, reviewed and indexed. Most products end up with a hybrid: operator content is translated at write time into the languages that account for most traffic, lazily into the long tail, and user-generated content only when someone asks.

**Cache by content, not by viewer.** A cached translation's key is the source text's fingerprint, plus the source and target languages. It's not the page, the listing ID or the user. A host with 30 apartments who pasted the same house rules into all of them pays for one translation, and an edit changes the fingerprint, so a stale cache entry can't be served. The translation table is exactly such a cache, with metadata.

**Set a budget.** Machine translation is a metered dependency, like any other paid API. Count characters per locale per day, alert on anomalies (a bulk import can translate a million listings overnight), and decide in advance what happens when the budget runs out: the lazy tier falls back to showing originals, and nothing else breaks.

### Fallback, labels and the original

Content fallback differs from message fallback (25.3a) in one important way: **the original is a real option.** A message missing in German falls back to English because English is the source. A listing missing in German can be shown in its source language, and many readers can make sense of it, or will use the "Translate" button. The choice depends on the surface:

- **Detail pages** show the best available translation, labelled, with the original one click away. If there's no translation, show the original, labelled with its language, and a button that requests one.
- **Lists and search results** can show the translated title where one exists and the original otherwise. Whether to hide untranslated items from a locale's listings is a product decision: hiding them makes the page consistent, and showing them makes it complete.
- **Pages meant for search engines.** Whether to publish machine-translated pages as separate indexable URLs is a quality and policy question, and search engines' guidance on automatically generated content has changed over time. Check the current guidance, and consider publishing locale URLs only for content with human or reviewed translations.

**Always label machine translation**, and say what it was translated from: "Translated by machine from Portuguese · Show original". This is honesty toward readers. It's also fairness toward authors, because the words on the screen aren't theirs, and it protects you when a translation causes a dispute, as the Japanese review did. For user-generated content, add a way to report a bad translation.

APIs that serve content should carry this metadata with each string rather than leaving clients to guess. The W3C's guidance on strings on the web recommends a language and a direction for each string:

```json
"description": { "value": "Apartamento com vista para o rio...", "lang": "pt-PT", "dir": "ltr",
                 "translation": null }
```

A client can then set `lang` on the element (25.2b: for the right letterforms, hyphenation and screen-reader voice) and show the right label.

### Rich text and what not to translate

Content is often formatted text: Markdown, HTML, bold, links, lists. Send the translation service the text *with* its markup, and tell it so. Both major machine-translation APIs can handle HTML and keep tags intact, and XLIFF has a standard way of representing inline markup for human translators. Don't translate the raw Markdown source, where a translator or a model can break the syntax, and don't strip the formatting and lose it.

Some parts of content should never be translated: addresses, names of places and people, codes, URLs, email addresses, and brand names. Mark them. In HTML, `translate="no"` is honored by both browsers and translation services. Otherwise, replace them with placeholders before translation and restore them after. A glossary (25.2a) keeps your own vocabulary consistent, so "Superhost" isn't rendered three different ways.

### Reference data is not content

Amenities, categories, property types and other controlled vocabularies are a *small, fixed set of things you own*, so treat them like interface messages: a stable ID (`sea_view`, `pets_allowed`) stored with the listing, and names that come from the translated message catalog (25.2a). The payoff is immediate:

- **Filters work in every language**, because they filter on IDs.
- **Search can match every language's name for an amenity**, because the names are known.
- **Nothing needs machine translation**, and a German guest sees *Meerblick* whatever language the host speaks.

Where free text is genuinely needed ("Other amenities"), keep it as operator content. Migrating the opening's 400 free-text variants is a one-time job: cluster the variants, map the common ones to IDs, and send the rest to a review queue.

### Structured fields for promises

The best translation of a promise is a structured field. "Pets: no", "Cancellation: free until 48 hours before check-in" and "Check-in: from 15:00" are values, rendered through messages that are translated once, correctly, into every language. They can't go stale because they aren't translations, and they can't be mistranslated because a person translated each message once. Free text then carries the description and the atmosphere, where an imperfect or stale translation is an inconvenience rather than a broken promise. The opening's refund dispute was about a fact the platform could have stored as a field.

Legal content, such as terms of service and privacy policies, is editorial content with an extra rule. It's translated by people, versioned per locale, and published together with a clause saying which language prevails if versions disagree. Deciding that clause is a legal question, not an engineering one (Module 34).

### Search, slugs, and finding content in any language

- **Index each translation in a field for its language**, with that language's analyzer (stemming, stop words, compound splitting for German), and index the original in a field for *its* language (Module 12). A query in German searches the German fields first and the source fields second, and results show the German translation where one exists.
- **Cross-language retrieval.** Multilingual embeddings can match a German query with a Portuguese listing without translating either (Module 39), which complements translated indexes for languages you don't translate into.
- **Translated slugs**, such as `/de/unterkunft/altstadt-wohnung-mit-flussblick`, are unique per locale, stored with the translation, and redirected when they change. They're linked to each other with `hreflang` (25.3a), and the language switcher uses them to land on the same listing.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any product where third parties write content that is read by people in other languages**: marketplaces, travel, e-commerce with independent sellers, course platforms, recipe and review sites.
- **Editorial content in several languages**, such as help centers, guides, and legal terms.
- **User-generated content read across languages**, such as reviews, Q&A and community forums.
- **Reference data shown in filters, facets or navigation**, which is almost every catalog.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Content that is single-language by nature**, such as internal notes, or messages between two people who share a language. Offering on-request translation is enough, if anything is.
- **Translating everything into every language at write time.** The long tail of languages and rarely viewed items costs the most and is seen the least. Translate those lazily or on request.
- **Translating user-generated content by default.** Show the original and translate on request. It's cheaper, more honest, and what readers of reviews generally prefer.
- **Human review of all machine translation.** Tier it (25.2a): human translation for editorial and legal content, and for your most viewed listings if the business case is there, and labelled machine translation for everything else.
- **Separate entities per locale when only the text differs.** Copies drift apart. Use a translation table.

## Trade-offs

- **Joins and rows.** A translation table doubles the queries or joins for every read, and adds millions of rows. It's the price of metadata and of languages without migrations.
- **Machine translation is a metered cost.** Budgets, content-addressed caching, and tiering decide whether it's affordable. A bulk import or an editing bug can spend a month's budget in a night.
- **Latency against freshness.** Translating at write time is fast to read and briefly stale after each edit. Translating lazily is fresh and slow for the first reader.
- **Quality and liability.** Machine translation will be wrong sometimes. Labels, the original one click away, structured fields for promises, and a statement that the original is authoritative together reduce the damage. They don't remove it.
- **Privacy.** Sending user-generated content, especially private messages, to a translation provider is processing personal data, with contractual and sometimes regional requirements (Module 34).
- **Authors want control.** Hosts who speak German want to write their own German, and some want to correct the machine. That's an `origin` of `author`, and machine translations must never overwrite it. It's another state to model and another screen to build.
- **Search indexes grow with every language**, and analyzers per language add operational work.
- **Policies multiply.** Fallbacks per surface, stale rules per field, translation timing per tier, and budgets per locale are each a sensible decision, and together they're a system that needs documenting.

## Alternatives

**One content language.** Requiring every listing to be written in English, or only in the local language, is the simplest option. It shuts out authors who don't write that language well, and readers who don't read it. For some B2B catalogs with a professional audience, it's acceptable.

**Leave it to the browser.** Readers can translate pages themselves with their browser's built-in translation. It's free and needs no work from you. It's also unlabelled, invisible to search engines, and hostile to frameworks that expect to own the page (25.2a). It's a fallback that users choose, not a strategy.

**Authors translate their own content.** Give authors fields for other languages, and they fill in what they can. It's high quality where it happens and patchy everywhere else. Combined with machine translation for the gaps, it's often the best outcome for marketplaces.

**LLM translation with context.** A language model given the listing's structured data (location, amenities, property type), the glossary, and an instruction about tone usually produces better translations than a generic machine-translation API, at a higher price per character. It fits the same pipeline, with the same labels, validation and budgets, and output quality should be evaluated the way Module 39 describes.

**Multilingual search instead of translated indexes.** Embedding-based retrieval can find content across languages without translating it at all. Results still need a translated title and description to be useful, so it reduces translation for search, not for display.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0: English assumed.** Single-language columns, or columns per language, filled without knowing the source.

**Level 1: source language and honest display.** Record each item's source language (asked, detected or inherited), show originals with a language label, and label every existing machine translation.
_Next when:_ authors who write in more than one language, which usually means already.

**Level 2: taxonomy and promises as structure.** Move amenities, categories and similar vocabularies to IDs with names from the message catalog. Move cancellation terms, pet policies and check-in times to structured fields rendered through messages.
_Next when:_ filters or facets in more than one language, or the first dispute about a translated promise.

**Level 3: a translation table with revisions.** Store translations with the source revision and origin, show stale translations with notices, and make fallback rules explicit per field and per surface.
_Next when:_ the first "the translation still says…" incident.

**Level 4: a translation pipeline for content.** Translate at write time into the main languages, lazily for the long tail, and on request for user-generated content. Cache by content fingerprint, set budgets and alerts, and never let a machine overwrite a human or author translation.
_Next when:_ translation costs or latency start to show up, or the product expands into more languages.

**Level 5: findability and quality.** Per-language search fields and analyzers, translated slugs with `hreflang`, human review for the most viewed content, and author-editable translations.
_Next when:_ search traffic and conversion matter in more than one market.

## Implementation

TypeScript over PostgreSQL. Most of this design is a schema and two queries, and the application code's job is to apply the policies: which label to show, when to request a translation, and when a machine is allowed to write one.

The schema keeps the original, stores translations with their provenance, and moves vocabularies and promises out of free text:

```sql
create table listing (
  id                  bigint generated always as identity primary key,
  host_id             bigint  not null,
  source_locale       text    not null,       -- 'pt-PT': what the host wrote in
  title               text    not null,       -- the host's own words, never overwritten
  description         text    not null,
  source_rev          integer not null default 1,  -- incremented on every edit to title or description
  pets                text    not null check (pets in ('no', 'small_dogs', 'yes')),
  cancellation_policy text    not null check (cancellation_policy in ('flexible', 'moderate', 'strict'))
);

create table listing_translation (
  listing_id    bigint      not null references listing on delete cascade,
  locale        text        not null,
  title         text        not null,
  description   text        not null,
  source_rev    integer     not null,         -- the revision of the source this translates
  origin        text        not null check (origin in ('machine', 'human', 'author')),
  translated_at timestamptz not null default now(),
  primary key (listing_id, locale)
);

create table amenity (id text primary key);   -- 'sea_view', 'wifi': names come from the message catalog
create table listing_amenity (
  listing_id bigint not null references listing on delete cascade,
  amenity_id text   not null references amenity,
  primary key (listing_id, amenity_id)
);
```

"Pets: no" in German is now `t(viewer, "listing.pets.no")`, translated once by a person, and it can't disagree with the host's current setting.

Reading a listing is one query plus a policy decision:

```ts
// listings/read.ts
type Shown = "original" | "fresh" | "stale";

const READ = `
  select l.id, l.source_locale, l.title as src_title, l.description as src_description, l.source_rev,
         t.title, t.description, t.origin, t.source_rev as t_rev
  from listing l
  left join listing_translation t on t.listing_id = l.id and t.locale = $2
  where l.id = $1`;

export async function listingFor(id: number, viewer: Viewer) {
  const r = await db.one(READ, [id, viewer.ui]);

  // A pt-BR reader can read a pt-PT listing: show the host's own words.
  if (sameLanguage(r.source_locale, viewer.ui)) {
    return { title: r.src_title, description: r.src_description, lang: r.source_locale, label: null };
  }

  const shown: Shown = !r.title ? "original" : r.t_rev < r.source_rev ? "stale" : "fresh";
  if (shown !== "fresh") {
    // Lazy tier: ask for a translation; the dedupe key makes a thousand simultaneous readers one job.
    await jobs.enqueue("translate-listing", { listingId: id, locale: viewer.ui },
                       { dedupeKey: `listing:${id}:${viewer.ui}:${r.source_rev}` });
  }

  return shown === "original"
    ? { title: r.src_title, description: r.src_description, lang: r.source_locale,
        label: { kind: "original", from: r.source_locale } }            // "Written in Portuguese · Translate"
    : { title: r.title, description: r.description, lang: viewer.ui,
        label: { kind: shown === "stale" ? "outdated" : r.origin,       // "Translated by machine from Portuguese"
                 from: r.source_locale, original: `/listings/${id}?original=1` } };
}

const sameLanguage = (a: string, b: string) => new Intl.Locale(a).language === new Intl.Locale(b).language;
```

> **Aside: `sameLanguage` compares languages, not locales, on purpose.** Showing a Brazilian reader a European Portuguese listing in its original form is almost always better than machine-translating it into Brazilian Portuguese. Two scripts of one language are the exception: a reader of Traditional Chinese shouldn't be shown Simplified as "the original in your language". Compare the script too for languages written in more than one (25.3a's matcher already knows which those are).

The translation job is where the rules about who may write a translation live:

```ts
// listings/translate.ts
export async function translateListing({ listingId, locale }: { listingId: number; locale: string }) {
  const l = await db.one(
    `select source_locale, title, description, source_rev from listing where id = $1`, [listingId]);
  const existing = await db.oneOrNone(
    `select source_rev, origin from listing_translation where listing_id = $1 and locale = $2`,
    [listingId, locale]);

  if (existing?.source_rev === l.source_rev) return;              // already current
  if (existing && existing.origin !== "machine") {
    await review.flag({ listingId, locale, reason: "source-changed" });   // a person's work: ask a person
    return;
  }
  if (!(await budget.reserve("mt", locale, l.title.length + l.description.length))) return;  // show originals instead

  const [title, description] = await mt.translate([l.title, l.description], {
    from: l.source_locale,          // the real source language, not an assumption
    to: locale,
    format: "html",                 // keep markup; honors translate="no" on addresses and names
    glossary: "listing-terms",
    cacheKey: "content-fingerprint", // identical house rules across 30 apartments: translated once
  });

  await db.none(`
    insert into listing_translation (listing_id, locale, title, description, source_rev, origin)
    values ($1, $2, $3, $4, $5, 'machine')
    on conflict (listing_id, locale) do update
      set title = excluded.title, description = excluded.description,
          source_rev = excluded.source_rev, origin = 'machine', translated_at = now()
      where listing_translation.source_rev < excluded.source_rev
        and listing_translation.origin = 'machine'`,
    [listingId, locale, title, description, l.source_rev]);
}
```

> **Aside: the `where` clause on the upsert is the race condition, handled.** A host edits twice in a minute, and the jobs for revisions 3 and 4 run concurrently. If the job for revision 3 finishes last, it tries to write an older translation over a newer one, and the guard refuses. The same clause stops a machine translation from landing on a row that a person has just written, even if that happens between the job's check and its write.

> **Aside: the same job serves both tiers.** On edit, the application increments `source_rev` and enqueues `translate-listing` for the listing's main languages (the write-time tier). The read path enqueues it for any other language the first time someone views the listing there (the lazy tier). User-generated reviews use a separate endpoint that runs only when a reader clicks "Translate", stores the result keyed by the review's fingerprint, and returns it labelled.

## Further Reading

- **W3C, "Strings on the Web: Language and Direction Metadata"**: why and how to carry language and direction with each string in JSON and other data formats, which is the basis for the API shape above.
- **OASIS XLIFF 2**, especially its handling of inline markup, for sending formatted content to human translators without breaking it.
- **Machine translation API documentation** for your provider, specifically on HTML and tag handling, glossaries, and "do not translate" markers. The major APIs all support these, with different details.
- **The Ruby Mobility gem's documentation on storage "backends"**, a clear comparison of column, key-value table, JSON and translation-table models for translated attributes, useful whatever language you use.
- **PostgreSQL documentation, "Text Search Configurations"**, and **Elasticsearch/OpenSearch language analyzers**: per-language indexing and stemming (Module 12).
- **Google Search Central** on localized versions of pages, `hreflang`, and the current policies on automatically generated content.
- **In this collection:** 25.2a (the message pipeline, whose stale-tracking and machine-draft ideas this adapts), 25.3a (the viewer, and fallback for messages), 25.2b (setting `lang` and `dir` from each string's metadata), 25.2c (normalizing text before fingerprinting it), Module 12 (search analyzers), Module 13 (caching), Module 34 (processing user content with third parties), Module 39 (multilingual embeddings, and evaluating model output).

A **Java** sibling with JPA would be worth writing, because an ORM is where the translation-table model most often goes wrong. Mapping translations as a lazy collection produces an extra query per listing on every list page, which is the N+1 problem from Module 6. Mapping them eagerly loads every language for every listing when a page needs one. Getting a single "the viewer's translation, or the original" into one query through an ORM, as the SQL above does directly, is instructive. Not yet written.

---

The habit to take from this: whenever text enters your database, ask three things. Who wrote it, in what language, and who will read it in what language? Then store the answers alongside the text, because every later decision about translating, showing, or searching it depends on them.
