# A Toggle Is a Production Change: Managing Flags and Experiments

_Module 26.2b — Admin Dashboard & Internal Tools_

## The Problem

Suppose you run a meal-kit subscription service with a website and mobile apps. About 400,000 active subscribers pick recipes each week, and 60 engineers ship behind feature flags, as 3.4 recommended: local evaluation, safe defaults, stable bucketing, an owner and an expiry on every flag. The flag platform works well technically. It has 600 flags, and about 90 people with access, including product managers and the growth marketing team, who also use it to run A/B tests.

One quarter:

- **The wrong environment.** A product manager moves `new-pricing-page` from 10% to 100%, believing she's in staging. She's in production. Conversion drops by 6%. The incident takes two hours to diagnose, because the on-call engineer looks at deploys, and there wasn't one. Flag changes don't appear on any timeline the on-call engineer looks at.
- **The kill switch nobody could find.** The recommendations service starts timing out and slowing every menu page. There's a flag that turns recommendations off, called `recs-v2-fallback-tmp`. The on-call engineer doesn't know it exists, and when someone remembers it, the on-call engineer's role can't change production flags. The one person who can is asleep. That's 40 minutes of a slow site, with the fix one click away.
- **The winner that wasn't.** A growth experiment tests a new "skip a week" flow. After three days the variant looks 4% better on retention, so the team changes the split from 50/50 to 90/10 "to capture the win sooner," then declares it a winner the following week. After full rollout, retention doesn't move. Checking daily, stopping early and changing the split mid-test together produced a result that was noise.
- **The split that was never 50/50.** Another experiment shows 52.3% of users in control and 47.7% in the variant, on a supposed 50/50 split across 180,000 users. Nobody notices. The variant redirected to a new page, and on slow mobile connections some users left before the page loaded and before the exposure event fired. The users counted in the variant were disproportionately the patient, fast-connection ones, and the results were biased toward it.
- **Two experiments, one checkout.** The growth team tests a free-delivery banner on checkout while the checkout team tests a new payment step. A quarter of users are in both. Each team reports a win, and nobody can say what the combination does.
- **The email list in the flag.** To give 38,000 beta testers early access, someone pastes their email addresses into a targeting rule. The web app uses a client-side SDK, which downloads the flag rules to the browser. For three weeks, every visitor's browser receives 38,000 customer email addresses.
- **600 flags, no owners.** About 240 flags have served the same value to everyone for months. Forty have owners who have left. Nobody knows which are safe to remove.

The thread here: **the flag platform is a production control plane operated by 90 people, and it's managed like a settings page.** And experiments are statistical instruments that are being configured like toggles.

## What It Is

This is **flag and experiment management**: the governance layer over a runtime control system. It has two halves.

**Flag management** treats every flag change as a **production change**: permissioned by environment and flag type, recorded, visible alongside deploys, and proportionate in its friction. It also covers targeting hygiene and the lifecycle from creation to deletion.

**Experiment management** treats an A/B test as an **instrument with a design**: a hypothesis, metrics and a sample size fixed before it starts, exposure recorded correctly, integrity checked automatically, and rules about when you're allowed to look and stop.

Placement relative to the rest of the map: 3.4 covered how flags work in code (evaluation, defaults, bucketing, expiry checks). 30.2 covers progressive delivery as a release practice. Entitlements, what a customer has paid for, are not flags at all and belong in domain logic (5, 15). An experiment *uses* a flag to assign variants, but a flag with a percentage split is not an experiment. What makes it one is everything around the assignment.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Flag changes don't appear on the dashboards or timelines used during incidents.
- The same permissions and review apply to every flag, whether it's a kill switch or a pricing test.
- On-call engineers don't know which kill switches exist, or can't use them.
- People have changed a production flag believing they were in another environment.
- Targeting rules contain lists of user IDs or email addresses.
- Experiment results are checked daily and stopped when they look good.
- Splits are changed while an experiment is running.
- Nobody checks whether the observed split matches the configured one.
- Several experiments run on the same page at once with no coordination.
- You have hundreds of flags and can't say which are still doing anything.

## How It Works

### Flags

**1. Govern by flag type, not uniformly.** Different flags have different risks and different urgency, so the right amount of friction differs. Declaring the type at creation lets the platform apply it:

| Type | Example | Who changes it in production | Review | Expected life |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Release | new checkout | the owning team | none for ramps; notify | weeks |
| Kill switch / ops | disable recommendations | anyone on call, instantly | none; alert on use | permanent |
| Experiment | skip-a-week flow | the experiment's owner, via the experiment | design review before start | weeks, fixed |
| Long-lived config | max recipes per box | owning team | a second person | months or more |

The kill switch row is the one teams most often get wrong, in both directions. A kill switch behind an approval step fails exactly when it's needed. The fix is to **declare them deliberately**: a clear naming convention (`kill.recommendations`), a line in the service's runbook, permission for everyone on call, an alert whenever one is used, and a periodic drill that actually flips it in production, so you know it works before the night you need it.

**2. Every change is visible where people look.** Each flag change records the actor, a reason, and a diff of the rules before and after (26.1b's two-principal rule applies if a change is made through automation). It's then published as an event to the places incident responders already look: the deploy timeline, dashboard annotations (16.5, 19), and the account timeline (26.2a) when the change targets specific accounts. "What changed at 14:02?" should return flag changes as readily as deploys.

**3. Make the blast radius obvious before the click.** The wrong-environment incident is a UI failure as much as a human one. Useful defenses:

- Production looks different: a colored frame, and the environment name next to the save button.
- The confirmation states the effect in numbers. "This changes new-pricing-page for about 390,000 users (100%), up from 39,000."
- Large jumps on sensitive flags require a reason, or a second person.
- Scheduled and staged changes ("10% now, 25% tomorrow, halt automatically if checkout errors rise") replace manual jumps.

Some teams go further and manage flag configuration **as code**, with changes made by pull request. That gives review and history for free, at the cost of speed. It suits long-lived config well and kill switches badly, and many teams mix the two.

**4. Targeting by attributes, not by lists.** Targeting rules should refer to attributes and named segments ("beta_tester = true", "region = CA"), with the segment membership stored in your own system, not pasted identifiers. Two reasons:

- **Client-side SDKs can deliver rules to the browser or app.** Anything in a rule may be visible to every user. Personal data, internal account names and secrets never go in rules. For client applications, prefer evaluating on the server and sending only the resulting values, or use the platform's private-attribute features.
- **Lists rot.** A pasted list of 38,000 emails is out of date the day after it's pasted, and nobody knows why a particular person is on it.

And the evaluation should explain itself, per user, for support: "Dana gets `variant-b` of `skip-a-week` because she's in the experiment's 50% and matched rule 2" (26.2a).

**5. A lifecycle with an end.** 3.4's expiry check in CI handles flags the code still knows about. The management side adds evidence from the running system:

- **Flags serving one value to everyone** for 30 days are finished rollouts. They should be removed, not left at 100%.
- **Flags evaluated nowhere** for 30 days are dead, or live only in a path nobody runs.
- **Flags with departed owners** get reassigned or scheduled for removal.
- **Code-reference scanning** shows where each flag is still used, so removal is a known amount of work.

The end state is "archived in the platform, removed from code, deleted," not "at 100% forever."

### Experiments

An experiment moves through states, and the useful rules attach to the transitions:

```
 draft ──► reviewed ──► running ──► analysing ──► decided ──► cleaned up
   │          │            │             │            │            │
 hypothesis  sample size  no changes   SRM check,   ship, drop   flag removed,
 metrics     & duration   to split or  planned      or iterate,  result archived
 unit        fixed        targeting    horizon      recorded
```

**6. Fix the design before it starts.** An experiment can't start without:

- **A hypothesis** stated as a change and an expected effect.
- **One primary metric**, decided now, not chosen afterwards from whichever moved.
- **Guardrail metrics** that must not get worse: cancellations, refunds, page latency, support contacts.
- **A minimum detectable effect and the sample size it implies**, and so a planned duration. Run for **whole weeks**, because behavior on Monday differs from behavior on Saturday.
- **The unit of randomization.** Usually the user. In B2B products it's often the whole account, because colleagues who see different versions compare notes and contaminate each other.
- **The exposure event**: exactly where a user first experiences the difference.

The sample size is often a sobering number. With a 5% baseline conversion rate, detecting a 5% relative lift (from 5.00% to 5.25%) at the usual 80% power and 5% significance needs about 120,000 users per variant. A product with 20,000 weekly users cannot run that experiment in any reasonable time, and should know that before it starts.

**7. Record exposure where the difference happens.** Counting everyone assigned, including users who never reached the changed page, dilutes the effect toward zero. Counting only users who reached it is right, but it must be done **the same way in every variant**. The biased 52/48 split came from logging exposure in the variant only after a redirect. The rule: log exposure at the moment the user's experience would diverge, in shared code, before any variant-specific behavior.

**8. Check integrity automatically.** A **sample ratio mismatch** (SRM) check compares observed assignment counts to the configured split. With large samples even small gaps are decisive: 52.3% versus 47.7% over 180,000 users is wildly unlikely by chance. An SRM doesn't tell you what's wrong, but it tells you the results can't be trusted, and a platform should refuse to show a result until it's resolved.

**9. Rules about looking and stopping.** Checking a standard test every day and stopping when it crosses significance raises the false-positive rate far above the nominal 5%. There are two honest options. Either fix the horizon in advance and don't decide before it, or use **sequential testing** methods designed for continuous monitoring. The platform should enforce whichever you pick. Guardrails are the exception: a guardrail that breaks badly can stop an experiment early, because stopping for harm doesn't require the same evidence as declaring a win.

Changing the split or targeting mid-test changes who's in each group over time, which can bias the result in either direction. If it has to change, restart the experiment with fresh assignment.

**10. Coordinate experiments that share a surface.** Experiments on the same page or flow go into **layers**: within a layer, a user is in at most one experiment, so they can't interfere. Experiments in different layers overlap freely, which is fine when they touch unrelated parts of the product. Layers cost traffic, since each experiment in a layer gets a slice. A long-running **holdout**, a small group that gets none of a quarter's changes, measures what the wins add up to together, which is often less than their sum.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Per-type flag governance** once more than one team, or anyone outside engineering, changes production flags.
- **Flag changes on the incident timeline** from the first time an incident is caused by one. It's cheap and removes a whole class of slow diagnoses.
- **Declared, drilled kill switches** for every dependency that can degrade the product and has a safe fallback.
- **Experiment design requirements** as soon as experiment results drive decisions that matter, like pricing, onboarding or retention flows.
- **SRM checks and horizon enforcement** from the first experiment. Both are small, and results without them are unreliable in ways that are hard to see.
- **Layers** once two teams want to test on the same surface at the same time.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **A small team with a few flags.** Ten flags and five engineers who talk every day don't need type-based permissions or approvals. Audit and the timeline events are still worth having.
- **Approvals on everything.** Requiring review for every ramp step turns flags into a slower deploy. Friction belongs on the risky changes.
- **Experiments without the traffic.** If the sample size calculation says six months, an A/B test is the wrong tool. Qualitative research, staged rollout with careful monitoring, or before-and-after comparisons with their caveats stated are more honest.
- **Experiments for decisions already made.** If the change ships regardless of the result, for legal, design or strategic reasons, run a staged rollout with guardrails instead.
- **Layers and holdouts with a handful of experiments.** They pay off when many teams test concurrently.

## Trade-offs

- **Governance slows people down.** Every confirmation and review step costs time, and the teams that ship most will feel it most. Too much of it and people route around the platform, often by hard-coding a value.
- **Honest experiments produce fewer wins.** Fixed designs, SRM checks and no early stopping reveal that many apparent wins were noise. That's the point, and it's also politically uncomfortable for teams measured on wins.
- **Sample-size discipline limits what you can test.** Many ideas can't be tested with the traffic you have, and layers make it worse by splitting traffic further.
- **Exposure logging has volume and correctness costs.** One event per user per experiment is cheap. One per evaluation is not. And getting the placement identical across variants takes care in every experiment.
- **Visibility requires integration.** Getting flag changes into incident timelines, and flag evaluation into support tools, is integration work with the observability and admin stacks.
- **Kill switches are code paths that rarely run.** A fallback that's never exercised may not work. Drilling them is the only fix, and drills have their own small risk.

## Alternatives

**No runtime flags: change behavior by deploying.** Simple and fully reviewed. It works for teams that deploy many times a day with fast rollback, and fails for kill switches, where a deploy is too slow.

**Buy a platform or build one.** Evaluation is easy to build; 3.4 showed most of it. The management plane and a trustworthy statistics engine are the hard parts. Vendors and open-source tools (LaunchDarkly, Statsig, Optimizely, Eppo, GrowthBook, Unleash, Flagsmith and others) differ mainly in how much of this article they enforce. Evaluate them on governance and experiment integrity, not on SDK count. OpenFeature (3.4) keeps the code side independent of the choice.

**Separate the tools.** Some teams use one system for release flags and kill switches (engineering-owned) and another for experiments (analytics-owned). This clarifies ownership and costs a second integration.

**Before-and-after comparisons.** Comparing a metric before and after a change is cheap and often all that's possible. Seasonality, marketing campaigns and other changes shipping at the same time make it weak evidence. Say so when presenting it.

**Qualitative research.** Watching five users try a flow finds usability problems that no A/B test at low traffic ever will.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — flags in code and config, as in 3.4.** Owners, expiries, the CI check.

**L1 — a runtime platform with audit and per-environment permissions.** Production changes are recorded with an actor and a reason. *Signal:* flags that need to change without a deploy.

**L2 — flag changes on the incident timeline, typed flags, and declared kill switches** with on-call permissions and a drill. *Signal:* the first incident caused by a flag change, or the first slow response where a kill switch existed.

**L3 — experiment design as a required template**: hypothesis, primary and guardrail metrics, sample size and duration, unit, exposure event. Automatic SRM checks. *Signal:* experiment results start deciding things that matter.

**L4 — horizon or sequential-testing enforcement, layers, guardrail auto-halts, holdouts.** *Signal:* several teams experimenting at once, or a shipped "win" that didn't hold up.

**L5 — lifecycle automation from evaluation data**: stale-flag reports, owner reassignment, code-reference scanning, removal tickets. *Signal:* flag count passes about a hundred, or nobody can say which are safe to delete.

## Implementation

Python, because both halves of the idea are policy and arithmetic: a check that applies governance by flag type, and the experiment checks that run before a start and before a result is shown.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class FlagChange:
    flag: Flag
    env: str
    actor: Staff
    reason: str | None
    users_before: int   # estimated from the last day of evaluations
    users_after: int

def check_change(c: FlagChange) -> list[str]:
    """Return the requirements not yet met; empty means the change may proceed."""
    if c.env != "production":
        return []
    missing = []
    t = c.flag.type
    if t == "kill_switch":
        if not c.actor.on_call and not c.actor.owns(c.flag):
            missing.append("on-call or owning team")
        return missing                     # never an approval step on a kill switch
    if t == "experiment":
        missing.append("change it through the experiment, not the flag")
        return missing
    if not c.actor.owns(c.flag):
        missing.append("owning team")
    if c.users_after - c.users_before > 50_000 and not c.reason:
        missing.append(f"reason (affects ~{c.users_after:,} users, up from {c.users_before:,})")
    if t == "long_lived_config":
        missing.append("second-person approval")
    return missing
```

After the change is applied, the same code publishes a `flag.changed` event with the rule diff to the deploy timeline, which is the one line that would have saved the two-hour diagnosis.

The experiment side checks the design before starting, and integrity before showing any result:

```python
from math import ceil
from scipy.stats import chisquare, norm

def required_per_variant(baseline: float, relative_mde: float,
                         alpha: float = 0.05, power: float = 0.8) -> int:
    p1, p2 = baseline, baseline * (1 + relative_mde)
    z = norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2) + norm.ppf(power)
    var = p1 * (1 - p1) + p2 * (1 - p2)
    return ceil(z**2 * var / (p2 - p1) ** 2)

# required_per_variant(0.05, 0.05) → about 122,000 per variant.
# At 20,000 eligible users a week that's ~12 weeks: say so before starting.

def srm_check(observed: dict[str, int], split: dict[str, float], threshold=0.001) -> bool:
    total = sum(observed.values())
    expected = [total * split[v] for v in observed]
    return chisquare(list(observed.values()), expected).pvalue >= threshold

# srm_check({"control": 94_140, "variant": 85_860}, {"control": .5, "variant": .5})
# → False (p is vanishingly small). Results are withheld until the cause is found.
```

> **Aside: the SRM is a symptom, and the cause is usually exposure logging.** In the redirect case, control logged exposure on page render and the variant logged it after a redirect had completed. The fix isn't in the analysis. It's to log exposure in shared code, at the moment the experiences diverge, before any variant-specific work happens. The very small threshold is deliberate: SRM checks run on every experiment, so a loose threshold would halt healthy ones.

## Further Reading

- **Kohavi, Tang and Xu, _Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments_ (2020).** The standard reference on experimentation at scale: metrics, guardrails, SRM, interference, and the many ways results go wrong.
- **Fabijan et al., "Diagnosing Sample Ratio Mismatch in Online Controlled Experiments" (KDD 2019).** A taxonomy of SRM causes drawn from real incidents at Microsoft and elsewhere.
- **Evan Miller, "How Not To Run an A/B Test."** A short, clear explanation of why repeated peeking inflates false positives.
- **Deng et al., "Improving the Sensitivity of Online Controlled Experiments by Utilizing Pre-Experiment Data" (2013).** The CUPED method, the most widely used way to reduce the sample sizes that make many tests impractical.
- **Pete Hodgson, "Feature Toggles (aka Feature Flags)."** Its taxonomy of toggle types is the basis for governing flags by type.
- **The OpenFeature specification's section on hooks**, a standard place to attach audit, telemetry and exposure logging to flag evaluation.
- **Your flag vendor's documentation on client-side SDKs and private attributes.** Read it specifically to learn what your rules expose to browsers and apps.
- Within this map: flag mechanics and expiry checks in **3.4**, entitlements versus flags in **5** and **15**, dashboard annotations in **16.5**, incident timelines in **19**, progressive delivery in **30.2**, named operations in **26.1a**, the actor on every change in **26.1b**, explanations per user in **26.2a**, and permissions for staff in **26.3**.

A TypeScript sibling would earn its place on the client side. Evaluating flags in the browser raises problems this article only mentions: rules and attributes visible to users, the flash of the wrong variant before evaluation completes, server-side rendering with the right variant, and logging exposure consistently across both. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying forward: **ask of every switch "who can flip it, who will see that it was flipped, and how would we know it did what we hoped?"** A flag with no answer to the first two is an unrecorded production change waiting to happen. An experiment with no answer to the third is an opinion with a p-value attached.
