# Make the System Explain Itself: Diagnostics, Health, and Exports for Support

_Module 26.2a — Admin Dashboard & Internal Tools_

## The Problem

Suppose you run an email marketing platform for small businesses. Customers keep subscriber lists, build segments ("opened in the last 90 days, lives in Canada"), and send campaigns and automated sequences. There are about 12,000 paying accounts, a support team of 15 and a customer-success team of four. The admin surface from 26.1a exists: search, an account page, named operations. It's good at *changing* things. It's much less good at answering the questions support actually gets, which mostly start with "why."

A typical month:

- **"Why didn't this subscriber get my campaign?"** It's the most common ticket. The answer could be any of seven things: unsubscribed, previously bounced, marked as spam, not in the segment at send time, deferred by sending limits, blocked by a failed domain check, or delivered to spam folder. Support escalates every one. An engineer spends 30 to 60 minutes in logs, and often can't answer at all, because the segment was re-evaluated at send time against data that has since changed.
- **"Why can't my colleague see Automations?"** Access depends on the plan, an add-on, a grandfathered legacy plan, a per-account override from sales, the user's role, and a feature flag for the new automations builder. Support looks at two of the six and guesses. About a third of the answers are wrong.
- **The error reference nobody can read.** The product shows "Something went wrong (ref 7f3a9c)." Customers paste the reference into tickets. Only engineers can search logs, and the logs contain subscriber emails, so support isn't allowed to look.
- **The churn nobody saw.** A 200,000-subscriber account's sending domain failed its authentication check after a DNS change. Their open rates fell by half over three weeks. Every signal needed to spot it existed somewhere: the failing check, the bounce rate, the drop in opens. Nobody saw them together, and the account cancelled saying "your deliverability is bad."
- **The export that outlived its purpose.** A customer's lawyer asks for everything held about one of their subscribers. Another customer wants their full list to migrate to a new tool. In both cases an engineer runs a query, puts a CSV in a shared drive, and sends a link. Eight months later one of those links, still live, containing 80,000 email addresses, turns up in a forwarded email thread outside the company.

The thread here: **the system makes thousands of decisions per account and keeps none of the reasons.** The data about one account is spread across logs, jobs, billing and configuration, indexed for engineers rather than by account. And when data has to leave, it leaves as an unmanaged copy.

## What It Is

This is **support tooling**, the diagnostic half of the admin surface. Its job is to make the system **explain itself, per account, in terms a non-engineer can use.** It has four parts, each covered below:

- **Explanations of effective state:** why this user can or can't do this, right now.
- **Recorded decisions:** why this thing did or didn't happen, at the time it happened.
- **A per-account timeline** that merges everything that happened to one account.
- **Health signals** that flag accounts in trouble before they say so.

Plus **managed exports**, for when data has to leave the system for a customer.

The neighbor to separate is **observability** (19). The two often share the same underlying telemetry, but they answer different questions. Observability asks "is the system healthy?", aggregates by service and endpoint, and is for engineers. Support diagnostics asks "what happened to this one account?", indexes by tenant and user, speaks in business terms, and has to be safe for people who shouldn't see raw logs. Analytics (16) is a third neighbor: it describes customers in aggregate, not one at a time.

## Recognizing the Problem

- The most common escalation from support to engineering is a "why didn't…" or "why can't…" question.
- Answering a "why" means reading logs, and support isn't allowed to read logs.
- Access depends on several layers (plan, add-ons, overrides, roles, flags) and no screen shows them together.
- Error references shown to customers can only be looked up by engineers.
- Things that *didn't* happen (a skipped send, a filtered row, a suppressed notification) leave no trace.
- Customer success learns an account was unhappy from the cancellation.
- Customer data exports are produced by hand and delivered as links or attachments.

## How It Works

### 1. Explain effective state, using the real logic

Most "why can't" questions come from layered state. The fix is to write the decision so that it returns **a verdict plus the reasons**, and to have the product use the same function:

```
Automations for jo@acme.test: DENIED
  ✗ plan Starter does not include automations
  · no automations add-on
  · no sales override
  ✓ role Editor would allow it (if the plan did)
  · flag new-automations-builder: not relevant to access
  → To enable: upgrade to Growth, or add the Automations add-on
```

The crucial rule is **one implementation.** If support tooling reimplements the rules to explain them, the explanation and the product will drift, and support will confidently give wrong answers. The product calls `explain(...).allowed`; the support tool shows the rest.

The same applies to other layered state: settings that inherit (account default, then workspace, then user), quotas with overrides, and feature flags whose targeting rules are complex (26.2b). "Where did this value come from?" deserves an answer as much as "what is it?"

### 2. Record decisions you can't recompute later

Effective state can be explained live. Many questions are about the past, though, and the inputs have changed since: the segment was evaluated on Tuesday against Tuesday's data. For those decisions, the only reliable answer is **a record made when the decision was made.**

The key insight is that **absence can't be searched.** If a send only logs successes, "why didn't she get it?" has no answer. The skipped cases need records too, with a **reason code**:

```
campaign 5512 → recipient r_8841
  outcome: suppressed
  reason:  previously_bounced (hard bounce, 2026-03-02, "mailbox does not exist")
```

Reason codes should be a closed, documented set (`sent`, `suppressed:unsubscribed`, `suppressed:bounced`, `excluded:segment`, `deferred:rate_limit`, `blocked:domain_unverified`...). A closed set lets you count them, show a breakdown ("of 40,000 selected, 38,200 sent, 1,300 suppressed as bounced..."), and translate each code into a sentence support can send to the customer.

Volume is the catch. Per-recipient records for every campaign can outgrow the campaign data itself. The usual compromise is **counts per reason kept indefinitely, and per-item records kept for a limited window** (30 to 90 days), which covers the period when people actually ask.

### 3. A timeline per account

Everything that happened to one account, merged in time order, from every source that has an account ID:

```
 admin actions ──┐
 config changes ─┤
 deploys, flags ─┤                        ┌──────────────────────────────┐
 errors ─────────┼──► indexed by account ─►  account page: timeline      │
 jobs, sends ────┤                        │  Mar 12 09:14  domain check  │
 billing events ─┤                        │                FAILED (DKIM) │
 logins ─────────┘                        │  Mar 12 09:02  DNS settings  │
                                          │                changed by jo │
                                          └──────────────────────────────┘
```

Two things make it work. The first is **tagging telemetry with the account ID at the source**, which is a discipline for the whole codebase, not something the timeline can add afterwards (19 covers correlation IDs; the account ID is the same idea at a coarser grain). The second is **translation into business terms**: "Campaign 'Spring Sale' finished: 38,200 sent, 1,300 suppressed" rather than a job ID and a status code.

"Who changed this setting, and when?" is one of the most valuable questions a timeline answers. That requires configuration changes to be recorded with before-and-after values and the actor (26.1b), which is also exactly what audit (24.4) needs.

### 4. Error references support can resolve

When the product shows an error reference, support should be able to paste it into the admin tool and get a **sanitized summary**: which account and user, when, which operation failed, the error category in plain words, and whether it matches a known issue or an open incident. Not the raw log line, and not the request body with subscriber data in it. Engineers get a link from that summary to the full trace. That one feature removes a whole class of escalations, and it's cheap if requests already carry IDs.

### 5. Health signals before a health score

Customer success wants to know which accounts are in trouble. The tempting build is a single health score from 0 to 100. The more useful first build is **a short list of specific signals, each with a reason and a suggested action**:

- Sending domain failing authentication, for how long, and what to fix.
- Bounce or spam-complaint rate above a threshold, rising.
- Usage down more than, say, 50% against the account's own previous month.
- A payment failing (15.3).
- Seats or sending volume at the plan limit, which is often an upsell rather than a problem.
- Errors for this account well above its normal rate.

A signal says what's wrong and what to do. A composite score says "62," and the first question anyone asks is "why 62?", which leads back to the signals anyway. Scores are useful later, for ranking thousands of accounts. They work best as an ordering over signals people already trust. Either way, they belong on the account page where success staff already work (16.5 made the same point about dashboards).

### 6. Exports as a managed process

Data leaves the system for customers for three main reasons: a **data subject access request** under privacy law (the customer's own subscriber asking what's held about them), a **migration or backup** of the customer's own data, and occasionally a **legal or investigative request**. The unmanaged version, an engineer running a query and sharing a file, creates a copy with no owner, no expiry and no record.

The managed version:

- **Self-serve first.** The best export is one the customer's admin runs in the product, with their own permissions. Most migration exports should be this.
- **Staff-initiated exports are named operations** (26.1a) with a purpose, explicit scope (which account, which data sets, which date range), a preview of row counts, and approval above a size or sensitivity threshold.
- **They run as background jobs** (10) and write to controlled storage, not to someone's laptop.
- **Delivery goes to the customer through the product.** The file appears in the customer's own account, behind their login, with an expiring link (24 to 72 hours) and a download record. Staff don't forward files.
- **Every file gets an export ID** in its name and metadata, so a file that turns up somewhere unexpected can be traced to its request.
- **Files are deleted after expiry**, and the record of the export is kept.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Explanations of effective state:** as soon as access depends on more than two layers, or "why can't they see X" is a common ticket.
- **Recorded decisions:** for any automated process that silently skips or filters things on the customer's behalf, like sending, syncing, importing, scheduling or matching, once "why didn't X happen" is a recurring question.
- **Timeline:** once a support team exists and tickets regularly require checking more than two systems.
- **Health signals:** once there's a success team, or once the largest accounts are worth enough that losing one silently would matter.
- **Managed exports:** the first time staff need to hand customer data to anyone. The shared-drive version is a data incident with a delay.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Simple permission models.** If there are two plans and two roles, a sentence on the account page explains access. A trace framework is unnecessary.
- **Recording decisions nobody asks about.** Per-item reason records are expensive. If nobody ever asks why a background re-index skipped a row, keep a count, not a record.
- **A merged timeline for a tiny team.** When everyone can read the logs and there are 50 customers, a saved log query filtered by account ID does the job.
- **Health scoring with little data.** With a few dozen accounts, success staff know them personally. A model built on thin data mostly produces confident noise.
- **Custom exports when the product already has one.** If the customer's own export feature covers the need, staff should point to it rather than reproduce it.

## Trade-offs

- **Decision records cost storage and write throughput**, sometimes more than the data they describe. Retention windows and per-reason counts contain this, but it's a real line item.
- **Explanations constrain how the logic is written.** A decision that returns reasons is more code than one that returns a boolean, and it has to stay the only implementation. Teams under deadline pressure are tempted to add a check elsewhere that the explanation doesn't know about.
- **Timelines concentrate personal data.** An account timeline that shows logins, IP addresses and subscriber-level events is exactly the kind of screen that needs masking, permissions and access logging (26.1a, 26.3).
- **Tagging telemetry by account is ongoing discipline.** Every new job, event and log line has to carry the account ID. The timeline is only as complete as the least careful component.
- **Health signals can become noise.** Too many signals with loose thresholds and success staff stop looking, the same failure as alert fatigue in 19.
- **Managed exports add friction.** A customer's lawyer who wants a file today will find a 24-hour approval step frustrating. The friction is the control; the trade-off is how much of it each export type gets.

## Alternatives

**Give support scrubbed log access.** A log tool with PII redaction and saved queries per question type ("why didn't subscriber X get campaign Y") can be a fine first step. It breaks down when the answer needs joining several sources, or when redaction can't be trusted.

**Customer-facing explanations.** Often the best diagnostic is one the customer sees themselves: "This subscriber wasn't sent to because they bounced on March 2" in the campaign report, or "Automations need the Growth plan" on the locked tab. Every explanation moved into the product removes tickets altogether. The explain functions and reason codes above are exactly what makes this cheap.

**Buy customer-success platforms** (Gainsight, Vitally, ChurnZero and similar) for health tracking. Reasonable when the signals are mostly usage and billing data you can sync. The account-specific technical signals (domain authentication, error rates) still have to come from your own systems.

**Help-desk integrations** (27) can show the account summary and health signals inside the ticket, next to the conversation. That's a presentation choice layered over the same data.

**Session replay** answers some "why" questions by showing what the user did. It doesn't explain decisions the system made in the background, which is where the hardest questions come from.

## How to Achieve It

**L0 — saved log queries filtered by account ID.** Engineers only. *Signal:* the same investigation run twice.

**L1 — error references resolvable in the admin tool**, and the account ID on all telemetry. *Signal:* customers pasting references support can't use.

**L2 — explain functions for the most-asked "why can't" questions**, shown on the account page and, where it makes sense, in the product. *Signal:* permission and plan questions near the top of the ticket categories.

**L3 — reason-coded records for the most-asked "why didn't" process**, with counts kept long-term and per-item records kept for a window. *Signal:* the escalation that takes an engineer an hour, repeatedly.

**L4 — merged account timeline and a first set of health signals.** *Signal:* a success team, or an avoidable churn that the data could have predicted.

**L5 — managed exports, with self-serve as the default and staff exports as named operations.** Do this earlier if staff are already handing out files. *Signal:* the first request for customer data from anyone outside the account.

## Implementation

TypeScript. Two small pieces carry the ideas: a decision that explains itself and is the only implementation, and a process that records the cases it skips.

```ts
type Step = { check: string; passed: boolean; detail: string };
type Decision = { allowed: boolean; steps: Step[]; remedy?: string };

export async function explainFeatureAccess(
  userId: string, feature: Feature,
): Promise<Decision> {
  const u = await loadAccessContext(userId); // plan, add-ons, overrides, role
  const steps: Step[] = [];

  const entitled =
    u.plan.features.includes(feature) ||
    u.addOns.some((a) => a.features.includes(feature)) ||
    u.overrides.some((o) => o.feature === feature && o.expiresAt > new Date());
  steps.push({ check: "entitlement", passed: entitled,
    detail: entitled ? "via plan, add-on or override" : `plan ${u.plan.name} excludes ${feature}` });

  const roleOk = u.role.permissions.includes(feature);
  steps.push({ check: "role", passed: roleOk, detail: `role ${u.role.name}` });

  return {
    allowed: entitled && roleOk,
    steps,
    remedy: !entitled ? upgradeHint(u.plan, feature) : !roleOk ? "Ask an account admin to change your role" : undefined,
  };
}

// The product's own check. There is no second implementation to drift.
export const canUse = async (userId: string, f: Feature) =>
  (await explainFeatureAccess(userId, f)).allowed;
```

The send process records every recipient it considered, including the ones it didn't send to:

```ts
type Outcome =
  | "sent" | "suppressed:unsubscribed" | "suppressed:bounced"
  | "suppressed:complained" | "excluded:segment" | "deferred:rate_limit";

async function sendCampaign(campaign: Campaign) {
  const outcomes = new OutcomeBuffer(campaign.id, { flushEvery: 5_000 });

  for await (const r of candidateRecipients(campaign)) {
    const skip = await suppressionReason(r); // same rules as everywhere else
    if (skip) { outcomes.add(r.id, skip); continue; }
    await mailer.enqueue(campaign, r);
    outcomes.add(r.id, "sent");
  }
  // Writes per-recipient rows (kept 60 days) and increments per-reason
  // counts on the campaign (kept forever). The breakdown in the campaign
  // report and the "why didn't she get it" answer both read from these.
  await outcomes.flush();
}
```

> **Aside: "sent" is recorded before it's true.** The outcome is written when the message is handed to the mailer queue, not when the receiving server accepts it. Delivery, bounce and complaint events arrive later, asynchronously, from the sending provider. They have to update the same per-recipient record, or support will tell a customer "it was sent" about a message that bounced an hour later. Treat the record as a small state machine (`queued → delivered | bounced | deferred`) rather than a single write.

The export side is mostly process rather than code: the export is a named operation (26.1a) whose `execute` enqueues a job, writes to controlled storage under an export ID, and publishes the file to the customer's own account with an expiry.

## Further Reading

- **Majors, Fong-Jones and Miranda, _Observability Engineering_.** The case for high-cardinality telemetry, meaning data you can slice by any account or user ID. It's the foundation that per-account diagnostics are built on.
- **OpenTelemetry's documentation on resource attributes and baggage**, for carrying an account ID through services and into every span and log.
- **The Cedar policy language documentation**, particularly how an authorization response reports which policies determined the decision. A good model of "a verdict with reasons" in a real authorization engine.
- **The European Data Protection Board's Guidelines 01/2022 on the right of access**, for what a subject access export must contain and how quickly. Useful for sizing the managed export process.
- **Stripe's documentation on request IDs and the request logs in its dashboard.** A public example of customer-visible references that resolve to a sanitized view of what happened.
- Within this map: background jobs in **10**, dunning signals in **15.3**, dashboards where people work in **16.5**, correlation IDs and alert fatigue in **19**, audit logs in **24.4**, privacy obligations in **34**, named operations and masking in **26.1a**, the actor on every change in **26.1b**, flag targeting explanations in **26.2b**, and help-desk integration in **27**.

A Go sibling would add real value on the recording side. At tens of millions of sends a day, the outcome buffer becomes a throughput problem: batching, backpressure against the database, merging late delivery events into existing records, and deciding what to sample. Seeing that pipeline explicitly would show what "record every decision" costs at scale. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying forward: **whenever your system decides something on a customer's behalf, ask where the reason goes.** A decision that isn't recorded at the moment it's made can only be guessed at later, and the person guessing will be a support agent talking to an unhappy customer.
