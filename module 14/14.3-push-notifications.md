# Push Notifications

_Module 14.3 — Email & Notification Systems_

## The Problem

The inbox from the previous article works. It's durable, it groups sensibly, the badge is correct. It has one structural limitation: it only reaches people who are looking at your product. The entire point of a notification is to reach someone who isn't.

So the team ships push. A field engineer gets paged when a site goes offline, in seconds, wherever they are. It works in testing on the developer's phone.

**Then the dashboard starts lying.** Sent: 12,400. Delivered: 12,400. Opened: 1,900. Support tickets say people didn't get paged. The team's first theory is that users are ignoring them, because that's what a 15% open rate on a 100% delivery rate implies.

It isn't what happened. "Delivered" in that dashboard means *Apple's servers returned 200*, which is a claim about a queue, not about a phone. Between that 200 and a human noticing, there is a device that may be off, a battery-saver mode that defers low-priority pushes indefinitely, a per-app throttle for the push type they chose, a token that stopped being valid four months ago when the user restored from a backup, and — for an unknown fraction of that 12,400 — an operating system setting where the user turned notifications off for the app entirely. **None of those produce an error.** They produce a 200 and silence, which is a shape the last two articles should have made familiar.

**And then the second problem, which is worse because it's permanent.** Six weeks earlier, someone had added a "you might like this feature" push to the onboarding flow, fired on day two. It ran for a month. A meaningful share of users responded by turning notifications off for the app — not for that campaign, because there is no such thing as unsubscribing from one kind of push. The switch is one switch. It took the outage pages with it, and nothing in any system anywhere recorded that it happened.

That's the shape of this channel. You are not sending a message to a device; you are asking a platform to interrupt a human on your behalf, using a permission that is granted once, spent globally, and revoked silently.

## What It Is

A **push notification** is a message delivered to a specific installation of your app through a persistent connection operated by a platform — Apple's APNs, Google's FCM, or, on the web, whichever push service the user's browser happens to use. You address it with a **device token** or **push subscription** that the client obtained after the user granted permission, and handed to your server.

You never have a connection to the device. You have an authenticated relationship with an intermediary who does, and they decide whether, when, and how your payload is rendered.

Three distinctions worth fixing now, because they're mixed up constantly.

**Push versus the inbox.** They are not alternatives. Push is an attempt to get attention; it is not storage, it carries no history, and a notification the user swipes away is gone. The inbox from §14.2 is the durable record. A correct design writes the inbox row first and treats push as a pointer to it — which also means a failed push degrades into "they'll see it next time they open the app" rather than into data loss.

**Alert push versus silent push.** An alert push asks the OS to display something. A silent (background, data-only) push wakes your app to do work without showing anything. They have different permission requirements, wildly different delivery guarantees, and iOS in particular treats background push as a hint it is free to ignore. Building a sync mechanism that depends on silent push arriving is a well-worn way to ship something that works on your desk and not in the world.

**Mobile push versus Web Push.** Same topology, different trust model. Mobile push payloads pass through Apple's or Google's infrastructure in a form they can read. Web Push, by specification, encrypts the payload end-to-end between your server and the browser, so the push service relaying it — which you don't choose, the browser does — learns only that *some* message went to *some* subscription.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Your "delivered" metric is indistinguishable from your "accepted by the platform" metric, and nobody has noticed they're the same number.
- The device-token table has never had a row deleted.
- The permission prompt fires on first launch, and mobile opt-in sits below 40% — or web opt-in below a few percent.
- A user reads something on their phone and the same notification is still sitting on the lock screen of the iPad in a drawer.
- Nothing in your system can tell you which users have push disabled at the OS level.
- The same copy is sent to email, inbox, and push, with the same length, and push truncates it.
- Push is sent from a code path that doesn't also write an inbox row.
- A time-sensitive notification arrives four hours late and nobody can explain why.
- Someone in Europe receives a promotional push at 3am because the send job runs at 9am Pacific.
- Your APNs authentication key or certificate expiry is not in anyone's calendar.
- A notification body contains content that would be a disclosure if read over someone's shoulder on a train.

## How It Works

### The topology

```
                  ┌── HTTP/2 + JWT ──► APNs ──────► iOS device ──► OS renders
your server ──────┤
                  ├── HTTP + OAuth ──► FCM ───────► Android device
                  │                      └────────► (relays to APNs for iOS)
                  │
                  └── HTTP + VAPID ──► browser push service ──► browser
                                       (Mozilla / Google / Microsoft —
                                        chosen by the browser, named in
                                        the subscription you were handed)
                                                    │
                                                    ▼
                                            service worker decrypts
                                            and calls showNotification()
```

FCM can act as a single front door to both mobile platforms, which is why most teams send everything through it and never speak to APNs directly. That's a reasonable default; it costs you a little fidelity in error reporting and adds a hop.

For Web Push there is no single endpoint to configure. The subscription object the browser gives you contains the URL to POST to, plus the keys needed to encrypt for that specific subscription. Your server signs requests with a VAPID keypair that identifies you to the push service.

### Tokens rot, and pruning them is not optional

A token identifies an *installation*, not a user. One user has several. An installation's token becomes invalid when the app is uninstalled, when the device is restored from a backup onto new hardware, when the platform rotates it, or after long enough inactivity.

The platforms tell you. APNs returns `410 Unregistered`; FCM returns `UNREGISTERED` or `INVALID_ARGUMENT`. These responses are the entire token-hygiene mechanism, and a system that doesn't consume them accumulates a table that is mostly dead addresses — the same failure, and the same fix, as the suppression list two articles ago, with the useful difference that here the error is immediate and unambiguous rather than an asynchronous bounce.

The data model that follows: `user → devices[]`, each with a token, a platform, an app version, and a last-seen timestamp. Sending to a user means sending to every live device and handling each outcome separately.

### Permission is the scarce resource

This is the part that isn't an engineering problem and determines everything anyway.

On iOS, and on Android 13 and later, displaying notifications requires a runtime prompt. In practice you get one chance: a denial is sticky and reversible only in Settings, which approximately nobody visits. On the web, browsers have gone further — Chrome and Firefox both suppress the prompt into a quieter UI for sites with poor accept rates, so a badly-timed ask doesn't just fail, it degrades your ability to ask later. Safari on iOS requires the site to be installed to the home screen before Web Push is available at all.

The technique that works is **priming**: show your own explanation first, in your own UI, at a moment when the value is obvious — after the user has been mentioned for the first time, not on first launch — and only trigger the OS prompt when they've already said yes. The OS prompt then becomes a confirmation of a decision already made, and the one-shot resource is spent on a user likely to accept.

A corollary that constrains product decisions permanently: **permission is global to your app.** There is no per-category opt-out at the OS level that you control. A user annoyed by marketing push has exactly one tool, and using it also disables your 2FA approvals and your outage pages.

### The headers matter more than the payload

The body is 4KB or so on every platform, which is enough for a sentence and a couple of ids and not enough for user-generated content. The headers are where the interesting decisions live, and they're the ones teams leave at defaults.

**Priority / urgency.** APNs `apns-priority: 10` means deliver immediately; `5` means the system may batch it to save power. Web Push has an `Urgency` header with the same intent. Marking everything urgent is not free — it's the signal platforms use to decide how generous to be with you, and power-management heuristics notice apps that always claim emergency.

**Push type.** APNs requires `apns-push-type` to match the content — `alert`, `background`, `voip`, and others — and enforces different rules for each. Background push is rate-limited to a handful per hour and comes with no delivery promise whatsoever.

**Collapse id.** `apns-collapse-id` and FCM's `collapse_key` cause a new notification to *replace* an undelivered one with the same key. This is exactly the supersession mechanism from the inbox article, implemented at the transport layer: a device that's been off for six hours should receive "3 new messages," not six separate stale alerts. It is also what makes a retried send harmless, which matters because push has no idempotency key.

**Expiration / TTL.** `apns-expiration` and FCM's `ttl` tell the platform how long to keep retrying to an offline device. Defaults are generous — often weeks — and are almost always wrong. "Your driver is arriving" delivered tomorrow is worse than not delivered. Set a TTL that matches how long the message remains true.

### What you actually know about delivery

Very little, by design. A 200 means accepted for delivery. There is no general per-message delivery receipt: Apple has moved toward exposing aggregate delivery metrics rather than per-notification confirmations, and FCM's delivery data is similarly aggregate and delayed. Neither gives you the thing you want, which is "did this specific person's phone receive this specific message."

The only high-confidence signal is the **open**, reported by your own client when the user taps. That makes your measurable funnel `accepted → opened`, with the actual delivery step invisible in the middle — so a drop in open rate is genuinely ambiguous between "people ignored it" and "it never arrived," and you will not be able to distinguish them from server-side data alone.

The design conclusion is the one from the top of the article: **push carries no information you can't afford to lose.** It points at a durable record; it is never the record.

### Multi-device and the persistence problem

Email disappears when read. Push doesn't — a delivered notification sits on the lock screen of every device it reached until something removes it. Read on the phone, still on the tablet, still on the watch, still on the laptop.

Clearing it requires action: a silent push instructing the app to remove delivered notifications with a given identifier, or a collapse-id replacement. Which also means the tidy version of this feature depends on the silent-push channel you were just warned not to depend on, so the honest answer is that multi-device dismissal is best-effort and you should design copy that isn't embarrassing when it's stale.

The badge number is a related coupling worth noticing: on iOS the badge is set by the payload, so your server must know the user's *current unread count* at send time. Your push path now reads from the inbox from §14.2, and if those two disagree the badge is the thing users see.

### The lock screen is a public surface

The rendered notification is visible on a locked device to anyone nearby. "Dr. Chen: your biopsy results are ready" is a medical disclosure delivered to a coffee shop. The mitigations are to keep sensitive detail out of the payload and fetch it after unlock (iOS notification service extensions exist partly for this), or to use deliberately generic copy for sensitive categories.

Note the asymmetry with Web Push's end-to-end encryption: on mobile, the platform can read your payload; on the web it can't. Both are visible on a lock screen. Encryption protects it in transit, not in the place where it's actually most exposed.

## When It's Appropriate

Push earns its cost when three things are true at once. The message is **time-sensitive**, it is **actionable by this person**, and **the user is not currently in your app**. Drop any one and a cheaper channel is better.

- An on-call page for a production incident: minutes matter, there's a specific responder, and they're asleep.
- A 2FA or login approval prompt: the whole flow is blocked until they respond.
- A direct message or a mention that someone is actively waiting on.
- A delivery at the door, a ride arriving, an auction ending in ninety seconds — anything where the window to act is shorter than the interval between app opens.
- Mobile-first consumer products generally, where the app is closed by default and there is no in-app surface to notice anything on.
- As the wake-up half of an inbox: push gets them there, the inbox holds everything.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Anything that can wait until the user next opens the app.** This is most notifications, and the inbox handles it for free.
- **Re-engagement and marketing push.** The highest-cost, lowest-trust use of a non-renewable permission, and the single most common cause of losing the channel entirely. If it has to happen, it should be a deliberate product decision with someone accountable for the opt-out rate, not a growth experiment.
- **Web push on a desktop-centric B2B product.** The tab is open; the in-app inbox already works. The prompt costs goodwill and buys a capability you're not using.
- **Push before there's an inbox.** Push-only notifications are the toast-only bug from the last article, at operating-system scale — and now the user can't even refresh to recover them.
- **Permission requested on first launch.** You are spending the resource before you have anything to spend it on.
- **Silent push as a load-bearing sync mechanism.** It will work in development and be unreliable in production, and the failure is invisible.
- **Running your own APNs and FCM integrations** before you've hit a limitation of a provider. Token hygiene, credential rotation, retries, and per-platform quirks are a lot of undifferentiated work.

## Trade-offs

- **Two more platform relationships**, each with credentials that expire, consoles you don't control, policies that change, and rejection rules that can gate your app release. An expired APNs key has silently killed production push for a great many teams, and the failure mode is a 403 nobody is alerting on.
- **You permanently lose delivery truth.** Every metric you build sits on an unknown denominator, and no amount of instrumentation on your side fixes it.
- **Permission is global and one-way.** Every push type shares the fate of the worst one, which makes the channel's health a cross-team concern that no single team owns.
- **The token table is a decaying dataset** requiring active maintenance forever, and its decay rate is driven by user behaviour you can't observe.
- **Client and server are coupled tightly.** Payload shape, notification categories, deep-link routing, and badge semantics all live partly in an app binary. Old versions persist on devices for years, so every payload change is a compatibility question.
- **The content is a privacy surface** with a different threat model — and a different regulatory posture — than anything inside your authenticated app.
- **Testing is genuinely hard.** Simulators don't receive real push, Low Power Mode and Focus modes aren't reproducible on demand, throttling behaviour is undocumented, and results differ by OS version. You will ship changes you cannot fully verify.

## Alternatives

**SMS.** No permission prompt, near-universal reach, works on a decade-old phone. Costs real money per message, carries no rich content or deep link that users trust, and is subject to its own registration and filtering regime. The next article's subject.

**Email with urgency.** Universal, durable, zero permission cost, and latency measured in minutes at best — plus everything the first article described about whether it arrives at all.

**Inbox and badge only.** No permission, no platform relationship, no privacy surface, and zero reach when the app is closed. For products people live inside all day, this is frequently sufficient.

**Someone else's push.** A Slack or Teams integration reaches your B2B users on a channel where the permission has already been granted, configured, and tuned by the user themselves. It requires no mobile app, no tokens, and no credentials to rotate. For internal tools and B2B products this is often strictly better than building push, and it's under-considered because it doesn't feel like building a feature.

**A managed push vendor** — OneSignal, Airship, Braze, or the notification-infrastructure platforms mentioned in the last article. They absorb token hygiene, retries, platform quirks, and scheduling. You give up a copy of your user data and put a third party on the path of your most urgent messages.

**Locally scheduled notifications.** For anything whose timing is known in advance — a meeting in ten minutes, a medication reminder, a scheduled workout — the device can schedule the notification itself. No server, no network, no token, no platform intermediary, and it fires on a plane. This is the most reliable notification mechanism available to you and it's routinely overlooked because it doesn't involve a backend.

## How to Achieve It

**L1 — one device table, one notification type.** Send through FCM as a single front door. Pick the single event users would name if asked what they'd want to be interrupted for, and send only that. Write the inbox row first; push second and best-effort. *Signal to start:* you have a time-critical event and users are discovering it late.

**L2 — consume the errors.** Map `410` / `UNREGISTERED` to deleting the device row, on every send. *Signal:* your send-failure count is nonzero and no code reads it.

**L3 — prime the permission and move the prompt.** Own explanation first, OS prompt after a yes, triggered at a moment of demonstrated value. *Signal:* mobile opt-in below roughly half, or any web opt-in number at all.

**L4 — set collapse ids, TTLs, and priority per notification type.** This is a table of five rows in your code and it removes most stale-and-duplicate complaints. *Signal:* users receiving bursts of outdated alerts after being offline.

**L5 — badge sync and multi-device dismissal.** Badge derived from the inbox unread count at send time; best-effort clearing on other devices. *Signal:* users with more than one device reporting phantom badges.

**L6 — per-type preferences, quiet hours, timezone-aware sending.** Which is orchestration, and belongs to §14.5.

## Implementation

TypeScript. The interesting part is the fan-out to a user's devices and what each per-device outcome means — push has no idempotency key, so the error handling *is* the design.

```ts
type Device = { id: string; token: string; platform: "ios" | "android" | "web" };

type PushIntent = {
  userId: string;
  category: "incident" | "mention" | "approval";
  title: string;
  body: string;           // keep it short; 4KB is the whole envelope
  inboxId: string;        // what this points at — the durable record
  collapseId: string;     // e.g. `incident:${incidentId}`
  ttlSeconds: number;     // how long this stays true
};

async function push(intent: PushIntent) {
  const devices = await devices_.forUser(intent.userId);
  if (devices.length === 0) return; // not an error; the inbox row still exists

  // iOS renders the badge number from the payload, so the push path has to
  // read the inbox's unread count. These two can disagree; the user sees this one.
  const badge = await inbox.unreadCount(intent.userId);

  const results = await Promise.allSettled(
    devices.map((d) =>
      fcm.send({
        token: d.token,
        notification: { title: intent.title, body: intent.body },
        data: { inboxId: intent.inboxId, category: intent.category },
        android: { ttl: intent.ttlSeconds * 1000, collapseKey: intent.collapseId },
        apns: {
          headers: {
            "apns-push-type": "alert",
            "apns-priority": intent.category === "incident" ? "10" : "5",
            "apns-collapse-id": intent.collapseId,
            "apns-expiration": String(nowSeconds() + intent.ttlSeconds),
          },
          payload: { aps: { badge, sound: "default" } },
        },
      })
    )
  );

  await Promise.all(results.map((r, i) => reconcile(devices[i], r)));
}
```

Two asides on that. The priority ternary is the whole "don't mark everything urgent" argument reduced to one line — and it's the line that gets deleted the first time someone decides their feature is also important. And note that a retry of this entire function re-sends to every device that already succeeded; the collapse id is what makes that harmless, which is the closest thing to idempotency this channel offers.

The reconciliation is where token hygiene actually happens:

```ts
async function reconcile(device: Device, r: PromiseSettledResult<SendResult>) {
  if (r.status === "fulfilled") {
    return devices_.touch(device.id); // last-seen, for pruning stale installs later
  }

  switch (r.reason.code) {
    case "messaging/registration-token-not-registered": // app uninstalled, device restored
    case "messaging/invalid-argument":                  // malformed or foreign token
      return devices_.delete(device.id);

    case "messaging/quota-exceeded":
    case "messaging/unavailable":                       // transient — the platform, not the token
      return retries.schedule(device.id, backoff(r.reason));

    case "messaging/third-party-auth-error":            // your APNs credentials, not the user's token
      return alerts.page("push credentials rejected");  // silent, total, and easy to miss

    default:
      return log.warn({ device: device.id, code: r.reason.code });
  }
}
```

The third case deserves the page. An expired APNs key produces a per-message auth error for *every* device and no user-visible symptom at all — nobody files a ticket saying "I did not receive a notification I didn't know was coming." Without an alert on it, this is a multi-week outage discovered by accident.

One thing deliberately absent from both snippets: nothing here treats a successful send as evidence the notification was seen. The only write of that kind happens when the client reports a tap, and it updates the inbox row, not the push record.

## Further Reading

- **Apple's UserNotifications and APNs documentation**, particularly the tables for `apns-push-type`, `apns-priority`, and `apns-expiration`. The rules about which combinations are valid are enforced, undocumented elsewhere, and the source of most first-integration failures.
- **Apple's Human Interface Guidelines — Notifications.** Unusually, the design guidance here is also the engineering guidance: the permission-timing argument is Apple's own.
- **Firebase Cloud Messaging documentation**, especially the HTTP v1 error-code reference and the pages on message priority and lifetime. The error codes are the token-hygiene specification.
- **RFC 8030 (Web Push Protocol), RFC 8291 (Message Encryption for Web Push), RFC 8292 (VAPID).** Short, readable, and they make the web's trust model explicit in a way no vendor documentation does.
- **MDN's Push API and Notifications API guides**, including the `userVisibleOnly` requirement — the web will not let you receive a push without showing the user something, and browsers will display a generic "this site was updated in the background" notice on your behalf if you try.
- **Chrome's documentation on quieter notification permission UI and abusive-notification enforcement** — the concrete mechanics behind "a bad ask degrades future asks."

A **TypeScript service-worker sibling** would reveal what this article structurally can't: everything after the payload leaves your server. Decrypting in the `push` event, the `userVisibleOnly` bargain, routing a notification click to the right deep link in an app that may not be running, and reconciling badge state on focus. The server side of push is a fan-out with careful error handling; the browser side is where the platform's constraints actually bite. Not yet written.

---

The habit worth carrying out of this one: some channels aren't bought with money or engineering, they're bought with a **permission grant held by a third party on behalf of your user** — and that grant is global, one-shot, and revoked without notice. Push is the clearest case. App store presence, browser extension permissions, SMS short-code registration, OAuth scopes your integration asks for, and calendar or contacts access all behave the same way: cheap to obtain once, impossible to recover, and spent by whoever on your team asks for the most with the least justification. It's worth knowing which of your channels work like this, because they need an owner in a way that a database table never does.
