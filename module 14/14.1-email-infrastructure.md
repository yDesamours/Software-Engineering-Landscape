# Email Infrastructure

_Module 14.1 — Email & Notification Systems_

## The Problem

A team ships a signup flow. The handler is four lines:

```ts
const user = await db.users.insert(input);
await smtp.send({ to: user.email, subject: "Confirm your account", html });
return res.status(201).json(user);
```

It works. It works locally, it works in staging, it works in production for about a month. Then it starts failing, and it fails five different ways that have nothing in common except that each one is invisible from inside the application.

**Week 3 — signup gets slow.** The SMTP handshake, TLS negotiation, and relay acceptance take somewhere between 200ms and four seconds depending on the provider's mood. Signup p99 now tracks a third party's p99. Nobody notices, because the median is fine.

**Week 6 — signup starts returning 500s.** The mail provider has an incident. Your availability is now the product of your availability and theirs, for a feature that has nothing to do with whether the account was created. The user record was written. The response said the signup failed. Support gets tickets from people who can't sign up and can't sign up again, because the address is taken.

**Week 9 — a customer gets five copies of the same invoice.** The job runner retried a send that had timed out. The message had already been accepted; only the acknowledgement was lost.

**Week 12 — "I never got the password reset."** You check the logs. The send succeeded. SMTP returned `250 OK`. That code means *the next server accepted responsibility for the message*, not *the human read it*. Everything after that happened in systems you cannot see, and the outcome — inbox, spam folder, silently discarded — was decided minutes later by a filter you'll never get an explanation from.

**Month 8 — everything stops arriving.** Not just the newsletter. Invoices, receipts, password resets, team invitations. A fraction of recipients had been clicking "report spam" on the product digest for months, and a long tail of dead addresses had been hard-bouncing every week because nothing ever recorded that they were dead. The receiving providers drew the obvious conclusion about the domain, and the domain is the same one the invoices come from.

The thread running through all five: **your application treats email as a function call that returns, and it isn't one.** It's a submission to a pipeline that runs for minutes, is graded by systems with their own incentives, and reports back — if at all — out of band.

```
 your app ──queue──► worker ──API/SMTP──► ESP ──SMTP──► recipient MX ──► filter ──┬──► Inbox
                                           │                                      ├──► Spam
                                           │                                      └──► discarded
                                           │
                                           ▼  seconds to hours later
                                      webhooks: delivered / bounced / complained
                                           │
                                           ▼
                                    ...back to you, if anyone is listening
```

The dotted return path is the part most teams never build, and it's the one that stops month 8 from happening.

## What It Is

Everything in that diagram other than the send itself is what people mean by **email infrastructure**: the queue that gets the send off the request path, the provider that does the delivering, the DNS records that prove you're allowed to send as your domain, the webhook consumer that learns what happened, and the suppression list that acts on it.

The distinction worth making immediately is **transactional versus marketing** email, because it's the one teams collapse and shouldn't. A transactional message is caused by a specific user action and is expected by that user: a receipt, a reset link, an invite. A marketing (or bulk) message is sent to a list on your initiative: an announcement, a digest, a win-back campaign. They differ in consent rules, in whether unsubscribe is optional, and — the part that bites — in **reputation**. Receiving providers score you partly per-domain. If both streams leave from the same domain, complaints earned by the campaign are paid for by the password resets.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Email is sent directly from an HTTP handler, inside or immediately after a database transaction.
- Signup or checkout latency correlates with your mail provider's status page.
- You cannot answer "did this specific user receive this specific email, and when?" without asking the provider's dashboard by hand.
- Retries in your job system produce duplicate emails, so someone has quietly disabled retries for email jobs.
- Nothing in your database records a bounce. The same dead address is mailed every week.
- Your sending domain is your apex domain, and the newsletter leaves from it too.
- `dig TXT _dmarc.yourdomain.com` returns nothing, or a `p=none` record somebody added in 2023 and never revisited.
- Unsubscribe state lives only in the marketing tool, so a product email still reaches someone who opted out.
- Your reported open rate has been suspiciously stable around 60–70% for years, and product decisions are being made on it.
- Support's answer to "check your spam folder" is the resolution for a noticeable share of tickets.

## How It Works

### Accept, then deliver

There are two hops, and only the first one is synchronous. Your application hands the message to an email service provider (ESP) over SMTP or an HTTP API; the ESP accepts it and returns. Later — usually seconds, sometimes hours after retries against a greylisting or throttling receiver — the ESP attempts delivery to the recipient's mail exchanger. Success or failure at that second hop comes back to you only as a webhook, and only if you've built somewhere for it to land.

This is why "sent" is not a state worth storing on its own. A message has a lifecycle:

```
queued ──► accepted ──► delivered ──┬──► opened / clicked
                │                   └──► complained (user hit "spam")
                ├──► soft bounce ──► retried ──► delivered | hard bounce
                └──► hard bounce ──► suppressed
```

Support tooling that only knows `sent = true` cannot answer any question a customer will actually ask.

### Authentication: proving you're allowed to send as your domain

Three DNS-based mechanisms, doing three different jobs. They're routinely described as "the deliverability checklist," which hides what each one is for.

**SPF** (a TXT record listing authorized sending hosts) answers: *is the server that handed us this message permitted to send for this domain?* It checks the envelope sender — the address used in the SMTP conversation — which the recipient never sees. It breaks when a message is forwarded, because the forwarding server isn't on your list.

**DKIM** attaches a cryptographic signature over selected headers and the body, verifiable against a public key in your DNS. It answers: *was this message actually authored by someone holding the domain's key, and is it unmodified?* Because the signature travels with the message, it survives simple forwarding.

**DMARC** is the one that matters conceptually, because it introduces **alignment**. SPF and DKIM both authenticate domains the user never sees. DMARC requires that one of them match the `From:` header the human actually reads, tells receivers what to do when that fails (`none`, `quarantine`, `reject`), and provides an address for aggregate reports. Without DMARC, passing SPF and DKIM says nothing about whether the visible sender is real — which is exactly the gap phishing lives in.

As of the 2024 bulk-sender requirements from Google and Yahoo, all three are effectively mandatory above 5,000 messages a day to their users, along with one-click unsubscribe and a complaint rate held under 0.3%.

### Reputation

Receiving providers score two things: the sending IP and the sending domain. The inputs are bounce rate, complaint rate, spam-trap hits, and engagement — whether recipients open, reply, and move messages out of junk.

Two consequences follow. First, **a shared IP pool means your neighbours' behaviour affects you**, and a dedicated IP means the opposite: you own the reputation, including the part where a brand-new IP has none and must be warmed by ramping volume over weeks. Second, **subdomain separation is the cheap structural fix**: send transactional mail from `mail.example.com` and bulk from `news.example.com`, so a campaign that earns complaints doesn't drag password resets down with it.

### The feedback loop and the suppression list

The ESP reports events back: delivered, hard bounce (the address doesn't exist — permanent), soft bounce (mailbox full, greylisted, temporarily unavailable — transient), complaint (the recipient marked it as spam, reported through a feedback loop), unsubscribe.

Hard bounces, complaints, and unsubscribes must flow into a **suppression list** that the send path consults before every message. This is the single most-skipped piece and the one doing the most work: continuing to mail addresses that don't exist is the clearest possible signal to a receiving provider that you are not maintaining your list, and continuing to mail someone who marked you as spam is how you convert one complaint into a pattern.

Soft bounces are different and must be treated differently. Suppressing on the first soft bounce deletes real customers whose mailbox was briefly full.

### Rendering

You have to decide where templates live. **In your codebase** (MJML, react-email, or plain templates) they're versioned with the code that sends them, testable in CI, and can read locale from your existing i18n. **In the ESP** they can be edited without a deploy, which marketing will want, at the cost of content and logic living in two systems and a preview step nobody can run locally.

Either way, email HTML is not web HTML. Tables for layout, inline CSS, no reliable flexbox or grid in Outlook's rendering engine, images blocked by default in many clients. Always send a `text/plain` alternative — some filters treat its absence as a signal, and some recipients genuinely read it.

### Idempotency

Email delivery is at-least-once, and the duplicate usually comes from your side: a send that succeeded but whose acknowledgement was lost, retried by your job runner. An **idempotency key** derived from the causing event — `receipt:{invoiceId}`, `reset:{tokenId}` — lets either the provider or your own send table recognize the second attempt as a repeat.

There is still a window, so the more durable habit is to design messages that survive being read twice. A receipt is fine. "Your trial ends in one hour," five times, is not.

### Tracking

Open tracking works by embedding a 1×1 image whose fetch is logged. Click tracking works by rewriting every link to route through the provider's domain first.

Both are worth less than they appear. Apple's Mail Privacy Protection pre-fetches images for a large share of recipients, which means open rates are inflated by an unknown constant — they're usable as a relative trend within one audience and near-useless as an absolute number. Link rewriting replaces your domain with the provider's in every URL, which costs trust with recipients and, when the rewriting domain is shared and abused, occasionally costs deliverability. And on transactional mail specifically, tracking whether someone opened their own invoice is data you probably don't want to be holding.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any product where email sits on a critical path.** Signup confirmation, password reset, payment receipt, team invitation. At 200 messages a day, none of the volume-driven arguments apply — but a password reset in a spam folder is a support ticket and possibly a churned user, and that's enough to justify authentication and a suppression list on their own.
- **Around 10,000 messages a month and up**, bounce and complaint handling stops being optional hygiene and starts being the thing standing between you and a reputation problem you can't quickly undo.
- **Above 5,000 messages a day to Gmail or Yahoo recipients**, the bulk-sender rules are a hard gate: SPF, DKIM, DMARC, one-click unsubscribe, complaint rate under 0.3%.
- **Multi-tenant B2B where customers want mail to come from their own domain.** Now you're generating DKIM keys per tenant, handing customers DNS records, and verifying they've been published. That's infrastructure by definition, and it needs to exist before the first enterprise deal asks for it.
- **Regulated or auditable messaging** — anything where "we notified the customer on this date" has to be defensible. That requires the delivery-state record, not just an application log line.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **An internal tool for 30 people on the same Google Workspace.** Relay through the workspace SMTP endpoint and stop. No suppression list, no webhooks, no DMARC project — you control both ends.
- **A dedicated IP below roughly 100,000 messages a month.** Too little volume to establish a reputation, and warming becomes a scheduled chore. The shared pool is better for you at that size, not worse.
- **Running your own MTA to save money.** At 50,000 messages a month an ESP costs tens of dollars. IP warming, TLS, blocklist delisting requests, feedback-loop enrollment, and bounce parsing cost a person. The arithmetic only flips at genuinely large volume or under a data-residency constraint that forbids the alternative.
- **Multi-provider failover before you've had a single provider outage.** It doubles your DNS surface, your reputation surface, and your template surface to insure against an event you haven't yet observed.
- **Open tracking on transactional mail.** All of the privacy cost, none of the insight.
- **A notification-preferences matrix on day one.** Two categories and a global unsubscribe covers almost everything until users start complaining about a specific message type.

## Trade-offs

- **You now operate a queue.** Failed sends become a backlog — a dead-letter queue full of unsent invoices is a real state your system can be in, and it requires monitoring that didn't exist when the send was inline.
- **Debugging leaves your machine.** "It went to spam for one recipient behind a corporate filter" is close to unreproducible. You are reading webhook payloads and raw headers, and frequently the honest answer to a customer is that you don't know why.
- **Truth is delayed, so your data model gets more complicated.** A message-state table, a support view that speaks it, and a UI that doesn't claim "sent" when it means "queued."
- **Suppression lists create their own bug class.** A customer who genuinely wants mail is suppressed by a two-year-old bounce from a mailbox that has since been fixed. Support needs a documented way to unsuppress, and that path needs an audit trail so it isn't used to re-add complainers.
- **Provider coupling leaks.** Template syntax, webhook payload shapes, message-stream concepts, and per-provider quirks spread through your code. Migrating providers is a project, not a config change — and the deliverability of the new provider is unknown until you're on it.
- **DNS becomes part of your deploy surface**, with a failure mode unlike any other you have: tightening DMARC to `p=reject` while a forgotten system still sends as your domain silently destroys that system's mail, and you find out days later from a human.
- **Cost scales with volume and with features** — dedicated IPs, longer event retention, and inbox-placement testing are all line items that arrive after the initial decision.

## Alternatives

**Direct SMTP through a mailbox provider.** Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 relay. Genuinely fine at small volume and zero effort. Hard sending caps, no event webhooks, and no separation between your product's mail and your employees' mail — which means a product problem becomes an employee-mail problem.

**Self-hosted MTA** (Postfix, Haraka, an in-house sender). You own IP reputation, delisting, feedback-loop enrollment, and the bounce parser. Justified at very high volume where per-message pricing dominates, or under data-residency rules that forbid a third party touching message content. Rarely justified otherwise, and the failure mode is expensive to reverse.

**Multiple providers behind an abstraction.** Resilience and pricing leverage, paid for with a lowest-common-denominator feature set, two sets of DNS records, two reputations to maintain, and a routing decision that has to be right under pressure.

**Not sending the email.** The strongest alternative and the least considered. An in-app notification center or a push notification has no deliverability problem at all because there's no intermediary grading it. A meaningful share of "our emails go to spam" is better solved by moving the message to a channel you control and sending less email — which raises engagement on what's left, which improves the reputation of the messages that genuinely have to be email. That's the subject of the next few articles in this module.

## How to Achieve It

The rungs, in the order that pays off fastest:

**L0 — inline SMTP call.** Where most systems start.

**L1 — off the request path.** Enqueue a job; a worker sends. This one change removes the latency coupling and the availability coupling together, and it's usually an afternoon. Note the dual write it creates: enqueue before the transaction commits and you can email about a user that doesn't exist; enqueue after and a crash in between loses the message silently. The clean version writes the intent to an outbox table inside the same transaction — the transactional outbox pattern, which shows up anywhere a database write must be paired with an external effect.

**L2 — one chokepoint.** Every email in the system goes through a single module taking a template id and a payload. Nothing is gained yet; everything afterwards becomes a change in one place rather than forty.

**L3 — authenticate the domain.** SPF and DKIM on a dedicated sending subdomain, DMARC at `p=none`, then read the aggregate reports for a few weeks to find the systems you forgot were sending as you — the CRM, the ticketing tool, a script on someone's laptop. Then tighten to `quarantine`, then `reject`. *Signal to start:* you're sending to external users at all.

**L4 — close the loop.** A webhook endpoint writing delivery events to a message-state table, plus a suppression list the send path checks. *Signal:* the first "why didn't they get it" ticket you can't answer, or the first hard bounce you can't prove you stopped mailing.

**L5 — split the streams.** Separate subdomain and separate provider stream or IP pool for bulk versus transactional. *Signal:* the first marketing campaign, or the first time a campaign's complaint rate is visible in transactional delivery numbers.

**L6 — dedicated IP and warming.** *Signal:* above roughly 100k messages a month, with evidence of shared-pool throttling.

Most products are correct to stop at L4.

## Implementation

TypeScript. Two pieces: the send path and the return path.

The send is written as a queued job, with the enqueue happening inside the caller's transaction:

```ts
type EmailIntent = {
  userId: string;
  template: "password_reset" | "receipt" | "invite";
  data: Record<string, unknown>;
  idempotencyKey: string; // "receipt:inv_8812" — derived from the cause, not random
};

// Called inside the transaction that caused it. The outbox row commits
// or rolls back with the business change; a relay publishes it after.
async function requestEmail(tx: Tx, intent: EmailIntent) {
  await tx.outbox.insert({ type: "email", payload: intent, key: intent.idempotencyKey });
}
```

The worker:

```ts
async function deliver(intent: EmailIntent) {
  const user = await users.byId(intent.userId);

  // Checked here, not at enqueue time: a bounce or complaint may have
  // arrived while this job sat in the queue.
  if (await suppressions.contains(user.email, streamFor(intent.template))) return;

  const { subject, html, text } = render(intent.template, intent.data, user.locale);

  const res = await esp.send({
    to: user.email,
    from: "Acme <no-reply@mail.acme.com>",
    subject, html, text,
    messageStream: "transactional",
    idempotencyKey: intent.idempotencyKey,
  });

  await messages.record({
    key: intent.idempotencyKey,
    providerId: res.messageId,
    state: "accepted",
  });
}
```

Two things are worth saying out loud about that function. `state: "accepted"` is deliberately not `"sent"` — it's the only claim the provider's response actually supports. And the idempotency key is only as good as the provider's honouring of it; if yours doesn't support one, check `messages` for the key before calling `esp.send` and accept that a crash between the send and the record still produces a duplicate. There is no arrangement that removes that window entirely, which is the argument for messages that tolerate being read twice.

The return path. Note that acknowledging the webhook and processing it are separated — providers retry on timeout, so a slow handler manufactures duplicate events:

```ts
app.post("/webhooks/email", verifyProviderSignature, async (req, res) => {
  // Persist raw, acknowledge, process out of band. Acknowledging before
  // durably storing loses events; processing before acknowledging causes
  // the provider to retry and send them again.
  await rawEvents.insertMany(req.body.events);
  res.sendStatus(200);
});

async function processEvent(e: ProviderEvent) {
  await messages.updateState(e.providerMessageId, e.type);

  switch (e.type) {
    case "hard_bounce":
    case "complaint":
    case "unsubscribe":
      await suppressions.add(e.recipient, { reason: e.type, at: e.timestamp });
      break;

    case "soft_bounce":
      // Transient: full mailbox, greylisting, a receiver having a bad day.
      // Suppressing on the first one deletes paying customers.
      const n = await softBounces.increment(e.recipient);
      if (n >= 5) await suppressions.add(e.recipient, { reason: "repeated_soft_bounce" });
      break;
  }
}
```

Three small decisions in there carry most of the value: suppression is keyed by address and stream (so unsubscribing from the digest doesn't suppress the invoice), complaints are treated as permanently disqualifying rather than as a preference toggle, and soft bounces are counted rather than obeyed.

## Further Reading

- **RFC 5321** — SMTP. Worth reading the reply-code section specifically; it makes the "accepted, not delivered" distinction unambiguous in a way no vendor documentation does.
- **RFC 6376** (DKIM), **RFC 7208** (SPF), **RFC 7489** (DMARC). Read DMARC first — alignment is the idea that makes the other two mean something.
- **RFC 8617** — ARC, which exists because forwarding breaks SPF and sometimes DKIM. Relevant the moment your users have mailing lists.
- **RFC 8058** — one-click unsubscribe (`List-Unsubscribe-Post`), now a hard requirement for bulk senders.
- **Google's Email sender guidelines** and **Yahoo's sender requirements** — the 2024 bulk-sender rules, and the closest thing to a specification for what the large receivers actually enforce.
- **M3AAWG Sender Best Common Practices** — the industry consensus document behind most vendor deliverability advice, without the vendor.
- **Postmark's and Mailgun's deliverability guides** — vendor material, but the honest kind; both explain the failure modes rather than only the product.
- **MJML** and **react-email** docs, for the rendering problem specifically.

A **Go sibling** would earn its place here, but for the worker rather than the send: this article's queue, retry, and webhook-processing shape is where Go's concurrency primitives and the standard library's `net/smtp` make the delivery pipeline's backpressure and rate-limiting concerns visible in a way the TypeScript version keeps hidden behind a provider SDK. Not yet written.

The transactional outbox referenced at L1 deserves its own article and gets one under event-driven architecture; this is the third place in the collection where a database write paired with an external effect has produced the same seam.

---

The habit worth taking from this beyond email: whenever your code calls something that acknowledges *receipt* rather than *completion* — an email provider, a push service, an SMS gateway, an outbound webhook, a payment processor — the same four pieces follow. Get it off the request path. Give it an idempotency key derived from the cause. Build somewhere for the delayed result to land. And keep a list of destinations you've learned to stop sending to. The rest of this module is that pattern applied to channels where the intermediary is friendlier, but never absent.
