# Notification Orchestration

_Module 14.5 — Email & Notification Systems_

## The Problem

The product now has every channel in this module. Email came first. The in-app inbox was added by the collaboration team. Push arrived with the mobile app. The Slack integration was built by whoever closed the enterprise deal that needed it. Each of them works.

At 11:07pm, someone posts a comment on a document.

A user's phone buzzes. Ten seconds later their laptop buzzes, because the Slack integration posted to the team channel. An email arrives. The web app's badge increments. **Four interruptions, one fact.** They read it on the phone, reply, and their reply generates four more for everyone else — one of whom is the original commenter, who is notified about the response to their own comment through four channels, one of which they are currently looking at.

Nobody built this. It's what you get automatically when four independent systems each answer the question "should I tell them?" and none of them can see the others. There is no place in the codebase where the sentence "this person is about to receive four copies of the same thing" is even expressible.

**The second failure is the mirror of the first.** Three weeks later, the same user's card expires and their account will be suspended in 48 hours. That notification is one email, in a mailbox that now contains forty a day from this product, and it looks exactly like the other thirty-nine. It was the only one that mattered and the system had no way to say so. Volume didn't just annoy the user; it destroyed the channel's ability to signal anything.

**So the team builds a preferences page.** Eleven notification types across four channels, minus the impossible combinations: thirty-four checkboxes. Three percent of users ever open it. Most of those who do use it exactly once, to turn everything off, and never come back. The page didn't give users control — it moved a decision the team couldn't make onto people with far less context, and then measured the result as engagement.

**And the digest that was supposed to fix the volume** runs daily at 9am, server time, which is 2am for a third of the customer base.

Look at what all four have in common. Not one of them is a channel problem. Email works, push works, Slack works. What's missing is a **decision** — a single place that knows this recipient, this event, everything else they've received recently, what they've told you they want, what time it is where they are, and which channels can actually reach them right now.

## What It Is

**Notification orchestration** is a layer between events and channels that decides, per recipient and per event, whether to notify at all, on which channels, at what time, and bundled with what else.

Structurally it replaces N×M scattered send calls with one pipeline. Domain code publishes an event and stops caring; the pipeline works out the rest.

Three concepts get conflated constantly here, and untangling them is most of the work, so this is the place to do it clearly.

**Consent** is permission: may we contact this person on this channel for this purpose? It's a legal and platform question — a marketing opt-in, an OS notification grant, a carrier-level SMS opt-out. Its owner is usually outside engineering, and violating it has consequences beyond an annoyed user.

**Preference** is desire: does this person want this? Expressed by the user, defaulted by you, and — importantly — *soft*. It has a floor beneath which it doesn't apply.

**Suppression** is capability: can we actually reach them? A hard-bounced address, an unregistered push token, a number the carrier says is disconnected. It's a fact about the world, discovered from feedback, not a choice by anybody.

The precedence rules follow from the definitions. Suppression and consent are hard gates — no preference and no urgency overrides them. Preference is soft and stops at the **transactional floor**: nobody gets to opt out of "your account will be suspended in 48 hours," and pretending otherwise produces both a support problem and, for billing and security notices, a legal one. Systems that model all three as one boolean per user end up either spamming people who opted out or failing to deliver a suspension notice, and usually both.

One neighbour worth naming and setting aside: **this is not a message queue, and it's not a workflow engine.** A queue moves messages that have already been decided on; orchestration decides whether a message should exist. Escalation chains — page, wait, page again, escalate — genuinely *are* workflows with durable state and timers, and that's the one part of this layer that borrows its machinery from workflow orchestration rather than inventing its own.

## Recognizing the Problem

- One event produces the same message on three or four channels.
- The preferences page is a type × channel matrix, usage is negligible, and the modal action is "disable everything."
- Notification-sending code appears in twenty files across six modules.
- Nobody can answer "what did we send this user yesterday, and why?" without reading logs from four systems.
- Digests, reminders, and scheduled sends run on server time.
- An account-suspension warning and a "someone reacted to your comment" are mechanically identical in every respect.
- Users ask for "just the important ones" and there is no representation of importance anywhere in the system.
- Adding a notification type requires touching an email template, a push payload, a Slack block builder, and the preferences UI — so people stop adding types, or stop asking permission.
- The email unsubscribe link either disables password resets too, or doesn't exist.
- A user who belongs to two workspaces receives two of everything.
- A bulk import of 5,000 rows produces 5,000 notifications.
- The person who caused an event is notified about it.

## How It Works

The layer is a pipeline. Every stage can drop, defer, or modify, and each one exists because of a specific failure above.

```
domain event
   │
   ▼
[1] audience resolution   who could plausibly care?  watchers, mentions, roles
   │
   ▼
[2] relevance & dedup     are they the actor? do they already know? is this a repeat?
   │
   ▼
[3] the three gates       consent (may we) · preference (do they want it) · suppression (can we)
   │
   ▼
[4] batching              send now, or open a window and bundle?
   │
   ▼
[5] scheduling            now, or 8am in their timezone? quiet hours, with an override
   │
   ▼
[6] channel selection     which surfaces, in what order, with what fallback
   │
   ▼
[7] rendering             one content model → N channel-specific renderings
   │
   ▼
 send ──► feedback: delivered, opened, acted on
              │
              ├──► back to [3] as suppression
              └──► back to [6] to cancel a pending fallback
```

### 1. Audience resolution

Recipients come from four places: **explicit subscription** (I'm watching this project), **implicit subscription** (I commented, so now I'm watching), **role membership** (all workspace admins), and **direct address** (I was mentioned).

The design point that matters is that the publisher must not know the audience. `document.commented` is a fact about a document; who hears about it is a policy that changes without the document code changing. Getting this boundary wrong is how notification logic ends up in twenty files.

### 2. Relevance and deduplication

The most common bug in every notification system is notifying the actor about their own action. The second most common is notifying someone who was demonstrably looking at the thing when it happened.

Beyond that, there's notification **identity**: `(recipient, type, object, window)`. Two events that resolve to the same identity are one notification. This is the same group key the inbox used for aggregation, reused one layer up — which is worth noticing, because if both layers dedupe independently with different keys you get results that are difficult to explain to anyone.

### 3. The three gates

Consent, preference, and suppression, evaluated in one place, in that order, each producing a reason when it blocks. The transactional floor is a property of the notification type, not of the user: types are marked as overriding preference (never consent, never suppression), and the list of such types should be short, reviewed, and hard to add to.

### 4. Batching

Three timing modes cover nearly everything:

**Immediate** — the default for anything urgent and actionable.

**Window batching** — the first event opens a window of N minutes; everything arriving inside it joins the same notification; the window fires on schedule. This is the fix for bursty activity, and the implementation detail is easy to get wrong: **do not reschedule the timer on each new event.** That's a debounce, and under sustained activity a debounce never fires at all — the busiest thread in your product becomes the one that never notifies anybody. You want a fixed window from the first event, or a debounce with a hard maximum wait.

**Scheduled digest** — daily or weekly, at a fixed time in the recipient's timezone.

Digests have a content problem that batching doesn't: a digest of two hundred items is exactly as useless as two hundred notifications, just cheaper to ignore. A digest needs ranking and truncation — the five things most likely to matter, plus a count — which means the pipeline needs some notion of per-item importance to rank by. That requirement tends to arrive as a surprise late in the project.

### 5. Scheduling and quiet hours

Quiet hours require the recipient's timezone, and the only reliable source is the client reporting it — not IP geolocation, which is wrong for travellers and VPN users in exactly the way that produces 3am notifications.

Two rules make deferred delivery work. Quiet hours need an **override for the transactional floor**: a security alert at 3am should still fire, because that's what it's for. And a notification held until morning must be **re-validated at send time, not merely re-timed**. In the eight hours it waited, the issue may have been resolved, the mention may have been deleted, the user may have read it in the app, or a later event may have superseded it. Sending a stale notification is worse than sending none, and it's the specific failure that makes users distrust digests.

### 6. Channel selection and fallback

Three strategies, only one of which is usually right.

**Parallel** — send to every enabled channel. This is the 11:07pm bug from the top of the article, and it is what you get by default when nobody chooses.

**Fallback with cancellation** — try the cheapest channel that plausibly reaches them, hold the next one, and cancel it if the first works. The concrete version is worth stating because it's cheap and it removes most duplication on its own: **write the inbox row immediately, push immediately, and schedule the email for five minutes later — then cancel the email if the notification is seen.** Almost everyone who was going to see it sees it in that window, and the people who get the email are exactly the people who needed it.

**Escalation** — page, wait for acknowledgement, page again, escalate to the secondary, then call. This is a stateful workflow with durable timers, it belongs to alerting products, and it's the only part of this layer that justifies reaching for real workflow machinery.

The general rule underneath all three: **default to the cheapest channel that reaches them, and escalate only on non-acknowledgement.** That requires an acknowledgement signal, which the module has already established you get reliably from the inbox, weakly from push taps, barely from email, and not at all from SMS.

### 7. Rendering

A notification has one canonical content model — type, actor, object, a few fields — and each channel has a renderer that turns it into that channel's constraints: 140 plain-text characters, a Block Kit card, an HTML email, a push title and body, an inbox row.

This is the stage that determines whether channels are cheap or expensive to add. Get it right and a new notification type is one content definition that automatically works everywhere. Get it wrong and every new type is five implementations, which is why teams with five channels quietly stop adding notifications — and then add them outside the pipeline instead.

## When It's Appropriate

- **More than two channels, or more than about ten notification types.** Below that, direct sends are honest and the abstraction costs more than it returns.
- **Whenever one event can reach one person by more than one path.** That's the duplication trigger, and it arrives the day the second channel ships.
- **When critical and trivial notifications share a system.** A billing failure and a reaction emoji in the same pipeline need a priority concept, or the pipeline flattens them.
- **When teams other than the notifications team add notification types.** The orchestration layer is what makes that delegation safe: the gates, the floor, and the volume controls apply to everyone's features automatically.
- **B2B products where customers have their own policies** — admin-set workspace defaults, org-wide quiet hours, compliance rules about what may be sent where.
- **Alerting and on-call products**, where escalation isn't an optimization but the entire value proposition.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **One channel, three notification types.** Send them. The pipeline is solving problems you don't have.
- **Building all seven stages before duplication exists.** Stages 1, 3, and 7 — a chokepoint, the gates, and the content model — pay for themselves immediately. The rest are answers to observed complaints.
- **A preferences matrix as the first response to complaints about volume.** Better defaults are cheaper, more effective, and don't require users to do work. The honest first move is usually to delete notification types.
- **Per-user quiet hours while the digest still runs on server time.** Fix the timezone bug; you may discover that was the whole complaint.
- **Escalation chains in a product that isn't alerting.** Durable timers and acknowledgement state are real infrastructure to carry for a feature nobody asked for.
- **A generic, config-driven rules engine for routing.** This is the characteristic overengineering failure in this domain and it is extremely tempting: build a DSL for notification rules, then a UI for the DSL, then a debugger for the UI, and discover several quarters in that the rules were ordinary business logic that would have been twenty lines of code and readable by anyone. Rules engines earn their place when *customers* need to author rules. They almost never earn it when only you do.
- **Buying a notification platform at four notification types.** You'll spend the integration effort you saved on synchronizing user data.

## Trade-offs

- **You've built a central dependency for every team's features.** It's a bottleneck for changes, a single point of failure for delivery, and a queue that, when it backs up, silently stops the whole product communicating.
- **You've deliberately made notifications slower.** Batch windows, quiet hours, and fallback delays all trade timeliness for signal. That's the right trade and someone will still file a bug about it, so the delays need to be explainable and per-type rather than global.
- **"Why didn't Alice get this?" now has seven possible answers.** Without a per-decision trace the layer becomes unsupportable within months — support cannot debug it, so they escalate everything to engineering, who also can't debug it. The trace is not optional instrumentation; it's a functional requirement, and it needs a retention period and a UI someone in support can actually use.
- **Preference state is a schema with a migration problem.** New notification types need a sane default for users with no row; removed types leave orphans; and a category reorganization silently changes what people receive.
- **The combinatorics defeat end-to-end testing.** Types × channels × preferences × timing × timezone is not enumerable, so you test stages in isolation and accept that some combinations ship unverified.
- **Deferred and cancellable sends require durable timers** that survive deploys, don't fire twice, and can be cancelled by an event arriving from elsewhere. That's genuine infrastructure, and it's the piece most likely to be underestimated.
- **A shared content model is a lowest common denominator.** Occasionally a channel would have been better served by a bespoke integration, and the pipeline makes that awkward rather than impossible — which is the correct trade, but it does mean your Slack messages are slightly worse than a Slack-only product's.
- **Users who exist in multiple tenants complicate every stage** — audience, dedup, preference scope, and digest assembly all have to decide whether they operate on the person or on the membership.

## Alternatives

**One channel per notification type, chosen at design time.** No preferences, no pipeline: the team that owns a notification picks its channel and that's the end of it. Crude, free, and it eliminates duplication completely. It loses fallback, user control, and digests, and it is entirely adequate for a product with one team and a dozen notification types.

**Let the customer orchestrate.** Route everything to Slack or a webhook and let them mute, thread, and filter with tools they already know. For B2B this is the inverted-routing argument from the last article, and it's a real alternative to building stages 3 through 6 rather than merely a complement to them.

**A notification platform** — Knock, Courier, Novu, Braze. This is where such products genuinely earn their keep, far more than for the inbox alone: workflows, preference models, batching, and quiet hours are exactly what they sell. The cost is that routing policy, which is business logic, now lives outside your codebase and reads user data from a copy you have to keep synchronized.

**A workflow engine for the escalation subset.** If you already run Temporal or Step Functions, escalation chains map onto them cleanly and you get durable timers, retries, and visibility for free. Adopting one *for* notifications is a much bigger commitment than the feature justifies.

**Deleting notification types.** The highest-performing intervention available, requiring no architecture at all. Most products have several notification types that exist because someone could build them, and removing one improves every remaining notification's signal. It's worth doing before building anything in this article, and worth doing again afterwards.

## How to Achieve It

**L1 — one chokepoint.** Every notification in the system goes through `notify(event, audience)`. Nothing is smarter yet; this is the same move as the email article's L2, and like that one it buys nothing immediately and makes everything afterwards possible. *Signal to start:* the second channel ships.

**L2 — content model and per-channel renderers.** A new notification type becomes one definition. *Signal:* someone avoids adding a notification because of how many places it would touch.

**L3 — the three gates in one place, with a why-not trace.** Consent, preference with a transactional floor, suppression — plus a durable record of every drop and its reason. *Signal:* the first "why didn't they get it" that takes more than ten minutes to answer.

**L4 — categories, frequency, and per-object mute.** Ship *mute this thread* first: it's the single highest-value control, it's asked for by name, and it's a per-object preference row rather than a settings page. Then categories with good defaults and an immediate/digest/never axis. *Signal:* users asking for fewer of one thing rather than fewer of everything.

**L5 — delay and cancel.** Hold the email five minutes; cancel it when the inbox row is seen. Cheapest possible fix for cross-channel duplication. *Signal:* the 11:07pm complaint.

**L6 — batch windows and digests**, per category, in the recipient's timezone, with re-validation at send. *Signal:* a single actor's activity burst produces more than a couple of notifications.

**L7 — quiet hours with a transactional override, and escalation chains** if the product is an alerting product. *Signal:* for the first, any user in a timezone other than yours; for the second, customers describing on-call rotations.

## Implementation

TypeScript. The pipeline is a sequence of gates that each return a decision with a reason, which is exactly the shape a discriminated union is for — and it means the why-not trace falls out of the design rather than being bolted on.

```ts
type Decision =
  | { kind: "continue" }
  | { kind: "drop"; reason: string }
  | { kind: "defer"; until: Date; reason: string }
  | { kind: "batch"; windowKey: string; reason: string };

type Stage = (ctx: Ctx) => Promise<Decision>;

const pipeline: Stage[] = [
  notActor,          // don't tell someone about their own action
  notAlreadyKnown,   // they were viewing the object when it happened
  notDuplicate,      // same (recipient, type, object) inside the dedup window
  consentGate,       // hard: legal / platform permission
  suppressionGate,   // hard: bounced, unregistered, opted out
  preferenceGate,    // soft: honours the transactional floor
  batchWindow,       // may return { kind: "batch" }
  quietHours,        // may return { kind: "defer" }, unless floor
];

async function decide(ctx: Ctx): Promise<Decision> {
  for (const stage of pipeline) {
    const d = await stage(ctx);
    // Every decision is recorded, including the boring ones. This table is
    // what support reads six weeks from now, and it is the whole reason
    // this layer stays debuggable.
    await trace.record(ctx.notificationId, stage.name, d);
    if (d.kind !== "continue") return d;
  }
  return { kind: "continue" };
}
```

Two things about that. The gates are ordered cheapest-first and hardest-first: `notActor` is a field comparison and drops a large fraction of everything, while `preferenceGate` may hit two tables. And `preferenceGate` sits *after* both hard gates deliberately — a user's stated preference is never consulted for a decision that consent or suppression has already made, which keeps the transactional floor from ever being able to override a legal opt-out.

The scheduling half is where the two subtle mechanics live:

```ts
async function dispatch(ctx: Ctx, d: Decision) {
  if (d.kind === "batch") {
    await batches.append(d.windowKey, ctx.notificationId);
    // Schedule ONCE, on the first event in the window. Rescheduling on each
    // event is a debounce: under sustained activity the timer never expires
    // and the busiest thread in the product notifies nobody.
    await timers.scheduleIfAbsent(d.windowKey, ctx.now.plus(WINDOW));
    return;
  }

  if (d.kind === "defer") {
    // Re-validated on wake, not just re-timed — see flushDeferred below.
    await timers.schedule(`deferred:${ctx.notificationId}`, d.until);
    return;
  }

  // Immediate path: the durable record first, then the free channels,
  // then a cancellable fallback on the channels that cost something.
  await inbox.write(ctx);
  await push.send(ctx);
  await timers.schedule(`email:${ctx.notificationId}`, ctx.now.plus(minutes(5)));
}

// Cancels the pending email when the user has already seen it elsewhere.
// This single line removes most cross-channel duplication.
onInboxSeen(async (notificationId) => {
  await timers.cancel(`email:${notificationId}`);
});

async function flushDeferred(notificationId: string) {
  const n = await notifications.get(notificationId);
  // Eight hours passed. Any of these may now be true.
  if (n.objectDeleted || n.superseded || n.seenInApp) {
    return trace.record(notificationId, "flushDeferred", {
      kind: "drop", reason: "stale_on_wake",
    });
  }
  await channels.send(n);
}
```

`scheduleIfAbsent` versus `schedule` is a one-word difference with a large behavioural gap, and it's the sort of thing that passes every test written by someone who isn't generating sustained load. Worth noticing too that `timers` here is carrying more weight than its name suggests: it needs to survive deploys, fire at-least-once without sending twice, and accept cancellation from an unrelated event. That's the piece to buy rather than build, whether from a job runner with delayed jobs or a workflow engine.

## Further Reading

- **Google's _Site Reliability Engineering_, chapters 6 ("Monitoring Distributed Systems") and 11 ("Being On-Call").** The alert-fatigue material is the most rigorous public treatment of "volume destroys signal," and every argument transfers to product notifications without modification.
- **PagerDuty's documentation on escalation policies, notification rules, and urgency.** The most mature publicly-inspectable model of escalation and per-urgency channel routing that exists; worth reading even if you never build escalation, because the vocabulary is good.
- **GitHub's notification settings**, used as a design study rather than as documentation. Watching versus participating versus mentions, per-repository overrides, and the reasons attached to each notification are a working answer to the preferences problem that avoids the thirty-four-checkbox failure — and you can inspect the whole thing in an afternoon.
- **Temporal's documentation on timers, signals, and cancellation** — the clearest explanation of what durable, cancellable scheduling actually requires, and useful as a specification even if you implement it on a job queue.
- **Knock's and Courier's documentation on workflow and preference data models.** Vendor material, and the schemas are instructive: they show which distinctions survive contact with many customers, particularly around preference hierarchies and channel fallback.
- **Nielsen Norman Group's writing on notification and interruption design**, for the half of this problem that no architecture solves.

A **Go sibling** would reveal what this article keeps behind an interface: the scheduler. Durable timers on a job queue, cancellation racing with firing, exactly-once-ish delivery of a delayed send, and batch windows under sustained load are all mechanics that a TypeScript pipeline can gesture at and a Go implementation with an explicit queue has to confront. The decision pipeline is the interesting part in one language; the timers are the interesting part in the other. Not yet written.

---

Five articles, one argument. Email established that a channel with an intermediary reports acceptance rather than delivery, and that the feedback loop is what keeps it alive. The inbox established that durability and per-user state are the substance of a notification system and real-time is an optimization over it. Push established that some channels are bought with a permission that's global, one-shot, and silently revocable. SMS established that a channel can have a marginal cost and a regulator, and that cost is sometimes the only thing stopping a product from sending too much.

This article is what happens when you hold all four at once, and the thing it should leave behind is a shift in where the difficulty lives. Delivering a message is a solved problem with vendors, SDKs, and documentation. **Deciding whether to deliver it is not, and it's the part nobody owns.** Every system that turns internal events into human interruption — notifications, alerting, monitoring, automated email, anything that can make a phone buzz — is ultimately rationing one resource that doesn't scale with your infrastructure, doesn't get cheaper, and can't be recovered once you've spent it. The quality of such a system is not measured by what it can send. It's measured by how well it says no.
