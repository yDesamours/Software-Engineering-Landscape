# SMS and Alternative Channels

_Module 14.4 — Email & Notification Systems_

## The Problem

A clinic scheduling product. The no-show rate is 18%, every no-show is an empty thirty-minute slot, and the clinics know exactly what that costs them. The product emails a reminder the day before. Open rate is around 40%, and the patients least likely to open it are precisely the ones most likely not to show up.

Push is unavailable — patients don't install an app for two appointments a year, and wouldn't grant notification permission if they did. The in-app inbox is irrelevant to someone who has no reason to log in.

So the team adds SMS reminders.

**It works immediately.** No-shows drop by a third in the first month. That's worth saying plainly, because the rest of this section is about what went wrong and it would be easy to take away the wrong lesson: SMS is the most effective notification channel most products will ever ship. The problems are not that it doesn't work.

**Month three: the bill.** 40,000 reminders at under a cent each was a rounding error. Then someone added an SMS for every appointment change, and a confirmation on booking, and the clinic chain that opened in three other countries turned out to cost six times as much per message. Notification volume had never been a budget question before. Now every new notification type is a spend decision, and the people adding them have no idea.

**Month five: the block.** Delivery to US numbers collapses over a weekend. No error — the provider accepts everything, delivery receipts just stop coming back positive. Carriers had begun filtering the traffic because it was application-to-person messaging sent from an unregistered number. A registration regime existed. Nobody on the team had heard of it, and registration takes weeks.

**Month six: the bill again, differently.** The signup flow has SMS phone verification. A bot farm discovers it and starts requesting codes to premium-rate numbers in a handful of countries where the terminating carrier shares revenue with whoever drives traffic to it. The invoice is $38,000. The attacker was paid out of it.

**Month seven: the replies.** A patient texts back "CANCEL" the evening before. Nobody has ever opened the inbound queue. Another texts "STOP", which the provider handles automatically and correctly — and that number is now opted out of everything, including the reminder they actually wanted, with no way to distinguish the two cases.

Meanwhile, in the same product, a completely different reachability problem. The staff-facing alerts — *a patient cancelled, the 2pm slot is free* — go to receptionists who spend the day in a shared inbox and a Slack workspace. SMS would be absurd. There's no app to push to. The messages are getting lost in email, and the customer has already asked twice whether the product can "just post it in our Slack."

Both halves are the same question. **What do you do when you need to reach someone and you don't own a surface to reach them on?** There are two answers, and they're opposites.

## What It Is

You can **rent the carrier network**, or you can **borrow another product's surface**.

**Carrier channels** — SMS, MMS, RCS, WhatsApp, voice — are addressed by phone number and delivered by the global telephone network through an intermediary such as Twilio, Vonage, MessageBird, or SNS. Universal reach, no app, no permission prompt, a real per-message cost, and a regulatory regime enforced by carriers rather than by your provider.

**Platform channels** — Slack, Teams, Discord, and the generic outbound webhook — are addressed by an identifier inside somebody else's product, reached through an integration the customer installed. Free, rich, interactive, and entirely subject to that platform's rules, rate limits, and continued willingness to have you.

One property of phone numbers deserves flagging immediately, because it has no analogue in the channels covered so far: **numbers are recycled.** A disconnected number is reassigned to a new subscriber, in the US typically after a minimum holding period of around 45 days. An email address you verified in 2024 is still, almost always, the same person's. A phone number you verified in 2024 might be a stranger's, and your appointment reminder is now a disclosure to them. Any long-lived phone number in your database is a claim with a decay rate.

Also worth naming and then setting aside: **SMS for one-time passcodes is a different topic with a different literature.** Its security properties — materially weaker than TOTP or passkeys against a targeted attacker, and exactly as effective against bulk credential stuffing — belong to Module 4, which covers them. What this article cares about is that OTP endpoints are the fraud surface, which is a cost problem rather than a security one.

## Recognizing the Problem

- Deadline-bearing messages go to people who have no app and no habit of checking email inside the window that matters.
- Your no-show, missed-deadline, or abandoned-flow rate is concentrated among people who demonstrably never saw the message.
- Support's answer to a time-sensitive failure is "check your spam folder."
- SMS spend appears on the infrastructure bill and no team owns the number.
- Delivery rates to one country dropped and nobody can say why.
- You send from a plain long code and have never heard of campaign registration.
- Inbound SMS goes to a queue nobody has opened.
- Phone numbers in your database have no verification date and no concept of going stale.
- An unauthenticated endpoint can cause an SMS to be sent, and there is no per-IP limit and no country allowlist in front of it.
- Customers ask whether alerts can go to their Slack and the answer is no.
- A customer has built their own integration by parsing your notification emails.
- Message copy is written once and rendered identically to a 160-character SMS and a Slack card.

## How It Works

### Carrier channels

**The path.**

```
your server ──API──► CPaaS ──► aggregator ──► carrier ──► handset
                       │                         │
                       │                         └── carrier filtering happens HERE
                       │                             (unregistered traffic, spam heuristics)
                       ▼
                 delivery receipts: queued → sent → delivered | undelivered | failed
```

Delivery receipts are a genuine improvement over push: you usually get a per-message terminal status with an error code, and "undelivered, invalid number" is information you can act on immediately. They are also less trustworthy than they look — some carriers report delivered on handoff to the handset rather than on acknowledgement, and none of them report *read*. Treat a receipt as good evidence about the network and no evidence at all about a human.

**Sender identity is per-country.** A long code, a toll-free number, a short code, or an alphanumeric sender ID ("CLINIC"), and which of those exist, what they cost, how long they take to provision, and whether replies are even possible varies by country. Alphanumeric sender IDs are normal across much of Europe and unavailable in the US. Short codes are expensive and take weeks. This is why "add SMS" is not one feature; it's one feature per market.

**Registration is a prerequisite, not a formality.** In the US, application-to-person traffic over standard 10-digit numbers requires brand and campaign registration (10DLC); toll-free numbers require verification. India has its own DLT regime, and other markets have theirs. Unregistered traffic is not rejected by your provider — it's accepted, billed, and then filtered by carriers, which is why the failure looks like month five: silence rather than an error. Lead times run days to weeks, which is the whole reason it belongs at the start of the project rather than at launch.

**Cost changes the design.** At roughly $0.008 per US segment plus carrier fees, and multiples of that internationally, SMS is the first channel in this module with a marginal cost. That has an unexpected upside: it's the only channel with a natural governor attached. Every other channel lets a team add a notification type for free, which is exactly why every other channel trends toward noise. Here, someone has to justify the spend. Used deliberately — a budget per notification type, shown next to its volume — the cost is a feature rather than a problem.

**Encoding is a cost multiplier hiding inside your copy.** SMS uses a 7-bit alphabet (GSM 03.38) giving 160 characters per segment. A single character outside it — an emoji, a curly apostrophe pasted from a design doc, an accented name — switches the entire message to UCS-2, which allows 70 characters per segment. Concatenated messages lose a few more characters per segment to header overhead. A 150-character reminder is one message; the same reminder with a smart quote is three, and you are billed for three.

**Consent and the global STOP.** Consent is legally required in most jurisdictions and has to be recorded with a timestamp and a source. STOP, UNSTOP, and HELP are handled at the provider or carrier level and apply to the whole sender, not to a message category — the same structural problem push has with OS-level permission, arriving from a completely different direction. There is no per-type opt-out to offer unless you build it in the layer above, which is §14.5's job.

**Toll fraud deserves its own paragraph.** The attack — SMS pumping, or artificially inflated traffic — works like this: an attacker finds any endpoint that causes you to send an SMS to a number they supply, usually a verification-code flow, and drives enormous volume at numbers in ranges where the terminating carrier shares revenue with whoever generates the traffic. They collect; you're invoiced. There's no breach, nothing anomalous in your application logs except volume, and the numbers get large in hours. The defences are all boring and all necessary: a country allowlist rather than a blocklist, rate limits per IP and per account and per destination prefix, a bot check in front of the trigger, a hard spend ceiling with an alert well below it, and — most effective — not using SMS as the default verification channel when something cheaper serves.

**Numbers go stale.** Re-verify periodically, treat permanent delivery failures as immediate invalidation, and note that regulators and carriers in some markets publish reassignment data specifically because this is a recognized harm rather than an edge case.

**Inbound exists whether you use it or not.** People reply to text messages; it's a conversational medium and they treat it as one. The minimum is routing replies somewhere a human reads. The version that pays for itself is a keyword flow — reply C to confirm, X to cancel — because a reminder that can be acted on without a phone call is where most of the no-show reduction actually comes from.

### Platform channels

**You are a guest.** A customer installs your integration, which grants a token scoped to their workspace and some set of channels. Your message is rendered by their client, using their formatting model — Slack's Block Kit, Teams' Adaptive Cards — and subject to their per-workspace rate limits.

**Routing control inverts, and that's the point.** With email and push, you decide who receives what, and you build preferences to let users adjust it. With Slack, the *customer* decides: which channel your alerts land in, who's in it, who mutes it, how it threads, how it forwards to their phones. You have outsourced the hardest part of notification preferences to a system your users already understand and have already configured to their taste. For B2B products this is frequently a better outcome than anything you would have built.

**Two depths of integration, and the shallow one goes a long way.** An incoming webhook is a URL the customer pastes into a settings field; you POST JSON to it and a message appears in one channel. No OAuth, no app review, no token storage beyond a string. The full app — OAuth install, channel picker, interactive buttons, slash commands, directory listing — is a real project with a review process attached. The first version covers most of the value.

**Interactivity turns a notification into a workflow.** An approve/deny button means the work happens where the alert arrived. It also creates an authorization surface that's easy to get wrong: the callback tells you a Slack user id, and you must verify the request signature, map that id to a real user in your system, and check *that user's* permissions. A button in a channel is visible to everyone in the channel, and "is in this Slack channel" is not an authorization model.

**The generic webhook** is the escape hatch for customers who want something you haven't built: you POST events to a URL they control and they do whatever they like with them. It's the cheapest possible answer to "do you integrate with X" for every value of X. Designing it properly — signatures, retries, idempotency, subscription management, delivery visibility — is a product in itself, and it's Module 17's subject.

## When It's Appropriate

**SMS**, when the message carries a deadline and the recipient has no app and no reliable email habit inside the window. Appointment and service-window reminders, delivery notifications, account-security events, outage notices to non-technical customers. The arithmetic is rarely close: if a missed appointment costs a clinic $120 and a message costs a cent, the reminder pays for itself at an effectiveness rate below one in ten thousand.

**SMS as the cheapest rung below a phone call.** Anywhere a human currently picks up the phone to chase someone, a text is a fraction of the cost and often works as well.

**Voice**, when a ringing phone is the point — on-call escalation after two unacknowledged pages, or anything where intrusiveness is the feature rather than the cost.

**WhatsApp or RCS**, in markets where SMS is expensive or unusual and the messaging app is where people actually are. Both require message templates approved in advance, which constrains what you can say and when you can say it.

**Slack or Teams**, for B2B products whose buyers live in chat: alerts, approvals, daily digests, and anything that benefits from being discussed. A threaded conversation under an alert is a collaboration feature you'd otherwise have to build, and you get it for nothing.

**A generic webhook**, for technical customers and for the long tail of integration requests you'll never build individually.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Marketing over SMS.** The most expensive channel, the least tolerant audience, and an opt-out that takes your transactional messages with it.
- **SMS for anything the user will see in the app inside the useful window.** If they're in the product daily, the inbox already did this for free.
- **SMS verification as a default** when an emailed link, a TOTP code, or a passkey serves — especially before the fraud defences above exist. The cheapest fix for toll fraud is having fewer SMS-triggering endpoints.
- **SMS for an internal tool** where all forty users are in the same Slack workspace.
- **A full Slack app** — OAuth, directory listing, interactive components — when a pasted webhook URL covers the first two years of demand.
- **Shipping five channels before two work well.** Each channel is a permanent operational commitment with its own credentials, failure modes, compliance surface, and copy. The cost of the fifth is not a fifth of the cost of five.
- **Voice for anything non-urgent.** It is the most intrusive thing you can do to someone, and it is remembered.

## Trade-offs

- **A feature that used to be free now has a variable cost**, driven by volume, geography, and copy decisions that no single team controls. Forecasting it is genuinely hard, and the first honest forecast usually arrives attached to a surprising invoice.
- **The regulatory surface is real, jurisdiction-specific, and changes without your involvement** — and non-compliance shows up as silent filtering rather than an error, so you learn about it from a metric rather than from an exception.
- **You've attached a direct financial exploit to your own API.** Few other features have the property that an attacker profits from your bill.
- **Global opt-out** means one careless campaign removes a recipient from your outage notices too.
- **160 characters, no trusted links.** Shorteners on shared domains are a spam signal and get filtered; branded short domains cost money and need warming of their own. Whatever you needed to say has to fit, in plain text, without formatting.
- **On platform channels you inherit everything**: rate limits, outages, API deprecations, review processes, and a rendering model that decides what your message looks like. When Slack changes something, your notification changes.
- **Guest status is revocable.** A customer's admin removes the app and you find out from a 401 on your next send.
- **Inverted routing cuts both ways.** Handing channel selection to the customer is mostly a gift, and it means you cannot guarantee anyone saw a critical alert — someone muted the channel, and that was their right.
- **Every added channel multiplies the copy problem.** One event now needs a Block Kit card, a 140-character text, an email subject and body, a push title, and an inbox row — and they have to degrade gracefully in both directions.

## Alternatives

**The three channels you already have, used properly.** A large share of "we need SMS" turns out to be "our email doesn't arrive," which is §14.1's problem and much cheaper to fix, or "we never built an inbox," which is §14.2's. Check that the existing channels are actually failing before adding one with a regulator attached.

**A phone call from a person.** For low-volume, high-value contact — a churning enterprise account, a failed payment on a large contract — a human calling is better than any automated channel, and is what the SMS is a cheap imitation of.

**Automated voice as an escalation tier** rather than a primary channel: text first, call if unacknowledged after N minutes.

**Physical mail.** Print-and-mail APIs exist, are unglamorous, and are occasionally correct — in some jurisdictions a mailed notice is the only form of contact with legal standing, which makes it the right channel for dunning, contract termination, and regulatory disclosure. Slow, expensive, and the only channel here that nobody can filter.

**Someone else's notification system entirely**, which for B2B usually means the platform channels above: the customer's Slack has already solved delivery, preferences, mobile push, and quiet hours, and did it better than you will.

## How to Achieve It

**L1 — one SMS type, one country, transactional only.** Provider SDK, a `phone_verified_at` column, consent recorded with timestamp and source. *Signal to start:* a deadline-bearing message that a measurable number of users demonstrably miss.

**L2 — register the traffic.** 10DLC or toll-free verification in the US, and the local equivalent in each market. *Signal:* any production volume at all. Start before you need it; the lead time is why this is a rung of its own.

**L3 — consume delivery receipts and STOP events** into the suppression store you already built for email, extended to be channel-aware. *Signal:* you are still sending to numbers that opted out or went dead.

**L4 — defend the trigger.** Country allowlist, rate limits per IP and per account and per destination prefix, a bot check on public endpoints, a spend ceiling with an alert far below it. *Signal:* any unauthenticated request can cause a send. Don't wait for a second signal.

**L5 — handle inbound.** Route replies to a human at minimum; add keyword confirm/cancel where the message asks for a decision. *Signal:* anyone has ever replied.

**L6 — platform channels, shallow first.** An incoming-webhook URL in a settings page. Upgrade to a full app when customers ask for channel selection or buttons, not before. *Signal:* the second customer asks.

**L7 — the generic outbound webhook**, which is Module 17.

## Implementation

Java. Two pieces: what has to happen before a message goes out, and what has to happen when the network reports back.

The pre-send guard is the code that doesn't exist in most SMS integrations and should:

```java
record Segments(int count, Encoding encoding) {}

static Segments segments(String body) {
    boolean gsm7 = GSM_03_38.matcher(body).matches();
    int limit  = gsm7 ? 160 : 70;   // one emoji or curly quote costs 90 characters
    int concat = gsm7 ? 153 : 67;   // concatenated segments lose room to the header
    int n = body.length() <= limit ? 1 : (int) Math.ceil((double) body.length() / concat);
    return new Segments(n, gsm7 ? Encoding.GSM7 : Encoding.UCS2);
}

public SendResult send(SmsIntent intent) {
    var number = PhoneNumber.parse(intent.to());

    // Allowlist, never blocklist. Toll fraud arrives from countries you have
    // no users in, and enumerating those after the fact is a losing game.
    if (!allowedCountries.contains(number.region())) {
        return SendResult.blocked("country_not_allowed");
    }
    if (suppressions.contains(number.e164(), Channel.SMS)) {
        return SendResult.blocked("suppressed");
    }
    if (!rateLimiter.tryAcquire(number.e164()) || !rateLimiter.tryAcquire(intent.accountId())) {
        return SendResult.blocked("rate_limited");
    }

    var seg  = segments(intent.body());
    var cost = pricing.estimate(number.region(), seg.count());

    // A ceiling, not a dashboard. The attack in month six ran overnight.
    if (!budget.reserve(intent.category(), cost)) {
        alerts.page("sms budget exhausted: " + intent.category());
        return SendResult.blocked("budget_exhausted");
    }

    return provider.send(number, intent.body(), callbackUrl(intent.id()));
}
```

The ordering is deliberate. The allowlist is first because it's the cheapest check and stops the highest-severity case; the budget reservation is last because it's the only one with a side effect worth unwinding. And `segments()` runs before pricing rather than after sending — the number of messages you're about to buy is knowable in advance, and is a product decision rather than a line on an invoice.

The return path folds into the store that already exists from the email article, with the store made channel-aware rather than duplicated:

```java
@PostMapping("/webhooks/sms")
void inbound(@RequestBody ProviderEvent e) {
    switch (e.type()) {
        case DELIVERED, SENT ->
            messages.updateState(e.messageId(), e.type());

        // The carrier says this number is invalid or unreachable, permanently.
        case FAILED_PERMANENT ->
            suppressions.add(e.from(), Channel.SMS, Reason.UNDELIVERABLE);

        // Provider-level opt-out: global to this sender by law, not by policy.
        // There is no "only the marketing ones" version of this.
        case OPT_OUT ->
            suppressions.add(e.from(), Channel.SMS, Reason.OPT_OUT);

        case INBOUND ->
            keywords.route(e.from(), e.body());
    }
}
```

The shared, channel-keyed suppression store is the structurally interesting part, and it's easy to miss because it looks like plumbing. A single user can simultaneously have bounced by email, opted out by SMS, and uninstalled the app — three separate facts about three separate channels. Something eventually has to read all three and decide what to actually do, and that something is the orchestration layer.

Here is the table that layer will need, which this module has now assembled one row at a time:

| | who owns the address | who owns consent | marginal cost | rich / interactive | durable |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Email | you | you (unsubscribe) | ~0 | limited | yes |
| In-app inbox | you | you | 0 | yes | yes |
| Push | the OS vendor | the OS, globally | 0 | limited | no |
| SMS | the carrier | the carrier, globally | real | no | on the device |
| Slack / Teams | the customer | the customer | 0 | yes | yes, theirs |
| Webhook | the customer | the customer | 0 | n/a | theirs |

Every routing decision in the next article is some function of those six columns.

## Further Reading

- **GSM 03.38**, the SMS character set — a page of tables that explains the encoding cost multiplier better than any prose about it. **3GPP TS 23.040** if you want the concatenation headers themselves.
- **Twilio's documentation on A2P 10DLC registration, messaging services, and delivery receipts.** Vendor material, but on US carrier rules it functions as the de facto specification; the error-code reference in particular is what to read before writing the receipt handler.
- **CTIA Messaging Principles and Best Practices** — the US industry consent document that carriers actually enforce, and the source of most rules a provider will quote at you.
- **FCC guidance on the TCPA and the Reassigned Numbers Database** — the regulatory framing for consent, and the official acknowledgement that recycled numbers are a known harm.
- **Provider write-ups on SMS pumping and artificially inflated traffic.** The CPaaS vendors document this attack well, precisely because they field the angry invoices; the defence checklists are short and worth copying wholesale.
- **Slack's Block Kit documentation, its rate-limit reference, and the incoming-webhooks versus apps comparison** — read the rate limits before designing anything that sends per-event.
- **Microsoft's Adaptive Cards schema** and Teams webhook documentation, which differ from Slack's model enough that a shared abstraction over both is more work than it first looks.
- **WhatsApp Business Platform message-template documentation**, for what pre-approval means in practice for your copy.

A **TypeScript sibling** would earn its place on the platform-channel half, built on Slack's Bolt SDK: the OAuth install flow, request signature verification, Block Kit rendering, and interactive action handling. The value is in seeing how much the SDK has pre-decided — token storage, the URL verification handshake, the three-second acknowledgement deadline on interactions — and what it costs to want something slightly different. Not yet written.

---

Four channels in, the pattern worth extracting is that a channel is characterized by three questions, and almost everything else follows from the answers: **who owns the address, who owns the consent, and who pays per message.** Those determine the regulation you inherit, the failure mode you'll be debugging, the opt-out you're able to offer, and — most practically — which team has to own the channel's health, because it is never the team adding the most notifications to it.
