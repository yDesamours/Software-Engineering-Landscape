# Long-Running Operations

_Module 10.4a — Background Jobs & Task Queues_

## The Problem

A document-review product. A firm uploads an archive — say 40,000 scanned pages — and the system OCRs each page, extracts entities, and builds a search index. One job, `archive.ingest`, about four hours.

**It has never finished in production.**

The deploys are the reason. They go out roughly every ninety minutes. Each one terminates the worker, the lease expires, and the job is redelivered — starting at page one, because there is nowhere it recorded having got to page 22,000. A job whose duration exceeds the interval between the events that kill it has, in the limit, a completion probability of zero. Nobody wrote that rule down anywhere; it just quietly holds.

```
deploys     ▼            ▼            ▼            ▼
attempt 1   ├────────────┤
attempt 2                ├────────────┤
attempt 3                             ├────────────┤
                                       0% … 0% … 0%
```

The other four symptoms arrive alongside it.

**Nobody can tell stuck from slow.** The UI shows a spinner. The worker shows no output for four hours. Support asks whether it's progressing and the honest answer is that no one knows; the only signal the job emits is its eventual completion, which hasn't happened yet.

**The retry is safe but not cheap.** 10.2a's idempotency did its job — a second run doesn't corrupt anything. It does re-OCR 22,000 pages that were already OCR'd, at the vendor's per-page rate. Idempotent means running twice is *harmless*; it does not mean running twice is *free*, and nothing so far in this module has addressed the difference.

**Cancel doesn't cancel.** The firm uploaded the wrong archive and notices at minute six. The Cancel button hides the row. The worker carries on for the remaining three hours and fifty-four minutes, spending OCR credits on documents no one will ever read.

**And the lease is a mess.** A four-hour job against a sixty-second lease is redelivered almost immediately, so a second worker starts the same four-hour job concurrently. The usual fix is to raise the lease to five hours — at which point a worker that dies for real holds the job hostage until dinner time.

Plus the thing 10.2b already covered: it occupies a worker for four hours, and the fleet's responsiveness is bounded below by its longest job.

## What It Is

The response to all six is the same, and it is not a feature — it's a decomposition. A long operation is restructured as a sequence of short, individually retryable units, plus the persisted state that says which units are done: **chunking** and **checkpointing**.

The unit worth naming is **chunk duration**, because a single number controls four things that look unrelated:

```
chunk duration ──┬──▶ rework when a worker dies      (≈ half a chunk)
                 ├──▶ whether you need lease heartbeats
                 ├──▶ how long "cancel" takes to take effect
                 └──▶ how badly the job blocks the fleet (10.2b)
```

Tune it once and all four move together. That is the entire article in one diagram, and most of what follows is justification.

One neighbour to separate: a long job is not a **workflow**. If the steps are homogeneous and independent — 40,000 pages, each processed the same way — you want chunking, which is a loop with a bookmark. If the steps are heterogeneous and dependent — request a quote, wait for approval, charge, ship, notify — you want a workflow engine, where the durable thing is the *position in a program* rather than a cursor over rows. Reaching for a workflow engine to iterate a list is heavy; reaching for a cursor to run a dependent multi-step process means reimplementing one badly.

## Recognizing the Problem

- A job whose duration is longer than the gap between your deploys.
- A job that has never completed in production, only in staging with a small file.
- A lease or visibility timeout measured in hours, set that way to stop redelivery.
- A Cancel button that changes a status and stops nothing.
- A progress bar that goes from 0% to 100%.
- A retry that repeats work that already succeeded, at per-item cost.
- Memory that climbs steadily for an hour and then drops — the whole dataset was loaded up front.
- A runbook line saying not to deploy while an import is running.
- Chunking that already exists but uses `OFFSET`, and each chunk is slower than the last.
- A support question — "is it stuck or just slow?" — with no mechanism capable of answering it.

## How It Works

**Turn duration into count.** The long operation becomes a batch record plus many short jobs. The two standard shapes:

**Sequential, self-rescheduling.** A job processes N items from a cursor, records the cursor, and enqueues itself with the next one.

```
[items 0–199] → checkpoint → enqueue [200–399] → checkpoint → enqueue [400–599] → …
```

The control flow lives in the queue instead of in the process, which is why a SIGTERM between chunks costs nothing. It preserves order, uses one worker at a time, and is the right default.

**Fan-out / fan-in.** A parent job splits the work into N chunk jobs that run in parallel; a completion count triggers a finalizer. Much faster, and the fan-in is the part that goes wrong — see below.

**Checkpoint by keyset, not offset.** `LIMIT 200 OFFSET 79800` makes the database walk 79,800 rows to skip them. Chunk 400 costs 400 times chunk 1, so the job decelerates as it progresses and the whole thing is quadratic. Chunk on a monotonic key instead — `WHERE id > $cursor ORDER BY id LIMIT 200` — which is constant-cost per chunk.

**Two levels of "done", and they do different jobs.** The cursor makes the job *resumable*. Per-item completion records make it *cheap to resume* and correct under concurrency. If OCR results are written per page with a unique key on `(run_id, page_id)`, then selecting the next chunk means selecting pages with no result row, and a redelivered chunk naturally skips what's finished. Idempotency prevents damage; checkpointing prevents rework. You want both, and they are not the same mechanism.

**Size the chunk to the termination grace period.** This is the rule that makes the number concrete. Kubernetes sends SIGTERM and waits — 30 seconds by default — before SIGKILL. If a chunk reliably takes less than that, a worker that stops claiming on SIGTERM finishes its current chunk and exits having lost nothing.

```
grace period   ├──────── 30s ────────┤
chunk (18s)    ├───────────┤                 drains cleanly, zero rework
chunk (4h)     ├───────────────────────────  killed mid-flight, full replay
```

**Progress: two numbers, honestly.** Done and total. The total is often unknown without a counting pass — either pay for the pass, estimate it and say so, or report a count with no denominator. A denominator you invented is worse than none, because it will be wrong in the direction that makes users file tickets.

Write progress once per chunk, not once per item: 40,000 updates to one row is row contention and table bloat for information nobody reads at that resolution. And keep progress as a derived cache, not the source of truth — the completion records are the truth, the counter is for the UI.

**Heartbeats: mostly deleted by chunking.** If chunks are short, each one fits comfortably inside a normal lease and there is nothing to extend. Heartbeating is only needed for units that genuinely cannot be divided — a single 20-minute video transcode — and it reintroduces 10.3a's problem: a process whose heartbeat fails but whose work continues is a zombie holding a lease it no longer deserves, and the work must be idempotent anyway.

**Cancellation is cooperative, and there are two different cancellations.** Process shutdown is one: SIGTERM, propagated as a cancelled context, which unwinds in-flight I/O. User cancellation is another: a row in a table that the worker has to *look at*. Nothing external can interrupt a running unit, so cancellation means three things — stop enqueuing further chunks, check the flag at chunk boundaries, and decide what happens to the work already done. Cancellation latency is therefore one chunk, which is the third thing on that list at the top.

**Partial results need a vocabulary.** A cancelled or failed long operation has done 60% of something. Either that's a legitimate state — an import where 24,000 contacts landed and 300 rows failed validation, with a downloadable error report — or it isn't, and you need compensation. Decide at design time; the UI needs the word either way.

## When It's Appropriate

- **Any job whose p99 duration approaches the termination grace period.** Below that, chunking buys little; above it, every deploy is a coin flip.
- **Per-item work over independent items**: rows, pages, files, recipients. Chunking is natural and the completion record is obvious.
- **Per-item external cost.** When a replay costs money — per-page OCR, per-address geocoding, per-message delivery — checkpointing pays for itself the first time a worker dies.
- **User-visible operations.** If someone is watching, progress and cancel are product requirements, and neither is retrofittable onto a four-hour monolith.
- **Anything that must survive a deploy**, which over a long enough window is everything.

## When It's Unnecessary or Overkill

- **Short jobs.** Under a few seconds, the batch record and the cursor are pure overhead.
- **Genuinely indivisible units.** A single large transcode, a `VACUUM FULL`, one third-party call that takes ten minutes. You can't chunk what has no seam; those need a longer lease with heartbeats and idempotency, and it's worth being honest that chunking is not universal.
- **Operations that must be atomic.** A ledger import where a half-applied state is unacceptable can't expose partial results — but it can still be chunked, by writing 400 chunks into a staging table and doing one atomic swap at the end. Chunk the computation; commit once.
- **Small totals.** Two hundred rows do not need a fan-in protocol.
- **When it's genuinely fine to be slow.** A monthly job nobody waits for, on a fleet with capacity to spare, can take four hours. Make it resumable anyway — that part is cheap — and skip the progress UI.

## Trade-offs

- **Partial states become real and must be modelled** — in the schema, in the API, and in the interface. "Completed with 300 errors" is a status somebody has to design.
- **Per-item jobs can cost more to dispatch than to run.** 40,000 jobs of 90ms each is mostly queue overhead; batch into chunks of a few hundred and the ratio inverts. The chunk exists partly because the per-item job is too small.
- **Fan-in is harder than it looks.** `done = done + 1` as a completion test is broken under at-least-once: a chunk that runs twice overshoots, and the finalizer either fires early or never. Count distinct completion records, or increment only by the number of rows an `insert … on conflict do nothing` actually inserted.
- **Parallel chunks destroy ordering.** If output order matters, either stay sequential or sort at the end.
- **Cancellation is best-effort.** Side effects already emitted cannot be recalled: emails sent, charges made, webhooks delivered. Cancel means "stop", not "undo".
- **A long operation now spans deploys by design**, so it will routinely execute half under the old code and half under the new. 10.1a's payload-versioning problem stops being a possibility and becomes a guarantee.
- **More machinery to debug.** "The import failed" becomes "chunk 173 of 400 failed twice and chunks 174–400 never ran," which is more informative and more work.
- **Resumability requires idempotency at the chunk level**, which requires a natural key for every unit. Sometimes that key doesn't exist yet and you have to invent one.

## Alternatives

- **Make the work fast enough not to be long.** 40,000 individual geocode calls against a provider with a 1,000-address batch endpoint is not a long-job problem. Bulk operations, set-based SQL instead of row-at-a-time, and batched third-party calls routinely take hours down to minutes.
- **Streaming.** Processing a file as a stream fixes memory, not duration — useful and frequently confused with chunking, because both involve not loading everything at once. Streaming has no bookmark.
- **A batch framework.** Spring Batch's chunk-oriented processing, restartability, and skip policies are this article implemented as a library, with the vocabulary already named.
- **A durable execution engine** — Temporal, Step Functions. Checkpointing, heartbeats, and cancellation become properties of the runtime rather than code you wrote. Step Functions' distributed map is fan-out/fan-in as a managed service. The cost is a new runtime and a new mental model.
- **The right tool for the size.** A four-hour transform over tens of millions of rows may belong in a warehouse or a data-processing framework rather than your application's worker fleet.
- **Lazy evaluation.** Compute per document on first access instead of pre-processing the whole archive — turns a four-hour job into 40,000 requests that may never all happen.

## How to Achieve It

**Level 0 — measure the duration distribution and where the time goes.** Per-item cost × item count, or a fixed setup cost? The answer decides whether you chunk or optimize.
_Signal you're here:_ "the import takes a few hours" with no breakdown.

**Level 1 — bound the work per job.** Process N items from a cursor, save it, re-enqueue. This one change buys deploy safety and resumability, and it's usually an afternoon.
_Signal you need it:_ any job longer than the grace period.

**Level 2 — per-item completion records with a unique key.** Retries become cheap rather than merely safe, and selecting the next chunk becomes "items with no completion row."
_Signal you need it:_ a retry that repeats successful work at cost.

**Level 3 — a batch record and honest progress.** Total, done, failed, status, `heartbeat_at`, and a per-item error list the user can download.
_Signal you need it:_ "is it stuck or slow?"

**Level 4 — fan-out for parallelism, with correct fan-in** and per-tenant concurrency bounds from 10.2b so one archive doesn't take the fleet.
_Signal you need it:_ sequential chunking is correct but too slow.

**Level 5 — cancellation, pause/resume, and a durable execution engine** if the steps become heterogeneous and dependent.
_Signal you need it:_ a cursor that's started carrying a state machine.

## Implementation

**Go**, because cancellation is the part of this article a language can actually help with: `context` propagates a shutdown into in-flight I/O rather than leaving it as something each call site has to remember.

The chunk handler is the whole design, and it's short:

```go
type Chunk struct {
	RunID  int64
	Cursor int64 // highest document id already processed
	Size   int
}

func ingestChunk(ctx context.Context, c Chunk) error {
	docs, err := pendingDocuments(ctx, c.RunID, c.Cursor, c.Size) // keyset, not OFFSET
	if err != nil {
		return err
	}
	if len(docs) == 0 {
		return finishRun(ctx, c.RunID)
	}

	for _, d := range docs {
		if err := ctx.Err(); err != nil {
			return err // aside 2
		}
		if err := ocrAndIndex(ctx, d); err != nil {
			return err
		}
		if err := recordDone(ctx, c.RunID, d.ID); err != nil {
			return err
		}
	}

	if err := noteProgress(ctx, c.RunID, len(docs)); err != nil {
		return err
	}

	if cancelled, err := runCancelled(ctx, c.RunID); err != nil || cancelled {
		return err // aside 3
	}

	next := Chunk{c.RunID, docs[len(docs)-1].ID, c.Size}
	return enqueue(ctx, "ingest.chunk", next)
}
```

> **Aside 1.** The last line is the loop. There is no `for` spanning the whole archive and no function that runs for four hours — the iteration lives in the queue, so every boundary between chunks is a safe place to be killed, deployed over, rescheduled onto another worker, or interleaved with someone else's work. That single structural change is what makes the job survivable; everything else here is refinement.

> **Aside 2.** `ctx.Err()` inside the loop is *process* cancellation — SIGTERM, wired through `signal.NotifyContext`. Returning here abandons the chunk, which is safe because `recordDone` has already committed the pages that finished: the redelivered chunk re-reads `pendingDocuments` and picks up where this one stopped. What it is *not* is user cancellation, which is a row in a table this process has no reason to have noticed.

> **Aside 3.** User cancellation is checked once per chunk, and that's deliberate: the flag lives in the database, so checking it per document would add 200 round trips to buy a latency improvement nobody can perceive. The consequence is that "cancel" takes effect within one chunk — which is the third entry in the diagram at the top of this article, and the reason 18-second chunks feel responsive and four-hour ones don't. Note what cancelling does: it stops enqueuing. It does not un-OCR anything.

Progress is one write per chunk, and it doubles as the liveness signal:

```go
func noteProgress(ctx context.Context, runID int64, n int) error {
	_, err := db.ExecContext(ctx,
		`update ingest_runs
		    set done = done + $2, heartbeat_at = now()
		  where id = $1`,
		runID, n)
	return err
}
```

> **Aside 4.** Two hundred times fewer writes than updating per document, and `heartbeat_at` answers "stuck or slow" without any extra mechanism: a run whose heartbeat is four minutes old with `done` unchanged is wedged, and one that's twenty seconds old is working. The `done` counter is a cache — the truth is the completion rows — which matters because the counter is the thing that will drift.

If you move to parallel chunks, the counter stops being safe as a completion test. A redelivered chunk adds its pages twice, `done` passes `total`, and the finalizer fires on an incomplete run. The fix is to let the constraint do the counting:

```sql
INSERT INTO doc_results (run_id, doc_id, text)
VALUES ($1, $2, $3)
ON CONFLICT (run_id, doc_id) DO NOTHING;
-- increment progress by the number of rows actually inserted
```

Which is the same move as 10.1a's `on conflict do nothing` and 10.3a's unique index, for the third time in this module: when at-least-once delivery meets a counter, put a uniqueness constraint between them and let arithmetic follow the database rather than the application's belief about what it just did.

## Further Reading

- **Spring Batch reference documentation, "Chunk-oriented processing" and "Restartability"** — the reader/processor/writer split, commit intervals, skip and retry policies. Worth reading even in another ecosystem, because every concept in this article has a settled name there.
- **Markus Winand, "No Offset" (use-the-index-luke.com)** — why `OFFSET` pagination degrades quadratically and what keyset chunking looks like instead.
- **Kubernetes documentation, "Termination of Pods"** — SIGTERM, `terminationGracePeriodSeconds`, and the ordering guarantees. This is the number that should be setting your chunk size.
- **AWS Step Functions, "Distributed Map state"** — fan-out/fan-in over large datasets with checkpointing and partial-failure tolerance, as a managed product. Useful as a specification of what a complete implementation has to handle.
- **Temporal documentation, "Activity heartbeats" and "Cancellation"** — heartbeating with a payload, so a resumed activity restarts from the last reported position rather than the beginning.
- **Amazon SQS API, `ChangeMessageVisibility`** — extending a lease on a unit you couldn't divide, and the documented consequences of getting it wrong.
- **Sidekiq documentation on Batches** — the fan-in callback as a product feature, and candid notes on why "the last job turns out the lights" is harder than it sounds.

A Java sibling would earn its place here more than anywhere else in the module, because Spring Batch has already made every decision in this article into a named, configurable thing — chunk size, commit interval, `ItemReader` state as the checkpoint, restart semantics, skip limits. Seeing the same problem as a framework's vocabulary rather than as hand-written control flow shows which parts are essential and which are just the code you happened to write. Not yet written.

---

The habit to carry out of this: when you meet a long job, don't ask how long it takes — ask what its chunk duration is. If the answer is "there isn't one," you already know its rework on failure, its cancellation latency, its deploy safety, and its effect on everything else sharing the fleet, because they're all the same number and that number is the whole job.
